Karunanidhi speaks at the release of Vairamuthu's poetry collection **`தமிழுக்கு நிறம் உண்டு`** (“Tamil Has a Colour”). The speech is structured less as a conventional political address than as a sustained literary response to the book: he reads, comments on, jokes with, criticises and praises individual lines, while repeatedly returning to Tamil language, social reform, rationalism and public responsibility.

He begins with Vairamuthu's preface, especially the idea that Tamil has survived repeated historical upheavals without losing its essential character. The metaphor of colour becomes central: just as the sky may display many colours yet retain its characteristic blue, Tamil has endured changing rulers, cultural pressures and linguistic influences without losing its underlying identity.

Karunanidhi also reflects on the word `இலக்கியம்`, recalling an earlier Muthamizh conference where scholars debated whether the word was Tamil or derived from Sanskrit. His point is practical: scholarly argument should clarify language for the public rather than consume endless time in unresolved disputes.

Much of the speech proceeds through close reading. He praises Vairamuthu's ability to turn ordinary bodily experience into poetry, including comic observations about waking, dressing, combing one's hair and travelling by train. He also gently points out a printing error in the book and uses it to illustrate the poet's own line that repeated correction can never remove every mistake.

In the poem “Twenty Commands,” Karunanidhi highlights social-reform lines: let roosters crow where caste no longer exists; let the sun rise where human beings awaken before it; let flowers bloom in the hair of widows. These lines lead him to discuss dropping caste titles, Ambedkar's vigilance on behalf of oppressed people, widowhood and Bharathidasan's reformist tradition.

The speech then turns to a poem about fire. Vairamuthu contrasts those who saw fire as a natural phenomenon with those who feared and deified it. Karunanidhi expands the point with a satirical story from the `Vipra Purana`, using myth itself to question credulous belief. He connects the poem's image of fire to rationalist critique and to the idea that inner fire—energy, sensitivity and moral passion—matters more than outward appearances.

Another section praises Vairamuthu's lines about animals carrying gods while human beings do not, and about animals that humans worship. Karunanidhi treats the humour as a philosophical criticism of human behaviour.

The later poems move toward social and political imagery. In “Ayodhya Raman Weeps,” Vairamuthu asks whether those mobilised for `kar seva` would also come to link rivers, restore forests and hills, or erase the poverty line. The poem then questions the logic of seeking a birthplace for a divine incarnation: if Rama is an incarnation, he is beyond ordinary birth; if he is simply human and born from a womb, the claim to divinity changes.

Karunanidhi next discusses a poem about a once-flowing river reduced to a narrow path. He admires the imagery of the river running white under moonlight, black like liquid night while carrying burnt charcoal, and red like betel juice spat by an old mountain woman. The image of a river that was once “like an elephant” but has shrunk “like its tail” becomes, for him, both a poetic triumph and a practical environmental concern.

A poem about the world's voice prompts another reflection: one cannot sew the world's mouth shut, but one can choose what to listen to. Karunanidhi cautions, however, against closing one's ears entirely. He retells the familiar father-son-donkey story to show the absurdity of obeying every passing opinion. One must listen selectively, learn from good criticism and reject harmful advice.

The closing section returns to Tamil identity. Vairamuthu compares a people living only on memories of ancient greatness to a desert plant surviving on moisture in the air. Karunanidhi accepts the warning: Tamil society should neither live only on old glory nor erase its past. Sangam culture should instead become a foundation for a new society—without caste and religious division, without untouchability, and with unity and equality.

He ends by invoking the old exhortation to the writer: **“ஏடா தம்பி, எடுடா பேனா”**—take up the pen. He tells Vairamuthu that such a pen will continue to win.
