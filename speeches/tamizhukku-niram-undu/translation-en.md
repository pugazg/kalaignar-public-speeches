Karunanidhi speaks at the release of Vairamuthu's poetry collection **`தமிழுக்கு நிறம் உண்டு`** (“Tamil Has a Colour”). He begins by describing the event as unusual: he is alone on the stage, and Vairamuthu has already recited many of the poems Karunanidhi had intended to quote. He therefore turns first to the book's preface and its argument that Tamil has survived repeated political, cultural and linguistic pressures without losing its essential character.

He develops the book's central metaphor: the sky may display many colours, but its enduring colour is blue; similarly, Tamil has endured many imposed “colours” without losing its own identity. He asks Vairamuthu to expand the substantial preface into a full-length historical work on Tamil's survival and the poets who sustained it.

Karunanidhi then recalls an older literary conference in Coimbatore where scholars debated whether the word `இலக்கியம்` was Tamil or derived from Sanskrit. His point is not to reopen that dispute but to insist that literary questions should ultimately illuminate ordinary readers rather than remain trapped in endless scholarly quarrel.

The rest of the speech moves through Vairamuthu's poems. Karunanidhi delights in their observations of everyday life, including lines about waking with one's head away from the pillow, struggling with clothes and socks, and the human inability to remove every error even after repeated correction. He uses a printing error in the book itself as a humorous example of the last point.

He praises lines that ask for equal rain throughout the world and a sky in which the stars remain visible, treating the apparent contradiction as an imaginative tension rather than a flaw. He also admires the poem's wish for youth to remain while wisdom grows old enough to acquire “grey hair.”

A set of poetic “commands” leads into social themes: caste abolition, the linking of the Ganga and Cauvery, the destruction of poverty rather than buildings, and the dignity of widows. Karunanidhi connects these lines with earlier reformist Tamil poetry and his own preference for the Tamil word `கைம்பெண்`.

The speech then becomes more satirical and rationalist. Discussing a poem about fire, he recounts a mythological story from the `விப்ர புராணம்` and uses it to ridicule unquestioned belief in puranic explanations. He returns to Vairamuthu's lines on fire as a symbol of inner energy: for someone without fire within, even the sun is only charcoal; for someone with inner fire, even charcoal can become a sun.

Another poem contrasts animals and humans, noting that gods are carried by animals while humans are not trusted in the same way. Karunanidhi treats the poem as both imagination and commentary on contemporary conduct.

He then quotes poems on mortality: people may think the earth belongs to them, but eventually they belong to the earth. A poem on Ayodhya asks whether Rama was an incarnation or a human being and redirects “kar seva” toward constructive tasks such as linking rivers, repairing forests and mountains, and destroying the poverty line rather than destroying structures.

Karunanidhi next discusses a poem about a river that once ran white under moonlight, black with charcoal, and red like betel juice, but has now shrunk to a path for donkeys. He offers, half-humorously, to have the Public Works Minister restore the river if Vairamuthu identifies it.

A poem advising one either to sew shut the world's mouth or close one's ears prompts Karunanidhi to tell the familiar story of a father, son and donkey: trying to follow every passerby's advice eventually leads to absurdity. The lesson is not to close one's ears entirely, but to listen selectively and learn from what is useful.

He closes with Vairamuthu's image of Tamil as a desert plant living on the moisture in the air, surviving on memories of former greatness. Karunanidhi warns against living only on ancient glory. Sangam civilisation should be a foundation for building a modern society without caste, sectarian division, untouchability or fragmentation. He ends by urging Vairamuthu to keep writing: **“ஏடா தம்பி, எடடா பேனா! உன் பேனாவிற்கு வெற்றி! வெற்றி!! வெற்றி!!!”**
