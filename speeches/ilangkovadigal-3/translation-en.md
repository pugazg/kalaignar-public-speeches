## Ilango Adigal (3)

Silambu Chelvar joked that I come to Tamil Arasu Kazhagam functions whenever a trial appears; I say I come to break the horn of the trial—remove the horn from “sothanai,” and what remains is “sadhanai,” achievement.

He also spoke of the sea of people at Poompuhar and treated today's gathering as a continuation of that welcome.

This function has risen, literally and symbolically, from earlier halls to the upper floor of Karpagambal Mandapam. I take it as a sign that Ilango's fame, Tamil's fame, Tamil history, Tamil feeling and Tamil self-respect are all rising.

## PDF 168 / printed p.167

Avvai Natarasan compared recent political trials to the traditional act of removing the evil eye. Even painful chillies burned in that ritual are used out of a mother's wish that her child live. In the same spirit, I treat criticism and tests as part of public life.

Balasubramaniam spoke of Kannagi as an advocate in *Silappathikaram*. She does not stop there; she finally becomes a judge demanding justice.

The epic's characters are not models whose literal actions must be repeated. What matters are the emotions and moral principles they embody.

## PDF 169 / printed p.168

The story of Manu Needhi Cholan does not mean we should literally kill a son beneath a chariot if a calf is struck today. It teaches a passion for justice.

Likewise Kannagi's confrontation with the Pandyan king teaches courage and the determination to demand justice; it is not a command that women today repeat every act of the story.

*Silappathikaram* is valuable because it plants such emotions and because it preserves Tamil history.

## PDF 170 / printed p.169

The Puhar canto can be read as an effort to cultivate unity among Tamils. At Poompuhar we have begun restoring cultural memories and institutions associated with the ancient city.

These projects are not my personal achievements. They depend on the cooperation of scholars, poets, artists and people such as Silambu Chelvar. Foundation stones and public commemorations should carry the memory of all who helped create them.

## PDF 171 / printed p.170

Silambu Chelvar said that Ilango's life and memory should be planted in Tamil hearts and that annual festivals should continue. I tell him and other Tamil scholars: think of what should be done; we in government will try to turn those thoughts into action.

You wished for an Ilango statue; it was done. You wished for festivals; they are being held. Though those in office may have little time even to dream, we will work to fulfil the worthy dreams you place before us.
