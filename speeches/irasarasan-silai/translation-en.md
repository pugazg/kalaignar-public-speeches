## The Statue of Rajaraja

The unveiling of the statue of the great king Rajaraja Chola, whose birth name was Arunmozhi Devan, is taking place here.

But we were unable to succeed in our effort to place his statue inside the great temple he himself built. We have therefore installed it outside, facing the temple he created.

Friends here spoke with regret that the Central Government refused permission to place Rajaraja Chola’s statue inside the temple—in the very land he ruled and within the temple he built.

They may have reasons for refusing permission. If the statue had been installed inside the temple, this gathering of hundreds of thousands would have had no room to assemble and would have suffered greatly! Perhaps that is why they told us, “Do not put the statue inside; keep it outside.”

Silambu Chelvar, in his speech, said that we should begin the struggle for State autonomy here in Thanjavur, from the statue of Rajaraja.

## PDF 100 / printed p.99

Is there no leadership in Delhi capable of understanding our demand and its justice? There is. Our Prime Minister of India can understand it—even if some Central Ministers do not.

Initially we did not connect the question of Rajaraja’s statue with the demand for greater powers and rights for the States. But consider this great Tamil king: victorious on many battlefields, conqueror of Kadaram, a ruler whose fame spread across lands where he marched. To be told that such a king may not have his statue placed in the very temple he built is not an ordinary matter. The builder of the temple has no place inside it; he must remain outside. When that answer came, a feeling began to rise in Tamil hearts: “If we do not possess even this right, what becomes of our other rights?”

Rajaraja Chola, a thousand years ago, stood as an embodiment of Tamil consciousness.

A thousand years later, even standing here in the form of a statue, he continues to awaken that consciousness in us.

Silambu Chelvar asked that the inscription say that because permission was denied to install the statue inside the temple, it has been installed here outside. We have done so.

## PDF 101 / printed p.100

He also suggested that we write that the statue has been placed here only “temporarily.” I did not have quite that much courage!

Nevertheless, the day is not far off when Prime Minister Indira herself will come and unveil the great king’s statue inside this sky-high temple.

When the statue question first arose, we did not tie it to State autonomy.

At the Sathaya festival marking Rajaraja Chola’s birth star, the idea arose to place his statue inside the temple. We began the necessary efforts.

We wrote to the Central Government. Even then, there was no thought of linking the matter with State autonomy. But the answer from the Centre was: a statue of the man who built the greatest temple in Tamil Nadu cannot be placed inside that temple.

A similar thing happened in 1966 under the Congress Government. Then Chief Minister Bhaktavatsalam wrote that the Vellore Fort contained a great temple without an idol and requested that an idol be installed there; he also asked that responsibility for maintaining the Vellore Fort be given to the State Archaeology Department.

Delhi’s reply was: impossible.

“Impossible” is therefore not an answer given only to a Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam Government. A Congress Government received the same answer.

## PDF 102 / printed p.101

When the answer remains “impossible”—whether one seeks to place a deity in the Vellore temple or the king who ruled Tamil Nadu in the Thanjavur temple—there are still people who mock the installation of Rajaraja’s statue.

Some of those very people once asked proudly, “I am a Tamil; is anything impossible for Tamil?” Today they ask: “A statue for Rajaraja? Who has actually seen him?”

We have statues of the sixty-three Nayanmars. Who among us has seen them directly? Who reproduced them exactly from life? Yet their statues exist.

In Rajaraja Chola’s case, moreover, his figure survives as a painting inside the Thanjavur temple itself.

This statue has been fashioned from that painting without sacrificing sculptural detail.

Those who ask, “You are erecting a statue—have you seen him?” did not raise the same question when a statue of Thiruvalluvar was installed.

Still others ask whether poverty will disappear if this statue is unveiled.

We can ask in return: “If this statue is not unveiled, will poverty disappear?”

Had they shown that poverty would vanish tomorrow if the statue were not installed, we might even have abandoned the ceremony.

## PDF 103 / printed p.102

Yet in Delhi—the very seat from which such questions are directed at us—Ramlila is held every year, and no one asks that question there.

Every year we also observe the sacred Independence Day and Republic Day.

No one asks whether poverty disappears because those celebrations are held.

They themselves say that even after ten more years of struggle poverty may remain.

Still Ramlila is conducted, and the President of India attends it. It is said that that festival alone costs eighty lakh rupees.

No one asks whether poverty has been eradicated by holding it.

Republic Day and Independence Day generate feeling and inner awakening. They are regarded as pure, sacred and emotionally meaningful. This function too is, to us, equally pure, sacred and filled with feeling.

Erecting statues is not unique to this country. Those who have travelled abroad know that there are hardly any great cities without statues.
