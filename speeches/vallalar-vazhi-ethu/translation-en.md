## What Is Vallalar's Way?

Among the sacred places that Ramalinga Adigalar chose as subjects for his songs is Tiruvarur, where I lived. Perhaps that is one reason I was chosen to release this book. Further, Vallalar called himself “Chidambaram Ramalinga Swamigal.” He had an ancestral connection with Chidambaram. I have a marital connection with Chidambaram; the town from which I first took a bride was Chidambaram.

Kunrakkudi Adigalar said: “Ramalinga Adigalar's principles, the Tamil he employed to express those principles, and the work he performed have all been spoken about by many great men — saints, eminent scholars, men of action, poet-laureates and revolutionary poets. Yet because his principles did not take root in the country, Thanthai Periyar had to appear on the public stage. Therefore, if the Dravidar Kazhagam is to be brought to an end, Ramalinga Adigalar's principles must be propagated properly.”

Many describe Adigalar as a Periyar clad in saffron. Perhaps conscious of this,

## PDF 19 / printed p.18

and perhaps somewhat apprehensive after having spoken so much, Adigalar said that one should put a full stop to the Dravidar Kazhagam — as though at least that one sentence were needed to survive among the monasteries.

A full stop must indeed be placed. But I ask only that all of us place the full stop where it will enable Tamil Nadu's future to assume the form of awakening.

Periyar alone is accused of expressing such harsh, uncompromising ideas. But had such blunt ideas never been voiced, we might not even have developed the capacity to regret many of the things that have now happened.

Listening to Adigalar's speech from beginning to end, I felt: it is fortunate that Periyar was born.

It is because he was born that he gave us the capacity to understand so many truths.

Some people can be awakened easily; many others cannot — they must be awakened forcefully.

It is said that when Ravana's wife Mandodari called “Kanna” in the morning, his twenty eyes blossomed open and he arose.

But to awaken his younger brother Kumbhakarna, the Ramayana says that thousands of rutting elephants were needed.

## PDF 20 / printed p.19

Tamil society had been in the condition of Kumbhakarna; that is why it had to be trampled by the elephant called Periyar.

Only because Periyar's heavy feet pressed upon it did Tamil society, which had been in Kumbhakarna's state, awaken and begin to speak, in Adigalar's own language. We must not forget that.

So, to awaken a society that still sinks into Kumbhakarna's sleep, revolutionaries like Periyar were needed — and are still needed.

In this vast volume of Ramalinga Adigalar's Arutpa, those who published it and placed it in our hands have explained his principles beautifully on one page.

For the convenience of those who wish to finish reading all these fine songs, they have classified and summarized Ramalinga Adigalar's principles:

* There is only one God — He is the Arutperunjothi.
* Minor-deity worship is not permitted.
* No animal sacrifice may be made in the names of such deities.
* Flesh must not be eaten.
* Differences of caste, religion and the like must not exist.
* One must observe the right of spiritual human unity that regards every life as one's own life.

## PDF 21 / printed p.20

* The discipline of compassion that removes the hunger of the poor is the key to the house of supreme bliss.
* Puranas and sastras do not reveal the final truth.
* The dead must be buried, not cremated.
* Rituals such as karumathi and tithi are unnecessary.
* A common, public-minded outlook is needed in everything.

These, they say, are all Ramalinga Adigalar's principles.

I ask: while we speak about these principles, how many do we accept? How many do we actually live by? To which of these principles have we submitted ourselves? We must put these questions to ourselves.

“Do not eat flesh” is Ramalinga Adigalar's principle. I do not know whether even Pollachi Mahalingam, who conducts this function, follows it.

It is impossible to accept every principle and ideal of a great person and shape oneself entirely in that image.

But we must prepare ourselves to follow those principles that society generally needs — principles that can make society grow and awaken.

## PDF 22 / printed p.21

Kunrakkudi Adigalar also recalled Perarignar Anna's statement, “One race, one God,” and pointed out that Thirumoolar himself had said this in an earlier age.

Periyar says there is no God at all. Adigalar noted that Anna adopted the principle “One race, one God” in order both to preserve what is worthy in our heritage and to help good new ideas flourish.

Because Periyar went that far, we are now able to seek a compromise with him: do not say that nothing exists; let us at least agree on one.

That too is recorded here as something Ramalinga Adigalar said. He opposed the worship of minor deities; therefore he rejected worship of many gods.

Whether mothers or men gathered here, after returning home, from tonight until tomorrow night, count how many deities you worship. Then you can decide for yourselves whether you are travelling along Ramalinga Adigalar's path.

Likewise he said, “Puranas and sastras do not reveal final truths.” Yet we respond: “Did you say that? Then we shall write a Purana about you!” We have turned Ramalinga Adigalar himself into the subject of a Purana.

## PDF 23 / printed p.22

Even in this book there are a few places where I differ. I said so when I spoke here previously.

There is the story of Ramalinga Adigalar writing by an oil lamp. The lamp was about to go out. He took water from a nearby vessel thinking it was oil and poured it into the lamp; because he thought it was oil, the lamp supposedly continued to burn. By telling such a story, we turn the very Ramalinga Adigalar who warned us not to believe Puranas and miracles into a miracle-performing siddhar. That is my firm view.

They have said that the book has been arranged by fixing the periods in which different writings were produced. That is true, and it is appropriate.

We have read many works written by the revolutionary poet Bharathidasan in his final years; they express many revolutionary ideas. Yet in his youth the same Bharathidasan wrote a hymn to Subramaniyar.

Suppose, two hundred years from now, someone compiles Bharathidasan's writings, pushes his later revolutionary writings to the front, and places the Subramaniyar hymn last, claiming: “At first he was like this; later, by God's grace, he reformed and became a devotee of Subramaniyan.”

## PDF 24 / printed p.23

If it were written that way, we would lose sight of how he changed from one period of his life to another.

Even today, when a person expresses an idea, there are people who ask whether it is the same idea he expressed ten years ago. At such a time, we must accept how necessary chronological definition is.

Therefore, when periods are defined and we examine what was written at each time, we see that Ramalinga Adigalar did not merely state these principles; he also criticized the differences between Saivism and Sanmargam.

Many immersed themselves in Ramalinga Adigalar's Thiruvarutpa. One was Rajamanickam Pillai. In the edition he compiled it is recorded:

On page 58:

“When someone asks whether God has hands, legs and such parts, we stand blinking, unable to answer.”

“Similarly, one should not place one's ultimate ideal in religions such as Saivism and Vaishnavism, or in doctrines such as Vedanta and Siddhanta.” This appears on page 69 of Rajamanickam Pillai's edition.

“And I myself am witness to all this. At first, the measure of the devotion I placed in the Saiva religion was beyond words. Pattinaththu Swamigal and

## PDF 25 / printed p.24

Velayudha Mudaliar and a few others know this. Look at what has now become of that devotion. No other witness is needed. Bring before the assembly the songs in my Thiruvarutpa and the songs of others; they themselves will bear witness. If you ask why I had such intense attachment then, it was because at that time my knowledge was small.”

“Now the Lord has raised me to a state I had not reached before. This gain has come from abandoning all those things. If you too abandon them, you will receive a great gain like mine. Those who have continued to hold on to them have gained nothing.”

This appears on page 60 of that book.

Thus much more could be said. Vadlur Vallalar gave Tamil Nadu revolutionary ideas.

Because those ideas were expressed in Tamil, and because they were not translated into other languages, they did not spread widely. But whenever revolutionary or new ideas are introduced into Tamil society, those who want to prevent them from entering society often elevate the person who voiced them, seat him in a lofty place, garland him, honour him — and discard the ideas themselves. Such

## PDF 26 / printed p.25

people skilled in these tactics are plentiful in this country.

The Siddhars said:

“Calling a planted stone a god,
offering it four flowers,
circling it and muttering,
what is that mantra?
Will the planted stone speak,
when the Lord is within?

Why call one a Parai woman? Why call another a Panathi?
Are flesh, skin and bone marked with such labels?”

They sang so.

But what did the country say?

What did those who believed such ideas must not be honoured say?

“They are Siddhars — only they can say such things.”

“We are ordinary human beings. We cannot do all that!”

“If Gandhi says a Harijan brother should be taken into the temple, he is a Mahatma; he can do it. We are merely human souls. Can we?” And with that they stopped.

By elevating those who spoke and lowering what they said, many clever people in this country have repeatedly succeeded.

## PDF 27 / printed p.26

The same thing has been done with Ramalinga Adigalar: place him high and say, “Ramalinga Adigalar may say it; can we? Great men may say it; can we accept it? Is it good for us to raise ourselves to that level and accept those ideas?” By repeatedly asking such questions, we have honoured the owners of the ideas with praise while destroying the ideas themselves.

From now on, whether or not we honour the persons who owned the ideas, we must pay special attention to respecting what they said.

Reading not only Ramalinga Adigalar's songs but also his prose makes our hearts unite with his ideas and creates within us the urge to work, as far as we can, to realize them.

It was pointed out that Ramalinga Adigalar said the country can prosper only when hunger is abolished.

If abolishing hunger is the goal, then equalizing ordinary people with the wealthy is the method to reach that goal.

The aim is to eliminate hunger. Yet sometimes hunger itself gives rise to fine thoughts.

Was it not a hungry poet who said, “O crane, O red-legged crane; your coral-sharp beak is like the split tuber of an old palmyra,” using the crane's beak as an image,

## PDF 28 / printed p.27

and thereby telling the world that its beak resembled a split palmyra tuber?

Even if he had no tuber to eat, he found a tuber as a poetic comparison. But do not ask whether hunger is therefore necessary in order to produce good songs!

The principle proclaimed by Vadlur Vallalar was that hunger must be removed. When we launched the rehabilitation scheme for beggars, I first recalled Vallalar's words, “Whenever I saw a withered crop, I withered,” and said that this scheme was being begun with that verse in mind. In many such ways Ramalinga Adigalar continues to guide us. The “gentle breeze that blew across the stage” of Tamil Nadu in his day continues to give us repose in summer, like the cool shade of a tree, and to bring us peace every day.
