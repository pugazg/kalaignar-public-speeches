## Chennai - Exnora and Rotary Organisations Appreciation Function

I am greatly delighted to have had the good fortune to take part today in this appreciation function, jointly conducted by various service organisations on behalf of Exnora International, and to receive the appreciation you are offering me.

Everyone who has praised me is someone familiar to me. I know them well, and they know me well. There is one special feature of this appreciation function: Mr. Seshan, who left Chennai saying there was no water, has returned to Chennai as soon as water became available. That itself is a special feature. (Applause)

While speaking, Seshan said, “I will not speak in *adukku mozhi*—layered, rhetorical phrasing—like others, like Kalaignar. I do not know how to speak in *adukku mozhi*.” Sir, that itself is *adukku mozhi*. (Applause) “I do not know how to speak in

## PDF 316 / printed p.315

*adukku mozhi*”—by saying it in a layered fashion, that itself became *adukku mozhi*. Some people think they are speaking *adukku mozhi* but end up speaking *thadukku mozhi*—stumbling speech. But your language is Tamil. Your language is one of fine feeling. Your language came from the heart.

I must say one thing at this great function. About five years ago, a question arose: would there continue to be an organisation called the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam? Would there continue to be its Rising Sun symbol? Would there continue to be the two-coloured flag given to that movement by Arignar Anna? A case was heard by the Election Commission. At that time I did not know my dear Mr. Seshan very well. I knew that at one time he had served in an administrative post as an officer in Tamil Nadu. I knew that later he had been an influential official in Delhi. I also knew that he was subsequently appointed Election Commissioner. I had not had opportunities either to be in frequent contact with him or to become personally acquainted with him. I knew only that he was very strict, honest, one who would correct a wrong wherever he found it, and one who would correct himself even when he realised that what he himself had done was wrong. That is the mark of a foremost human being. That was all I knew about Seshan.

It was a period when we waited anxiously to know what verdict would come from the Election Commission in that case. To conduct that case—

## PDF 317 / printed p.316

our General Secretary, the Professor, brother Murasoli Maran, and my dear friend Arcot Veerasamy had camped in Delhi. On the day the verdict was to be announced, I was in Salem. While speaking to Veerasamy, I told him, “Come back having saved both the flag and the party’s name. Otherwise, when you return, you will not see Karunanidhi.” Today Karunanidhi is standing before you. You praise me saying that I brought the Cauvery and brought Krishna water. The person who made it possible for me to be here then was our T. N. Seshan, who delivered a fair verdict. (Loud applause) Since until now I had not had an opportunity to praise him and take pride in doing so, I say this because this is the right place to have him before me face to face and praise him.

Though such a person did not speak here in detail about the Cauvery and the Krishna, he understood their intricacies and expressed the view that the Ganges should also be linked with the Cauvery. He knows the plan to link the Ganges and the Cauvery. It had already been proposed and discussed when K. L. Rao was Union Minister for Irrigation. At that time, over the Cauvery issue, I, Karnataka Chief Minister Veerendra Patil, Kerala Chief Ministers E. M. S. and Achutha Menon—

## PDF 318 / printed p.317

and the Chief Minister of Pondicherry were engaged in a campaign before the then Union Irrigation Minister in Delhi. One day K. L. Rao invited us and the other southern Chief Ministers to dinner at his residence. After dinner, he explained what linking the Ganges and the Cauvery would mean. In explaining it, he said that enormous pumping stations would have to be created at five or six places. Only by doing so could the rises and depressions along the route be overcome and water brought from the Ganges to the Cauvery through huge pipelines. Then he asked, “Do you know how much electricity would be required for the first pumping station to be built in North India?” He said that the electricity being used throughout India at that time would be required for that one pumping station, and explained that the scheme was not one that could then be welcomed as feasible.

But today, as Mr. Seshan desires and as you desire, even if the Ganges and the Cauvery are not linked at once, I believe that if, in the first stage, rivers such as the Godavari and the Cauvery are linked, then one after another the rivers can be linked together until India can formulate a national river policy and connect its rivers and waterways. The reason is that scientific development has today reached such heights. It is with that confidence that I say this.

It has been thirty years since the Cauvery dispute began. It has been fifteen years since the final agreement on the Krishna issue was made and brought into effect. While you praise me for the Cauvery, over which we have struggled for thirty years, and for the Krishna water, for which public works have been carried on for fifteen years, I in turn praise you. If you believe that this happened because of me, should I not thank you, who placed me in this responsibility?

Regarding the Cauvery issue, as Mr. Seshan pointed out here, he referred to the agreement made many years ago, specifically in 1924. Even before that, an agreement had been made in 1892. Before then, there were no governments in Mysore State that concerned themselves with the Cauvery. When kings such as Hyder Ali and Tipu Sultan ruled the Mysore region, they barely had time even to confront enemies on battlefields. Therefore, the time had not ripened for them to devise schemes to use the water resources of that region for the benefit of the people. Only after their rule did the Wodeyar kingdom begin in the Mysore region, and it was they who began building dams in order to use these water resources for the people’s benefit.

It was when such dams began to be built that British rule arrived. Because Tamil Nadu’s farmers conveyed their grievances under British rule—

## PDF 320 / printed p.319

the agreement made when the Krishnarajasagar was built there was the agreement entered into in 1924. Ten years after that agreement, the Mettur Dam was built in 1934. How much water was to come to this dam? How was it to be received? The agreement would not end in 1974; it would not expire. The 1924 agreement included clauses stating that in 1974 the surplus water was to be calculated and shared by the two States. But some people campaigned saying, “The agreement made in 1924 has ended; it has ended.” That campaign was led principally by the Karnataka Government. Because people in Tamil Nadu did not fully understand the details, they too thought that it ended in 1974. But that was not the truth.

At a time when engineers and experts in that field provided this explanation and warned the Karnataka Government, a Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam government under the leadership of Arignar Anna was formed in Tamil Nadu in 1967. The information Tamil Nadu then received was that the Karnataka Government was already constructing dams at two or three places, including Hemavathi. As they went on building dams, the extent of our irrigation resources would shrink. Therefore, the thought arose in Tamil Nadu that construction of those dams had to be stopped—

## PDF 321 / printed p.320

At that time Anna was Chief Minister. I was Minister for Public Works. I then held the Public Works portfolio that brother Duraimurugan holds today. Anna sent me to Delhi, in my capacity as Public Works Minister, and asked me to hold talks. K. L. Rao was then the Union Irrigation Minister. In Karnataka, Veerendra Patil was both Chief Minister and in charge of Public Works. He, the Kerala Chief Minister, the Pondicherry Chief Minister and all of us held discussions; talks began in 1968 and reached their final form only in 1998.

There were countless developments in between. After Anna, I became Chief Minister; after me, my dear friend M. G. R. became Chief Minister. After that, the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam returned to office. So many changes occurred before we returned to office in 1996. In the meantime, the people of the great farming community of Thanjavur filed a case in the Supreme Court on behalf of their association. The case was pursued with the demand that a tribunal should be constituted.

While that case was pending in the Supreme Court, we had come to power in 1989. V. P. Singh was then Prime Minister in Delhi. We too joined the case brought by the Thanjavur farmers’ association. When the matter came before the Supreme Court, the Assembly was in session here. V. P. Singh—

## PDF 322 / printed p.321

spoke to me by telephone. I hurried out from the Assembly and spoke on the telephone. He said that the Supreme Court was asking what the Union Government’s position was, and that the Union Government had to convey it through its counsel. He asked, “What do you say? What should we say?” I replied, “You must state the State Government’s position unequivocally.” He asked what that position was. I said, “There is no longer any benefit in talks. More than twenty-five rounds of talks have already taken place. Say that there is no remedy other than a tribunal.” He said, “Then pass a resolution in the Assembly and send it.” We immediately passed a resolution in the Assembly stating that a tribunal was necessary and sent it to Prime Minister V. P. Singh. Once he conveyed it to the Supreme Court through the Union Government’s counsel, the Supreme Court ruled that if they were no longer prepared for negotiations there was no other way except a tribunal, and ordered the Union Government to establish one. Within a month, V. P. Singh constituted the tribunal.

Once the tribunal was constituted, we made a request on behalf of our government: “Let your final award come later; first give an interim award.” The tribunal said that it had no authority to grant an interim award. We went again to the Supreme Court. “They say they have no authority. You must tell us whether they do or do not have that authority,” we asked. The Supreme Court immediately—

## PDF 323 / printed p.322

ruled that the tribunal had the authority to grant interim relief. We argued again on that basis. Then our government was dismissed. Under the government that followed, the tribunal’s interim award was obtained. Karnataka was to provide Tamil Nadu with 205 TMC of water. The tribunal, in accordance with the Supreme Court’s ruling, gave an interim award laying down month by month how many TMC were to be supplied—from June through May, so much in June, so much in July, and so on. The people of Tamil Nadu asked only that this be implemented. But the Karnataka Government would not agree to implement it. It refused.

That argument continued for the past seven or eight years, and now an agreement has been reached. It was reached in the presence of Prime Minister Vajpayee, with the Karnataka Chief Minister, Pondicherry Chief Minister, Kerala Chief Minister and me sitting together and arguing for nine hours. Brother Duraimurugan and Law Minister Aladi Aruna came in support of me and joined me in the arguments. The benefit obtained from that is not for us—

## PDF 324 / printed p.323

alone. I wish to state that this agreement benefits the farmers of all four States. (Loud applause)

Some thought we could call this a victory and thereby pick a quarrel with Karnataka. But I do not regard it as a victory. Because,

> Kannada and spirited Telugu,  
> graceful Malayalam and Tulu,  
> born from your womb, though one became many...

Just as those lines say, whether it is Kannada, spirited Telugu or graceful Malayalam, they are all southern States. For that reason I did not call it a victory. Even now I do not regard it as a victory. Yet even today some say that through this agreement Karunanidhi has betrayed Tamil Nadu. I know whether those who say so speak knowingly or without knowing. Whether you know it or not, I do not know. They say it despite knowing that it is a good agreement. They say that the draft plan framed during Gujral’s time must now be implemented, while leaders of parties in the coalition headed by Vajpayee say, “We will not accept the draft plan of Vajpayee’s time; this is a betrayal.” In other words, they say that Vajpayee and Karunanidhi together have betrayed the people of Tamil Nadu.

## PDF 325 / printed p.324

You know that I am not one who supports every position taken by Mr. Vajpayee, or every position taken by the Union Government. You know very well that, today, forever and always, Karunanidhi supports whatever position is good for Tamils, whatever is good for the country, and whatever is good for democracy. It is in that manner that this agreement has now been accepted by everyone. It has been accepted by the Karnataka Chief Minister, the Kerala Chief Minister, the Pondicherry Chief Minister and the Tamil Nadu Government. I ask you to consider what betrayal there is in it. Karnataka had said for eight years that it would not accept the tribunal’s award. If an agreement has now been reached in which the very first clause says, “Accepting the tribunal’s interim award and in accordance with it...”, please consider whether that can be called betrayal. And I—born in Thanjavur, having drunk Cauvery water, lived on Thanjavur soil, played on Thanjavur soil, eaten rice cooked from paddy grown on Thanjavur soil, and lived among you, a son of this soil—

## PDF 326 / printed p.325

to say that I betrayed the Tamils and committed treachery may perhaps be useful for politics. But I believe that this greeting and appreciation being offered to me today on behalf of Exnora—a camp of people possessed of a genuine spirit of service—serve to prove that it is not the truth.

Some call me a traitor. You appreciate me, saying that I have done a good thing. To put it briefly, some people inflict wounds; today you have applied medicine. For that, I wish to convey my thanks to Mr. Nirmal, to all of you belonging to the Exnora organisation, and to all the affectionate friends who offered words of appreciation.

Likewise, there is Krishna water. To bring Krishna water, we have spoken with N. T. R., Chenna Reddy, Janardhana Reddy and Chandrababu Naidu—four or five Chief Ministers in this way. As far as I am concerned, I myself have spoken with four or five Chief Ministers. On this question of Krishna river water, the person who wrote a letter and contacted the Union Government about it as early as 1948 was Omandur Ramasamy Reddiar, who was then Premier of the Madras Presidency and assumed responsibility as Chief Minister. It had been discussed even before that. It was discussed in Parliament too. Krishna water was discussed in 1953, 1963 and at other times.

## PDF 327 / printed p.326

But it was Omandur Ramasamy Reddiar who wrote asking that the Union Government implement it immediately. After that it was shelved. Even though successive Chief Ministers made efforts, it did not come to fruition.

My dear friend M. G. R. and Andhra Chief Minister N. T. Rama Rao announced the Krishna water project on Chennai’s seafront in the presence of Prime Minister Indira Gandhi, and an agreement was entered into. To implement that agreement, how many times have I, our friend Veerasamy, others and the Public Works Minister gone to Andhra? How many times have we spoken with those Ministers? How many times have we argued with those officials? You also know how many times they in turn came here. The Krishna water released last year—if one may put it in the language of the Puranic storytellers—ran into our bad time: because of rains and floods, we did not receive it fully as we had expected. After a year, with many arrangements made, today the water has begun arriving in Chennai fully in accordance with the agreement, in the months in which it is supposed to reach us.

Yesterday there was one function. The day before yesterday there was another function. The day before yesterday, at a place near Valluvar Kottam, for this entire area—

## PDF 328 / printed p.327

a huge tank was inaugurated under the Krishna water and Chennai drinking-water scheme, large enough to benefit nearly three lakh people. As I was returning after that function, I looked at the height of that tank. I also looked at the height of the chariot at Valluvar Kottam. That chariot brings back so many memories for me. When the sculptors asked how the hall at Valluvar Kottam should be designed, it was I who drew the plan saying that it should be like the Thiruvarur temple chariot. But yesterday, when I looked at the height of this tank, the tank appeared higher to me than that. That chariot is indeed endowed with divinity. Yet the reason the tank seemed higher than that divinity is that it is a tank filled with water capable of sustaining thousands of deities. Likewise, there was the inauguration of a large water-storage tank in the Chepauk constituency. There too, by using Krishna water, as brother Stalin pointed out here, the scheme has expanded to the point where the quantity supplied to Chennai residents can rise from 70 litres to 100 litres per person. These functions took place yesterday and the day before yesterday.

As many tanks as need to be opened across Chennai city will continue to be opened, so that Chennai’s drinking-water requirement can—

## PDF 329 / printed p.328

be met in full. Yesterday I announced one thing—a long-term plan. Efforts toward it are proceeding rapidly and intensively. Even though there was a slight lull in between, the effort has now begun moving faster and faster. That plan is to clean and purify the Cooum River, the Buckingham Canal and the Adyar—these three waterways. The Mayor so often says, “Beautiful Chennai, Beautiful Chennai.” To create a truly beautiful Chennai, my long-standing desire is to see these three rivers placed like three pearl garlands around the neck of the city called Chennai. I believe that desire will soon be fulfilled.

Nirmal has conducted this function today and brought here thousands of branch members and administrators from his service movement, showering appreciation upon me. The very word *Nirmal* means purity. We say “nirmalam”—pure. *Nirmal* itself means purity. Exnora is the name of this organisation. When I spoke about it last year at Kalaivanar Arangam, I asked what “Exnora” meant. Nirmal said that X stood for “excellent.” He said N,O stood for “novel”—something new. He said R,A stood for “radical,” meaning change. I—

## PDF 330 / printed p.329

said while speaking then: Exnora contains “X”; it contains “no”; it contains “radical.” This radical will always remain. “X” means “former”: an ex-minister is a former minister; an ex-chairman is a former chairman. Therefore, this radical has no “X.” X—“no”; Exno—“ra,” radical. That is how I explained it at that function. (Loud applause)

Therefore, this movement, conducted by many pure-hearted people with a spirit of service, includes lakhs of people across many States in India and carries out good work for the country. To be appreciated by them is a great distinction, a unique distinction, that I have received in my life. Saying this, I offer my thanks as an offering at your lotus feet and conclude my speech.
