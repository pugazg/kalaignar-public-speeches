# Old History and the Younger Generation — English translation

Karunanidhi opens the Tamil forum at Alagappa Engineering College by stressing why Tamil literary associations matter. Tamil speakers, he says, had to recover an awareness of the antiquity and distinction of their own language through the work of scholars, writers and researchers; the younger generation must now turn that recovered awareness into protection of language and community.

He then turns to the writing of history. Referring to Manonmaniam Sundaram Pillai and Vincent Smith, he argues that Indian history should be approached from the southern peninsula as well as from the conventional northern starting point. He contrasts Alexander's use of historians to document campaigns with Tamil rulers who were accompanied chiefly by poets. Tamil literature therefore became an indispensable historical archive, even though poetic praise can exaggerate.

The speech discusses the idea of a distinct Dravidian civilisation and cites P. R. Seshhu Iyengar, Anna's history professor, along with U. V. Swaminatha Iyer and other scholars who preserved and interpreted Dravidian and Tamil cultural materials. Karunanidhi makes clear that scholarship and cultural preservation cannot simply be reduced to contemporary political identities.

He then recalls maritime Tamil history and ancient trade. Tamil rulers possessed fleets, and Tamil pearls, pepper, rice, ivory and other products were prized in Rome and the Mediterranean world. Stories about Roman royal jewellery and Cleopatra's pearl are used not for their luxury alone, but to remind the audience that valued objects in distant courts were linked to Tamilakam.

From history he moves to inheritance. Earlier leaders, scholars, poets, warriors and martyrs are compared to the scars on a coconut tree: each mark records what has already been given, but those very marks become footholds enabling the next generation to climb and obtain the fruit. The people who must now climb are the young people sitting before him.

Karunanidhi argues that language, social equality, economic justice and political awareness depend on the younger generation. He recalls the 1965 language agitation and then turns to literary and epic examples of youthful courage and concentration: Nedunchezhiyan, Karikala, Abhimanyu, Indrajit and Arjuna. The lesson drawn from Arjuna's archery test is that a clear goal requires undivided attention.

Modern public figures are placed in the same pattern. Ambedkar, Nehru, Periyar and Anna entered public life in youth; Anna was only twenty-nine when he went to prison in the 1938 Tamil language agitation. The 1965 student martyrs are presented as later examples of young people staking their lives on linguistic identity.

The conclusion is a direct appeal to students: the future of Tamil Nadu and India rests with the younger generation. Older generations must leave behind the knowledge, institutions and pathways that allow the young to act. Without youth, he says, there is no redemption or dawn for the country.
