## 'Isaithamizhin Unmai Varalaru' Book Release Function

Mrs. Revathi Sankaran, who has been compering the programme, described my coming up to speak as the payasam. At no meal does payasam come last. Curd rice comes last. And curd rice cannot go down without pickle; I say this to console my friend Sathyaseelan here, who compared himself to the pickle. (Laughter)

This function, being held on behalf of the Sekkizhar Mandram with the cooperation of Ragapriya, has been arranged for the release of P.T.R. Kamal Thiagarajan's book *Isai Thamizh-in Unmai Varalaru* — “The True History of Tamil Music.” I too listened closely to all the speeches delivered at this function. You too have listened closely.

Tiruppambaram Shanmugasundaram, who seems to have been preparing for the past few days, came here as though he had come specifically to conduct a debate in a competitive spirit and set forth what appeared right to him, and showered us with his views

## PDF 352 / printed p.351

I greatly appreciate him. He has said, “Arrange another programme and I will take care of it there.” I take great pleasure in listening to such interesting debates. For when people who know what we do not know debate it and arrive at a conclusion, those of us who are in a position to accept that conclusion derive great pleasure. I tell him that I too will make arrangements for us to enjoy that pleasure soon, and our friends will do so as well.

You should not imagine that merely because someone is a Chief Minister he knows everything. As Thiagarajan, who delivered the vote of thanks here, pointed out, though I was born into a musical family, and though my father was a great musical scholar of his time — a scholar to whom Thiruvavaduthuraiyar and Thiruvenkattar would come to Thirukkuvalai to clear their doubts, discuss matters with him and learn certain things — I did not involve myself in that field. I have explained the reason at some functions. When I was growing up as a young boy, I was sent for music training. But the teacher who taught me music and the people associated with him, whenever wealthy men, important men, and people belonging to the upper castes of that village came along the street,

## PDF 353 / printed p.352

would take the towel from their shoulders and tie it around their waists. I saw that condition. I could not accept it. I could not bear it either.

The very next day, of my own accord, I stopped going to the house where music was taught. When my parents and relatives pressed me and tried to send me there again, I said, “I will not go to a place where self-respect is taken away. Even if I learn music and become a great master, if I have no right to wear a towel over my shoulder, I do not need that music.” (Applause) Otherwise, today I might have stood before you as a Tiruppambaram Shanmugasundaram, a Thiruvenkadu Subramaniam, a Kulikkarai Pichaiyappa, a Thiruvaduthurai Rajarathinam, or a Sirkazhi Govindarajan.

But does that mean music has become so lacking in value today? No. It is precisely so that it should not lose its value that Kamal Thiagarajan has composed and given us this book, *Isaithamizhin Unmai Varalaru*.

There is no need for me to introduce him to you and speak about him. Kamal Thiagarajan belongs to a prominent family in Tamil Nadu. He is the son of the cultured P.T. Rajan, who was one of the leaders of the Dravidian movement. He is the brother of our Assembly Speaker, Palanivel Rajan.

## PDF 354 / printed p.353

When he wrote this book, gave it to me and asked for a foreword, I was astonished when I read it. I have gone to that home in Madurai many times. On all those occasions I met Kamal Thiaga Rajan. From his nature, his reserved manner, and his habits and ways, I did not then realize that he carried such a great treasury in his mind. Only after reading this book could I understand what a gifted scholar he is.

P.T. Rajan's family is known to everyone in Tamil Nadu. He occupied high office. His pleasant nature, simple way of life, and the quality of embracing everyone made him a cultured man. He shone as a Tamilavel. The treasures of his household today are Palanivel Rajan, who occupies the office of Speaker of the Assembly, and Kamal Thiagarajan, the author of this book, who has received your affection, appreciation and good wishes.

If I am to speak about this book, it is only proper that I first disclose my own limitations. It would not be proper for me to speak as though I know everything about music. No one can know everything. Different people know different things. The same is true of me. Just because I am a Chief Minister, if I go to a hospital, interfere in a matter I do not know, and ask a doctor how an operation is performed, discussing the finer points of surgery,

## PDF 355 / printed p.354

that would be nothing but officious lecturing done merely because one holds ministerial office. Likewise, if I were to conduct analyses here on the finer points of music — saying Kamal Thiagarajan could have written the book in this way or that — it would be useless work, even unnecessary work.

There is a story from Greece. There, a painter would each day display the paintings he made outside his studio so that everyone passing along the street could see them. One day he painted a picture and placed it at the entrance to his studio. A cobbler passing that way examined the picture closely and told the painter, “There is a flaw in this painting. The footwear (shoe) you have put on the foot in this picture is not right,” and pointed out the error. The painter looked at it and said, “Yes, it is a mistake. I will correct it,” thanked him, and sent him on his way. Two days later the painter again painted a beautiful woman and, as usual, placed the picture at the front of his studio. That day too the cobbler came that way. He looked at the painting and stopped. The painter came near

## PDF 356 / printed p.355

and asked him what was wrong with it. The cobbler said, “Nothing much. This eyebrow could have been drawn a little more neatly.” The painter immediately became angry. “My man, stay with the shoe; do not come into all this,” he said. (Laughter)

In the same way, I should stay with politics, poetry and art. If I enter the field of music, Kamal Thiagarajan too may say to me what that painter said to the worker. (Loud laughter)

Those who spoke at this book-release function spoke with some anguish about the stature Tamil once had and its present condition. The Tamil Thai Vazhthu sung first begins with the lines from Manonmaniam Sundaranar: “In this Bharata land, whose radiant face shines like a richly adorned maiden-land girdled by seas and waters...” After I assumed office in 1969, we searched for a Tamil Thai Vazhthu of our own. We selected this song, but we left out only the inauspicious words in it — in the passage, “like Aryan, whose worldly usage has vanished and disappeared — your youthful excellence undiminished...” — because inauspicious words should not find a place in auspicious functions. We then made that song the Tamil Thai Vazhthu sung at government functions. If you listen carefully to the song, it says, “O great Tamil maiden who *was* fragrant with fame in every direction.”

## PDF 357 / printed p.356

That is, one can also say Tamil “was” so. It was then fragrant with fame in every direction. But (Applause) this can also mean that it is not so today.

Considering today's condition, we may worry that it was so then but is like this today, and take a pledge to engage ourselves in efforts to make it renowned, worthy of praise, worthy of respect and worthy of honour.

A major idea that occupies this book is how the musical instrument called the yazh left Tamil soil, and how it left Tamil music. Kamal Thiagarajan has explained the causes and consequences of this in many places. Even if we say that the instrument called the yazh no longer exists, we still love the word *yazh*. The Revolutionary Poet Bharathidasan did not sing, “When sorrow comes, will you not take up the yazh and bring joy?” at a time when the yazh was in use. It was in this era, when the yazh was no longer in circulation, that the Revolutionary Poet Bharathidasan sang this song. For there is such sweetness even in simply pronouncing the word *yazh*.

The yazh is not in use today. The author has investigated all the reasons for how it disappeared from Tamil Nadu. For that he has read hundreds of books. Even though he read, researched, learned and wrote this book in that way, the

## PDF 358 / printed p.357

characteristic refinement of that family, and the modesty characteristic of that family, resound through this book. He writes in the book:

“The history of music has been forgotten; it has been concealed. A true explanation cannot be found in any book. Therefore, as the first task I have undertaken, I will state categorically that the books that have so far narrated the history of Tamil music have, for this one reason, not told the true history. I know that no one can deny that either. Having realized that the true history of music has not been told, if I were then to say that I searched for it and am now telling it to you, that too would not be true.”

Think how modest that sentence is.

One might say that this true history, without my even going in search of it, came and fell into my lap by itself.

He gives an analogy for this too... He is not merely a musician. He is not merely a flute maestro. He is not merely the author who has written so wonderfully about music in this book. He is a good writer. (Applause) Only with that command of writing can one firmly impress the idea one wants to convey. See how beautifully he impresses this idea. He says:

“Many years ago, in the magazine *Ananda Vikatan*, a writer named Devan wrote the story *Thuppariyum Sambu*. This Sambu would not go about searching for criminals

## PDF 359 / printed p.358

He would not go looking for clues. The clues to the crime would come to him by themselves. The criminals too would get caught by him of their own accord. The story depicts such an expert. In the same way, I did not go in search of this true history. It came seeking me on its own. As far as the true history of Tamil music is concerned, my role in bringing it to light is just like the role of Thuppariyum Sambu.”

Thus, with great modesty, without an attitude of elevating himself — without the attitude that only he could do this or that he had read so many books for it — he has handled these sentences with the refinement characteristic of him and of that family.

The author writes further:

“Looking at books written on the basis of Carnatic music, or books written only on the basis of Tamil music, I could not be satisfied. If we are to believe those who wrote about Carnatic music, they give us an impression as though this musical tradition was born and grew somewhere in North India, passed through many lands, and then suddenly came and leapt into our land. We could not be satisfied by that. It did not appear at all acceptable to us.”

## PDF 360 / printed p.359

These are not lines he wrote merely because he comes from the tradition of the Dravidian movement. They are lines written in the name of true history.

He also writes: “Those who write about Tamil music, with some unshakable belief, keep saying ‘Carnatic music’ and ‘Tamil music’; not only have they failed to explain the reasons for it, but it would not be an exaggeration to say that they look at Carnatic music with a suspicious eye.”

“Before I wrote this book, the Vidwat Sabha known as the Madras Music Academy invited me, under the endowment established by Mrs. Chellamma Natarajan, to present an article on music. The title they gave was *Contribution of Tamil to Karnatic Music* — that is, ‘The Role of Tamil in the Growth of Carnatic Music.’ I too compiled and wrote the points mentioned above. Only after I finished writing did I realize that the title they had given did not fit it. Carnatic music is one thing. Tamil music is another. This article did not accept that Tamil merely played a part in that musical tradition. The conclusion of my article was that Tamil music itself evolved into Carnatic music. Therefore I wrote to them again: ‘The title you gave does not fit the article I have written. So I request that it be changed to *New light on the Musical History of Tamil Nadu*.’ Along with this

## PDF 361 / printed p.360

request, I sent them a copy of the article. They too understood my feelings and thoughts and accepted this new title. This title may be understood as ‘A New Perspective on South Indian Music.’ By modifying this same title, I present it in this book as *Isai Thamizh-in Unmai Varalaru* — ‘The True History of Tamil Music.’” That is what he has written.

I would state categorically that this is a very, very important section of this book. For after writing an article whose conclusion was that Tamil music itself evolved into Carnatic music — contrary and opposed to the argument that Tamil music merely had a part in Carnatic music — the title *Contribution of Tamil to Karnatic Music* certainly would not fit. Kamal Thiagarajan has presented this to the world in a way that tells the true history. He states that the conclusion of his article was that Tamil music itself evolved into Carnatic music. This book is thus structured to spread the greatness of Tamil music.

Tiruppambaram Shanmugasundaram mentioned this here. He spoke about gamakam and even demonstrated it by singing. Kamal Thiagarajan points to the position that because that gamakam did not suit the yazh — because it could not be accommodated on it — the yazh itself disappeared.

## PDF 362 / printed p.361

He has written very beautifully in this book that if a song is sung without gamakam, it will not be pleasing.

“Among people who know music, it is customary to say that when someone sings without gamakam, he is singing ‘baldly.’ In ordinary usage, ‘bald’ means a state without hair. In literature it is said that if the hair of the kavari deer is removed, it will destroy its own life. (When speaking of some things, people say ‘Out of the Way.’)”

As he says this, he points to that characteristic of the kavari deer. He shows the loftiness of honour through the claim that the kavari deer will not live even if it loses a single hair. There are people in this country who can have their whole heads shaved and still feel that their honour has not gone. Some people even alter the Tirukkural — changing “Like the kavari deer that cannot live if its hair is removed, the honourable will give up their lives when honour is threatened” into “Like the kavari deer that cannot live if life departs, they will remove their hair when honour is at stake.” We are living in such a time. But Kamal Thiagarajan writes:

“Literature says that if the hair of the kavari deer is removed, it will destroy its own life. A human being too values the hair greatly as long as it remains on his head. Once it has fallen from the head, he feels disgust on seeing it — ‘People who have fallen from their station are like hair fallen from the head’ —

## PDF 363 / printed p.362

this Kural too shows how a person regards hair that has fallen from the head. ‘Bald’ does not refer only to the state of having no hair on the head. When one refers to a woman and says ‘bare neck’ or ‘bare hand,’ it indicates that those parts bear no ornaments. Looking at the comparisons given above, it does indeed seem appropriate to describe singing without gamakam as ‘singing baldly.’” In order to explain why a song sung without gamakam is called a “bald song,” he has used so many analogies.

Therefore, he is not merely a distinguished scholar. He is not merely a distinguished engineer. He also stands as a distinguished writer, a rival to me (Applause), and with that observation, wishing that his service may live and flourish, I conclude my speech here.
