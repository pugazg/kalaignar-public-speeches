# Haiku Poems — Research Book Release Function

Karunanidhi speaks at the release of Nirmala Suresh's research book on haiku and begins by joking that many in the audience may be nodding along without really knowing what haiku is. He recalls how Anna used to take party colleagues to English films: some laughed simply because everyone else laughed, even when they did not understand the dialogue. The anecdote becomes his playful introduction to a poetic form that was still unfamiliar to many Tamil readers.

He argues that the function is more than a book release: through Nirmala Suresh's work, haiku itself is being introduced more systematically to Tamil audiences. He cites her description of haiku as puzzling to those who do not grasp it but a treasure to those who do, a compact form whose simplicity, structure, imagery, intuitive flash and concentration fit experience into three lines.

The speech repeatedly illustrates haiku's characteristic turn in the final line. One poem describes a boy “wiping the sky” from tables after a storm; another sees a fallen flower apparently returning to its branch, only to reveal in the final line that it is a butterfly. A dead cat is shown with its mouth open in pouring rain. Karunanidhi uses these examples to explain haiku's compressed visual logic.

He discusses the “zoom lens effect” described by a modern haiku scholar: the poem moves from a broad scene to a tightly focused image, like a camera. He applies that idea to a poem beginning with a plain, narrowing to the lips of a cow, then to the moisture on the grass. He jokes with film director Vasanth about lenses and “two lips,” turning literary analysis into stage humour.

Other poems lead into social and philosophical themes. Smoke rises and bends toward the sky without being over-explained. Scattered rice causes chickens to fight. One poem says that while the skin of men and women differs, their bones reveal the same humanity, which Karunanidhi connects with an older Siddhar verse. He also praises Nirmala Suresh's explanation of Zen as a human disposition rather than the exclusive property of any nation.

He quotes a haiku imagining the moon with a handle, becoming a beautiful fan, and another by Arivumathi about exhausted ploughing cattle and a crow searching their wounds. These examples allow him to praise haiku's ability to compress observation, pain and irony into a few lines.

In the closing section Karunanidhi turns personally to Nirmala Suresh. He recalls knowing her as a television newsreader before learning that she was the daughter of Tiruchi Irudhayaraj, an old associate of Anna. He describes her affectionately as a daughter and takes pride in her achievement: a substantial 444-page research work on haiku.

He ends with a final three-line image of his own emotional state: the hall is full of ministers, yet he weeps because one place is empty.
