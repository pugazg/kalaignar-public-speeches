### Thiruvannamalai Arunai Engineering College Graduation Ceremony

I take great joy and pride in participating in the graduation ceremony of Arunai Engineering College, in the inauguration here today of the Anna Computer Research Centre building, in meeting all of you, and, though more than 130 among you are receiving degrees today, in presenting degrees symbolically to a few of them.

There is no circumstance that permits me to speak here at length. The reason is that you know I have another programme. You also know that the person who has arranged that programme is K. Pichandi. That is why, while everyone else spoke for ten minutes, he alone concluded his speech here in just a minute or two so that the programme could be finished quickly and the leader could come.

When I see all of you seated here, my confidence in the future Tamil Nadu and in society grows stronger. With that mental

## PDF 206 / printed p.205

comfort, I am duty-bound to appreciate and congratulate all of you. At a time when the need for engineering colleges in Tamil Nadu is increasing, my dear younger brother Velu started this engineering college; and not merely started it, but within six or seven years has shown remarkable growth and made it stand out today as one of the reputed colleges. Thinking of this again and again, I take great pride and joy in having him as a younger brother.

He insisted that I should come to today's function. At first I thought of avoiding it. Only after coming and seeing all of you, after seeing this festive atmosphere, after seeing all your radiant faces, do I realise today that it was wrong for me to have thought of avoiding it at first. In a state of such fulfilment and exhilaration, I feel satisfied not only that such a wonderful function has been conducted here, but also that this college has grown to a level at which it can compete successfully with the many colleges that are developing in every way today. With that sense of fulfilment, I appreciate younger brother Velu, who has been running this college for these seven years, the distinguished teachers who work here in support of him, and all of you, the student wealth of this institution.

## PDF 207 / printed p.206

For students in colleges to remain peaceful, disciplined and successfully attain their goal is a matter of very great responsibility. It gives me immense happiness to know that all of you in this engineering college are carrying out that responsible task.

Before 1991, the number of engineering colleges in Tamil Nadu was 38. Of them, government colleges were 5; government-aided private engineering colleges were 3; and self-financing colleges were 30. During the previous five-year administration, 33 engineering colleges were permitted. Thirty-three colleges were permitted in five years. But I am happy to tell you that in these 27 months the number of self-financing engineering colleges permitted by this government is 87.

The reason self-financing colleges are today being permitted extensively, in greater numbers and in countless measure in Tamil Nadu is that we give all these colleges “just like that.” Velu knows what “just like that” means. (Loud applause)

Many self-financing colleges know it too. But all 87 self-financing colleges we gave were indeed given “just like that.” The reason for giving them in this manner is that students are asked for too much money in self-financing colleges. But if more colleges come into existence, demand will fall. To the extent that self-financing colleges become plentiful and inexpensive—

## PDF 208 / printed p.207

and proliferate across Tamil Nadu, even poor and ordinary students will be able to obtain that professional education more easily. That is why, during these 27 months of our government, we have granted permission for 87 engineering colleges. Likewise, if we take polytechnics, the number of polytechnics functioning in Tamil Nadu before 1991 was 128. Of those 128, government polytechnics were 21, government-aided polytechnics 35, and self-financing polytechnics 72.

During the previous five-year administration, permission was given for fourteen self-financing polytechnics. Since we came to office in 1996, the number of polytechnics permitted during these 27 months is 97; I am happy to make that known to you. It is this government that has achieved records by granting permission for arts and science colleges to be started in large numbers on a self-financing basis.

During the five years from 1991 through 1995, the number of arts and science colleges permitted was 67. But in these 27 months alone, that number is 74.

It was precisely in order to give separate importance to higher education that this government, in 1997–98, divided the Education Department that already existed in the Secretariat into the School Education Department and the Higher Education Department, and created a new department for higher education. I mentioned that from 1996 until now we have permitted 87 engineering colleges.

## PDF 209 / printed p.208

In these colleges, the number of students who applied for seats last year alone was 17,000. I take great pride in informing you that this government achieved the feat of providing those 17,000 students the subjects they desired in the colleges they desired.

In engineering-college admissions, this government has become a guide for all India by using a single application and, at Anna University, setting up at a cost of 25 lakh rupees a computerized information display system that enables male and female students to join the course of their choice in any college in Tamil Nadu. Through that, from 1997–98 onward, the single-window system has been implemented, making this government a guide for India itself. Not only that; you know how much concern this government shows for students from families without a graduate.

In medical education, dental education, engineering education, veterinary education, agricultural education and agricultural engineering education, among the male and female students selected for each form of professional education, this government has, from 1996 onward, borne the higher-education expenses of the ten highest-ranking students belonging to families in which there are no graduates at all. You can see this as an innovation found nowhere else in India.

## PDF 210 / printed p.209

As the friends who spoke here pointed out, from 1997–98 onward, 15 percent of seats in engineering colleges have been reserved for rural male and female students, creating a quiet revolution so that people in rural areas too may obtain opportunities for professional education and advance. I wish to state that this too is the work of this government.

In order to honour soldiers who lost their precious lives in military service, this government has extended a helping hand to their children by increasing from three to ten the engineering-college seats that had until now been reserved for them. The reason I state all these details is to show the extent to which, in engineering and in other fields of professional education, this government has introduced innovations and framed schemes so that ordinary, common, poor and rural students can benefit.

In that way, those who studied in engineering college and are leaving today after receiving their degrees may, as younger brother Durai Murugan noted here, send their descendants to this same college for training. Or they may send them to many other colleges. Whoever they may be, whether they are engineers receiving these degrees now or engineers who will receive them in the future, what I ask of all of them is this:

## PDF 211 / printed p.210

Although it is unavoidable that you use this excellent and skilful capacity you have acquired for your own well-being and family life, I ask you to use it also for social life, social uplift and social progress.

Engineering has risen to this level today. It has advanced to the level of computers and to the level of software. In a period before this field had developed, the Kallanai was raised by Karikala Chola. The Brihadisvara Temple at Thanjavur came into being under Rajaraja Chola. At that time there were none of these scientific innovations. Such opportunities and facilities did not exist. Yet, even in an age without them, those structures were built with skill and have stood firm and sound for nearly a thousand years and more, serving even today as places visited by people from across the world. If they shine today in an astonishing manner, we can see to what extent engineering had developed even in that age.

I wish to ask one thing of those in the engineering field. You should not take it wrongly. Whether it is any building, a dam, a riverbed, raising a riverbank, deepening or widening a tank, constructing a college, or even building a tower—

## PDF 212 / printed p.211

in all these things, we must show not only our skill but also our truthfulness.

Whatever the level of skill, if truthfulness is absent, we become people who have forgotten the lesson learned in engineering. To put it pointedly: if a doctor lies or makes a mistake, only one or two lives may be lost. If an engineer makes a mistake or lies, hundreds of lives may be lost.

For example, if a doctor treats two or three people for illness and makes a mistake, the number who ultimately die may be one, two, or five or six. But if a large hall is built and a contractor enters into an agreement, claims that it has been built solidly, yet fails to mix the soil and cement that should be mixed, instead using too much soil and too little cement, then when that hall collapses the engineer may have to bear responsibility for hundreds of lives. That is why the most important thing I have to say in this field is that one must not falsely say, “We have built it well.”

There are so many stories about why one must not lie. There is the Harischandra Purana itself. Why go that far? There is the Tiruvannamalai legend itself. One day in Tiruvannamalai, a quarrel arose between Brahma and Vishnu over who was greater. Vishnu said, “I am the greater one.” Brahma said, “I am the

## PDF 213 / printed p.212

greater one.” Seeing them quarrel, Parameswaran came to them and asked, “Why are you fighting?” They replied, “We are fighting over who is greater.” He asked, “Why fight over this? I will tell you a way. Will you do it?” They asked, “What is it?” Parameswaran said, “Whoever first sees the hair at the top of my head or the sole of my foot, returns and tells me first—he is the greater, the higher one.” Immediately Mahavishnu took the form of a boar and burrowed into the earth to see Siva's feet, because Parameswaran had assumed a cosmic form stretching from sky to earth.

Brahma took the form of a swan and flew upward to see Siva's hair. One flies upward to see the head. The other burrows downward in boar form to see the feet.

While both are trying in this way, a screw-pine flower falls downward from above, from Siva's head. Brahma, in swan form, sees it and asks, “Where are you coming from?” The screw-pine flower says, “I am coming from Parameswaran's head.” Thinking that this was just what he needed, Brahma in swan form asked the screw-pine flower, “You were on his head, were you not?” The flower said, “Yes.” Brahma then said, “In that case, tell Siva one lie. Say that I saw you on his head.”

## PDF 214 / printed p.213

Saying “All right,” the screw-pine flower came to Siva and bore witness: “My Lord, I was on your head. Brahma came then and saw me.”

But Siva discovered the truth. Because he discovered the truth, Brahma, who had lied, was cursed by Parameswaran, and even today he has no temple anywhere. Even now, look: every deity will have a temple, but Brahma will not.

Likewise, he cursed the screw-pine flower that it should no longer be a flower fit for worship. Though every flower may be fit for worship, and though the screw-pine has such fragrance, it is not a flower fit for worship. Because of lying, Brahma too received such a curse in those very days. Such is the story!

Thus this land of Tiruvannamalai is a land that teaches what punishment follows from lying. You who go from this land as engineers must never even dream of falsehood or entertain it in thought; you must think about all the dangers that can arise from falsehood and discharge your duty.

For a responsible task has been entrusted to you today. What I gave you was not merely a degree, nor merely a prize. What I have given is, in a sense, the destiny of the people of this country, placed in your hands. You must reflect on that and act accordingly.

## PDF 215 / printed p.214

By acting in that way, you must add more and more honour to Arunai Engineering College. If someone asks, “Which college did you study in?” and you say, “I studied at the Arunai Engineering College founded and run by younger brother Velu,” that will be fame for you and fame for Velu. And therefore, I say, fame for me too—and I congratulate all of you.
