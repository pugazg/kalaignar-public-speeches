## PDF 240–244 / printed pp.239–243

Karunanidhi opens by recalling his long association with the college and thanks the students for inviting him to inaugurate the Bharati forum. He praises the growing prestige of speaking Tamil in Tamil literary gatherings and dwells on the forum's militant slogan that if blood is shed for classical Tamil, the land itself may turn red.

He interprets red as the colour of revolution, refers to revolutionary change in China and the Soviet Union, and says Tamil Nadu's transformation remains incomplete. He also remarks on democratic life: ruling party and opposition are not merely “two eyes,” he says, but the two sides of a coin—damage either side and the coin loses validity.

## PDF 245–250 / printed pp.244–249

He explains that he learned the assigned topic, “Bharati and the New Women,” only that morning. From there he turns to Bharati's short life, literary productivity and the admiration expressed for him by Bharathidasan.

Karunanidhi then discusses Bharati's prose fiction, especially *Chandrikaiyin Kathai*. He recounts the scene in which a dying mother urges the widow Visalatchi to remarry, reject selfish scriptures written by men, seek a good husband through a widow-remarriage organisation, and protect the newborn child Chandrikai. For Karunanidhi, the importance of the passage lies in how radical such words were in Bharati's own time.

He contrasts that reformist vision with Bharati's own early marriage, observing that the very customs within which Bharati had lived helped him understand why change was necessary. He also retells the schoolboy pun on “Bharati chinna payal,” using it as an example of Bharati's precocious wit and poetic skill.

## PDF 251–255 / printed pp.250–254

The “new woman” Bharati imagined, Karunanidhi argues, was not a matter of fashion but of capability, citizenship and freedom: a woman able to govern, make laws, possess patriotism and language pride, reject superstition and participate in building a scientific society.

He uses examples of mothers shaping national character, including Japan and the mother of Shivaji, and places Bharati alongside figures such as Thiru. Vi. Ka., Periyar and Anna in the struggle for women's advancement.

Karunanidhi quotes Bharati's criticism of parents who celebrate romantic love in drama and epic but violently oppose the same love in their own homes. The hypocrisy is illustrated through the story of Ambikapathi and Amaravathi.

He closes by asking whether Bharati's dream has been fulfilled. Violence against women across India shows, he says, that it has not. Recalling Gandhi's measure of genuine freedom—the ability of a woman to travel safely at midnight—he argues that even daylight safety is not assured in many places. Political independence has been won, but preserving and deepening freedom remains the responsibility of the younger generation.
