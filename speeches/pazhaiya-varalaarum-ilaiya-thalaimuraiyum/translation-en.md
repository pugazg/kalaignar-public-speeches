Karunanidhi begins by welcoming the students and connecting the inauguration of the Tamil forum with Valluvar's ethical teaching. Tamil forums, he argues, should not exist merely as ceremonial student bodies but as places where young people learn the depth of their language and convert that knowledge into responsibility toward language and society.

He then turns to historiography. Tamil history exists, he says, but has not been written in the proper order or from the proper starting point. He invokes Manonmaniam Sundaram Pillai and Vincent Smith to argue that Indian history should be approached from the peninsula and the river valleys of the South rather than beginning automatically from the Ganga. One reason Tamil history has been inadequately preserved, he says, is that ancient rulers took poets rather than professional historians to war. Yet those same literary works—akam, puram and other Tamil classics—remain indispensable source material.

The speech next discusses the Dravidian past as a historical and cultural category rather than merely a political slogan. Karunanidhi cites P. R. Sesh Aiyangar and U. V. Swaminatha Iyer while stressing that scholarship about Dravidian history and Tamil literature came from people of different social backgrounds.

He celebrates the maritime history of ancient Tamilakam and its trade with Rome and Greece, using stories about Roman royalty and Cleopatra's pearls to illustrate the prestige attached to Tamil pearls and other exports. He contrasts that prosperous past with the Tamil Nadu of the present and asks who must restore such strength.

His answer is the younger generation. Earlier generations, he says, are like the scars on a coconut tree: they mark the places from which useful branches have been given, and later generations use those scars as footholds to climb higher. The youth before him are therefore the people who must climb further and produce new benefit for society.

Karunanidhi links this duty to the 1965 anti-Hindi language agitation and argues that language, community, equality and political awareness depend heavily on youth. He then draws a long series of examples from Tamil history and epic tradition: Nedunchezhiyan, Karikala, Abhimanyu, Indrajit and Arjuna. The point is not literal imitation but the capacity of youth for courage, concentration and achievement.

The Arjuna episode becomes a lesson in single-minded purpose: when others see the tree, leaves and branches, Arjuna sees only the target. Karunanidhi extends the same idea to modern leaders, naming Ambedkar, Nehru, Periyar and Anna as people whose public engagement began while they were still young.

The closing pages return to Tamil political history. Anna entered the language struggle before the age of thirty, and the young people who sacrificed their lives in the 1965 agitation are presented as heirs to that spirit. Karunanidhi does not exclude older generations, but insists that tomorrow's Tamil Nadu and India are ultimately in the hands of today's youth.

He ends with a direct call to the younger generation: if freedom is to be preserved, if decline is to be reversed and if a new age of awakening is to emerge, youth must carry the responsibility.
