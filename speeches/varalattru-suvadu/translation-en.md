# Historical Trace

**Speech delivered by Leader Kalaignar  
at the inaugural function of the Students' Union  
of Madurai American College:**

When I came to this college before, the autonomy you asked me for has been granted to you; but the autonomy I asked for has still not been granted. (Applause)

Because the difference between *thannatchi*—autonomy or self-rule—and *thaniyatchi*—separate rule—has not been understood; because there has been no time to understand that difference; and because the time and attention of those above are being used for all sorts of other things (laughter, loud applause), we have not received what we asked for.

In the words *thannatchi* and *thaniyatchi*, if one understands the difference between the Tamil forms **'ன்'** and **'னி'**, they have still not come forward to grant our proper demand for autonomy.

I have come to this college before, when I held responsibility for governing Tamil Nadu, as Chief Minister. The lack of a Chief Minister is still being made up for by our principal. I mean the college principal. (Laughter)

Today I stand before you as the leader of an opposition party. A Chief Minister and an Opposition Leader sharing the same stage is something that has happened in Tamil Nadu! (Applause) It is a sign of the dignity of democracy—

# Chellappa's Sweet Verse!

At the outset, Nedumaran spoke in welcome. That a Nedumaran from Madurai should welcome me (loud applause) is something that gives me joy!

True to the college principal's name, “Chellappa,” he has sung a *vellappa* about me in the name of a eulogy. (Laughter) He has sung a verse as sweet as jaggery.

Whether or not I deserve the words of distinction offered about me by the college principal, by Nedumaran who delivered the welcome address, and by Srinivasagam who presides over this union is open to question. Yet I take it that they welcomed me in keeping with the Tamil custom of welcoming guests wholeheartedly and unstintingly.

If the words of praise that have been offered can encourage me, give me enthusiasm, and help me work without equal as I move more swiftly along my path, then I accept those words of praise in a personal sense.

Today, the Indian subcontinent is in a state of agitation—a situation caught within the question mark of who governs us and who is going to govern us.

Students whose minds can easily become subject to agitation and tension have gathered in this great hall without agitation, without tension, and without clamour, so that the long-standing fame of American College may spread in all eight directions, and in support of this union—

A country should contain people who are aware of its history.

A country may possess any number of ancient distinctions. But only when it is filled with people who understand the history that belongs to that country can it attain a level of growth capable of competing with the world.

In that sense, even historians in India, thinking together or thinking individually about the histories that belong to us, have given us many historical works.

# By the Riverbank: The Truth Anna Spoke!

As far as Tamil Nadu is concerned, scholars and poets have a major grievance. Many years ago, when Perarignar Anna delivered a lecture at Annamalai University, he referred to the question of whether the history of India ought to be written from the banks of the Kaveri or begun from the banks of the Ganga. The subject assigned to his lecture was “By the Riverbank”!

Although the title “By the Riverbank” was given to him suddenly, he was not startled; he did not hesitate; nor was he bewildered as to how he was going to speak on that subject.

It was in that address, “By the Riverbank,” that he said: “It was a mistake to begin the history of India from the banks of the Ganga; it should have been begun from the banks of the Kaveri.”

The reason he said so was that he also cited the record describing the Tamil people as the ancient people who “arose before stone appeared, before earth appeared.” The Lemuria continent, which joined India and Sri Lanka, was at one time lost to a sea-deluge. That place too has the qualification for history to begin from there.

As early as twenty-one years before the birth of Christ, Thirumavalavan Karikala Peruvallattan ruled with Puhar and Uraiyur as his capitals, with fame known across the earth!

At that time, envoys from this Pandya region were in the imperial court in the city of Rome discussing the relationship between Tamil Nadu and Rome; that too is among our historical records.

Can we read all these things in detail in history books? We cannot!

Fortunately, the literature composed by Sangam-age poets—the verses praising the kings who lived in those days—and the copper-plate records all form the background from which we can assemble our history. It is by using that background—those literary works—that the people of Tamil Nadu must shape their history.

History does not mean only the histories of the periods when the ancient Tamils ruled; our Tamil language, an ancient language, has a history of its own!

The research conclusion of scholars is that grammar was formulated for Tamil roughly three thousand years ago. Although some scholars differ, they do not reduce three thousand years to two thousand; rather, they say that Tamil had a grammar as early as four thousand years ago. There are scholars who say that Tolkappiyar formulated the grammar of Tamil, but there are no scholars who conceal its antiquity.

Even amid such differences of opinion, we know that grammar was established for classical Tamil in the long-distant past.

Even Tolkappiyan had a teacher. His name was Atankottu Asan; he was born in a village called Atankodu in Kanyakumari district. That village exists even today in the southern Kanyakumari district, and Tolkappiyar was his student.

If the period in which a language acquired grammar itself goes back three or four thousand years, we must think about how far back the historical period in which that language arose must go!

Beginning by communicating through primal sounds—then beginning to communicate through hand-signs—then moving the lips to convey what one was thinking—after that, when the two lips close the sound “m” arises, and when those lips open the sound “a” arises; that becomes “imma,” “amma”; then it changes into “ippa,” “appa” (the students applaud and cheer with an exclamation of “Appappa....!”); after that, they themselves gave names to the things they saw and called them so; starting as rough Tamil and then gradually gaining richness—if it had attained grammatical refinement four thousand years ago, it must be a language older than five thousand years.

If Tamil is the one such ancient language in the world, no one can deny it! (Applause)

—Thus, our language too has a history of its own!
