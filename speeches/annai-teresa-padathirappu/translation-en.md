# Unveiling of Mother Teresa's Portrait

Karunanidhi begins by apologising for arriving late because of official duties and says he must leave after paying tribute so that he can return to those duties.

Recalling remarks he had given to Chennai television and radio after Mother Teresa's death, he says that in a world where hawks and vultures circle, the **`அன்னப்பட்சி`** that had been swimming in the pond of social service had disappeared. He then develops the Tamil wordplay around `அன்னம்`: it can refer to a bird, to Mother Teresa, and to the food she gave to the poor. He says that rather than saying Mother Teresa has disappeared, it is more fitting to say that this white dove of peace, friend of the poor and beacon of hope for the distressed, now fills the hearts of people throughout the world.

He recalls that Mother Teresa received the Padma Shri in April 1962, when Jawaharlal Nehru was Prime Minister. More memorable than the award itself, he says, were Nehru's words about seeing her receive it. Nehru compared the sight of Mother Teresa taking the award from the President's hands to the sight of her tenderly carrying a gravely ill child in both arms. Karunanidhi recounts that Nehru told his sister Vijayalakshmi Pandit that the scene moved him so deeply that he could scarcely control his tears.

Karunanidhi next recalls a tribute attributed to West Bengal Chief Minister B. C. Roy on Roy's eightieth birthday. Asked about Mother Teresa, Roy said that in his old age the first person who came to his mind was not Mahatma Gandhi, with whom he had worked in the freedom struggle, nor his close friend Jawaharlal Nehru, nor even relatives and intimate associates. Instead, as he climbed the steps to his office, the single figure filling his heart was the frail form of Teresa, who had surrendered her entire life to protecting destitute people, the sick and children and to wiping away their tears.

Karunanidhi says that B. C. Roy's sentiment belongs not to Roy alone but to everyone. Mother Teresa should not be thought of as having simply left or separated from them. Her labour, incomparable service, compassion for poor people, tenderness toward the sick and concern for the distressed have become permanently imprinted in their hearts.

He concludes that the true tribute is to put those values into practice. With hearts softer than flowers, people should show love and compassion toward the poor. He calls on the gathering to follow that path and to make it the pledge of the memorial occasion.
