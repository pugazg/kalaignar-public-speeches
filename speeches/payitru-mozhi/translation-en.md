## Medium of Instruction

The students of Madras Christian College have formed a Tamil association and, through it, are conducting a Muthamizh festival. This is something to be praised, congratulated and celebrated. I can see the enthusiasm of you, my student friends, at this function.

Speaking at a gathering of students gives strength to the speaker. Sometimes the speaker needs that strength!

Every adult has first been a student. Every student should regard student life as a golden period, thinking that in the future he may have to guide students or shoulder important responsibilities in the country. Not even a little of this period should be wasted. Student life is an age without fear, an age that can express ideas with speed and force. It is in that stage that plans for the future arise in our hearts, are stored there, and one day become useful. No one should forget this.

## PDF 84 / printed p.83

Just as the students of this Christian College are attached to Tamil, I see this attachment budding today in many colleges across Tamil Nadu. We must not forget that for five or six years in between, Tamil attachment had dimmed somewhat. In 1938 the streets of Tamil Nadu rang with the call that Tamil children, young people and students should possess Tamil consciousness, love for Tamil and an unquenchable affection for it. I do not wish to enter into the political analysis of why that happened. In the years that followed, an atmosphere arose in which people felt that Tamil could do everything, that Tamil would sustain us, take us to the shore and support us; and that if harm came to Tamil we must protect it even by giving our bodies or our lives.

Then, for a period, the doubt arose: can Tamil provide us a livelihood? We began to forget Tamil. The fear that forgetting Tamil would destroy Tamil feeling, the resurgence of Tamil society and Tamil tradition has caused Tamil consciousness to rise again. We see that with joy.

Tamil has many distinctions not possessed by other languages. We do not say this merely because we are Tamils. Foreign scholars who took part in the World Tamil Conference, even though they approached Tamil through other languages, now speak in world forums about Tamil’s greatness.

## PDF 85 / printed p.84

They recognise that the culture, civilisation and history of the Tamils possess distinctions beyond many other literary traditions in the world.

Even the word “Tamil” itself contains a uniqueness not found in other language names. Consider the name Tamil. Within that very name are features of our phonology: “த” represents vallinam, the hard consonants; “மி” contains mellinam, the soft consonants; “ழ்” represents idaiyinam, the medial consonants. One letter from each of the three classes is strung together like pearls to form the name Tamil.

Tamil civilisation, trade and valour did not remain within Tamil Nadu. They crossed the seas as far as Kadaram. Tamil valour was written on the walls of the Himalayas. The tiger, bow and fish—the emblems of the Tamil dynasties—were engraved on mountain peaks. Cheran Senguttuvan marched north, made Kanaka and Vijaya carry the stone on their crowned heads, brought it south and erected the statue of Kannagi. This is not merely a story; it is a chapter in our heroic history.

Through valour, commerce and cultural relations, the Tamil had contact with countries across the world and spread his fame widely.

I ask one thing of my student friends. I too was once a student.

## PDF 86 / printed p.85

But not like you. I never saw a college. My formal education ended with high school. The only “college” I studied in was the college of Kanchipuram, not an ordinary college! Yet even in my final school years, Tamil had attracted me deeply. I mention my attachment not to speak personally about myself but to show that many students of that time displayed such concern for Tamil.

In 1938 another language was compulsorily imposed in Tamil Nadu. I was then studying in Thiruvarur. Even at that age, surrounded by hundreds of students, I participated in a peaceful agitation to explain that an alien-language imposition could cause Tamil to decline.

Agitation does not merely mean shouting or taking part in violence. Whether on the path of Mahatma Gandhi or the path of Arignar Anna, one method of agitation is to express what lies in one’s heart. I followed that method. To protect Tamil from domination by another language, we composed Tamil songs and ran publications.

When I came to this college today, student friends demonstrated how telegrams could be sent in Tamil. They also showed an inscription concerning the older Tamil form of a place-name. After I sat down here, they showed me a telegram in Tamil saying, “I wish this festival to proceed sweetly.”

## PDF 87 / printed p.86

I saw that Tamil can accomplish everything. Yet I regret that our students today do not possess Tamil feeling with the intensity students possessed in 1938, 1942 and 1944, or after an interval in 1965. I want the feeling that existed in 1965—not a repetition of everything that happened then.

Whenever Perarignar Anna spoke at a conference or function, or wrote an article, he gave even greater importance than politics to Tamil, the Tamil language, Tamil society and the protection of Tamil tradition. He held a firm conviction about them.

Students today may belong to any political party. But Tamil is the national language of our State, our own life-breath. The feeling for Tamil must live within us like the blood cells and pulse of our bodies. Politics has no place in that. It is precisely because this is not a party matter that we say Tamil can do everything: Tamil can be a medium of instruction; science and geography can be taught in Tamil; technical and scientific terms can be created in Tamil.

## PDF 88 / printed p.87

A beautiful book entitled “Tamil Can” was published by C. Subramaniam to show that Tamil can accomplish all this. But “Tamil can” still remains largely in the form of a book rather than being fully realised in practice.

In 1968, seeking to give it practical form, Perarignar Anna, then Chief Minister, moved in the Tamil Nadu Legislative Assembly a resolution that within five years Tamil would become the medium of instruction. It was passed unanimously. But because of political difficulties that followed, doubts among students, and economic anxieties—whether one who studied only in Tamil could secure his own and his family’s welfare—we have struggled to prove, within the five-year period Anna envisaged, that Tamil can indeed serve as the medium.

Can the Government act directly? It can. But the Government expects cooperation from students; I expect it too. You told me through a telegram that Tamil can do everything; you showed me inscriptions demonstrating Tamil’s capacity. Develop such attachment and affection for Tamil that you can turn around and ask me: “When everything can be done in Tamil, why have you still not put it fully into practice?” That is the true way to make Tamil live and grow.
