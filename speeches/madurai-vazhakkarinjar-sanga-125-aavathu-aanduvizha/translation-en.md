### Madurai Bar Association 125th Anniversary

When I was invited to speak, they invited me by pointing out, “There is expectation inside the hall—and expectation outside as well.”

In the Tamil expression “எதிர் பார்ப்பு” (“expectation”), if a few letters in the middle are removed, it becomes “எதிர்ப்பு” (“opposition”). (Applause)

Opposition inside the hall, opposition outside as well. (Applause) Since I am the Chief Minister, I am one who has to face opposition in the Legislative Assembly too. I also have to face opposition that appears here and there outside. I stand before you with the confidence that all of you have given me the strength to face both these forms of opposition.

The Madurai Bar Association—the lawyers' association—was registered in 1915, as was pointed out in the welcome address. At that time, only one hundred lawyers were members of this association. But although this association was begun in 1872 with four barristers and approximately

## PDF 95 / printed p.94

50 lawyers, I am proud when I think that today, in a way that gives all of us reason for happiness, 2,500 lawyers are registered in this association.

I can also understand that it is because this association has grown to this extent that you have placed some important demands here.

You too know that if there is no demand at a function attended by ministers, that function has no flavour. I, who attend such functions, know it very well too.

The Chief Justice of the Chennai High Court, who is presiding over this function, and Justice Raju have spoken about the responsibilities of lawyers and the responsibilities of the courts.

Law Minister Aladi Aruna, making use of this opportunity, referred—without pointing them out while still pointing them out—to the lawyers' agitations taking place nearby, and requested that they all give up the protest. I second what he has moved. (Applause)

You may ask, “How is that possible? He is one of the ministers. How can the Chief Minister second what he has moved?” I want to point out to you that it was I who asked him to move it, and I am seconding it. (Applause)

## PDF 96 / printed p.95

Whatever the issue may be, as far as lawyers are concerned, if they display their fighting ability in the courts through argument, I am one who would welcome it. From whichever side and for whichever party they argue—even if they argue for injustice, perhaps because of their profession, arguing on a professional basis for something unjust—I am one who greatly appreciates that skill in argument. Therefore, I wish to state at this function, with great humility and affection, that their struggle should remain in the skill of argument and that lawyers themselves should not take the law into their own hands. You may therefore ask, “How are we to make our voice echo? How are we to express our thoughts? How are we to draw the government's attention?”

If, while I am walking, you merely clap your hands from some distance in order to draw my attention, I will turn around. You can attract my attention. There is no need for you to call me only by tapping me on the back.

Whatever your view may be on any issue, agitations are not necessary for that; protests are not necessary; one telephone is enough. To go even further, in all of India, when the telephone rings, the one Chief Minister who picks it up directly himself, without assistants first taking it and handing it over, is

## PDF 97 / printed p.96

me. Therefore, whatever the issue may be, you can contact me and speak to me.

Which town needs a court? In which town should a court not be removed? In which town should a new court be built? None of these requires a protest. Speak; hold discussions. A situation may arise in which I have to call both sides and speak to them. If in any matter you do not obtain justice on your side, invite me myself to lead that protest. I am ready to come. (Loud applause) I stand before you obliged to tell you this.

We have to obtain the law, and the benefit of the law, through lawyers. That is why Perarignar Anna said: “The law is a dark room; in it, the lawyer's argument is a lamp. Ordinary people are unable to obtain that bright lamp.” Today there are many lawyers in Tamil Nadu who have prepared themselves so that ordinary people too can obtain that bright lamp. I take immense pleasure in thinking that many such lawyers are members of this association, and that for 125 years this association has prospered, gained fame, and introduced distinguished people to Tamil Nadu and to India.

## PDF 98 / printed p.97

It was said here that great experts had emerged from this association. You were also happy to see some senior lawyers called here and honoured by me, by our Justice Liberhan, and by Raju. You offered your congratulations. It was not only such distinguished lawyers; those who are members of the Legislative Assembly were also called here and honoured. You saw that too. I too watched it with happiness.

Not only that: the cultured Tamilavel P. T. Rajan, who had been a member of this Bar Association, served as a minister in the Justice Party government when the Tamil region was the Chennai Province. I wish to point out with great satisfaction that the Madurai Bar Association has the distinction of having produced many such ministers.

P. T. Rajan's son, P. T. R. Palanivel Rajan, was also a member of this association. You know that he now sits as the Speaker of the Tamil Nadu Legislative Assembly.

Our friend Madhavan was a member of this Bar Association here—he served as Law Minister of Tamil Nadu in the DMK government.

O. P. Raman was a lawyer here. He served as Fisheries Minister in the DMK government. Younger brother Pon. Muthuramalingam was a member of this Bar Association—he held responsibility in the DMK government as Food Minister and as Information Minister.

## PDF 99 / printed p.98

Likewise, Sathyamurthy served as a minister in the AIADMK government. He too was a member of this Bar Association. Santhosham served with great responsibility as a member of the Tamil Nadu Legislative Council. In this way, many lawyers, many learned people, and many martyrs have been given to the Tamil people.

Here our friend, senior lawyer Vai. Sankaran, was invited as an honoured person and given an award. You all know very well that he is the son of Madurai Vaidyanatha Iyer; how great a figure of sacrifice Madurai Vaidyanathan was; that he took part in the freedom struggle; and that even in those days he accomplished the extraordinary act of personally leading oppressed people into the Madurai temple for temple entry. This Bar Association has contained such distinguished people. We are celebrating its 125th anniversary here with dignity and distinction.

You have spoken in detail about the court and about the branch of the High Court that is to be created here. Not only now; whenever I come to Madurai, you have asked on behalf of the Bar Association. You have asked through reporters and obtained answers. You have even come to Chennai and asked. We too, as Aladi Aruna pointed out, have been engaged in that effort for a long time.

## PDF 100 / printed p.99

As far as Madurai is concerned, it has been more than two hundred years since courts began functioning here. They once had the distinction that judgments were delivered in Tamil in Madurai as early as 1803. In the beginning, even the major courts here functioned only in small buildings. Later, all those courts were moved to Tirumalai Nayakkar Mahal, which had served as the palace of the Madurai Nayaks, and the courts functioned in Tirumalai Nayakkar Mahal.

Up to 1970, the functioning of the courts in Tirumalai Nayakkar Mahal, situated in a traffic-congested part of the centre of Madurai city, had become a problem. It had become a difficulty. Therefore, when the demand arose that the courts in Tirumalai Nayakkar Mahal should be moved elsewhere so that they could function separately, the DMK government, for the first time, sought to bring all of them together and make them function in a better manner. After Perarignar Anna's death, during the period when I assumed responsibility, I expanded further and accelerated the effort that had begun during Anna's time. Buildings for the Madurai courts were completed, and on 16 June 1970 it was I who inaugurated that court complex. (Applause) Our friend even pointed this out in the welcome address.

He asked, “You are the one who inaugurated the court itself. Should you not inaugurate its branch?” You raised applause and said, “Yes.”

## PDF 101 / printed p.100

I inaugurated it. If I continue to be there, I will come and inaugurate it. Even if I do not continue to be there, I will come again and inaugurate it. (Loud applause) I give you that assurance.

You all know that the Justice Jaswant Singh Commission has recommended establishing here a branch of the Chennai High Court—that is, a bench. In 1972, during the period when I was Chief Minister, we argued and struggled with the Central Government that, accepting that recommendation, a branch should be established in Madurai; and after many years we have obtained the necessary permission. You know that too.

At present, for establishing that High Court branch, a site has been selected at Ulaganeri village in Madurai district, and the necessary land-acquisition work is now under way. Six notifications under Section 6 of the Land Acquisition Act were published in the Tamil Nadu Gazette, the government gazette, on 11-3-98. I convey to you my confidence that all that work will be completed by the end of May.

For this land-acquisition expenditure, on 16-4-98 the government sanctioned two crore and fifty-two lakh rupees and issued the order. (Loud applause) I state that here. After this land is acquired, I cannot on my own say that the branch will be established here. We are now in a period when what ought to be said by some is being said by all sorts of people to all sorts of others. (Loud

## PDF 102 / printed p.101

applause) Because this is something Chief Justice Liberhan has to determine, decide, and announce. I cannot announce it myself. He is the one who must announce it.

There are now two issues. One is this: if that branch must be created here urgently and quickly, it can first be created temporarily in the existing old building; while it is functioning there, a new building can be constructed on the newly acquired site and the High Court branch can then be established there. We have to choose one of these two routes. Together, all of us entrust that responsibility to the Chennai High Court. I have confidence that they will understand that responsibility and act on it immediately.

Therefore, as far as this demand is concerned, it is not in my hands; but it is in our hands. In that word “our” is our High Court's Justice Liberhan. Therefore, at this pleasant function, I give you the assurance that we will make every effort within our power to bring this matter that is in our hands to an early conclusion. I thank all of you and take my leave.
