## Kambar Festival (2)

I have already explained the question, “Why do I participate in the Kambar Festival?” Since I have given that explanation before, I do not think it is necessary to repeat it today.

The Tamil people of Karaikudi are capable of receiving an explanation once and keeping it firmly in mind. I therefore believe there is no need to explain the same thing again and again.

This government — and we who belong to the ruling party that runs it — have no hostility whatever toward Kamban's scholarship. One can know that simply from the fact that we took steps to erect a statue for Kamban and placed it in a prominent position on the Chennai seafront.

As soon as I came and sat in the chair, Sa. Ganesan came behind me. Those of you seated opposite may have thought he was asking, “Why did you come late?” Instead, he warned me: “Please do not move the chair. This umbrella is attached to your chair. If you move the chair, the umbrella will fall.”

## PDF 43 / printed p.42

I understood that very well.

I am deeply obliged to thank the elder Sa. Ganesan for lovingly warning me that if my chair moved, the umbrella would topple. Although his warning applied to this stage, I also appreciate all of you who understood where else that idea might apply.

This annual festival should continue with greater facilities and greater opportunities. In that way we are today beginning to write, on a broader scale, a new chapter showing that Tamils will not forget their poets and will always discharge their debt of gratitude to Tamil scholars.

I am weighing my words carefully: I said that we are “beginning today to write it on a broader scale.”

Thirty-five years ago Ganesan had already begun in Karaikudi the work of paying that debt of gratitude to Kamban. Today that effort is being expanded.

One example is the new form being given to Poompuhar. Another is the government's distinguished effort to build, at a cost of many lakhs of rupees, a great memorial for Thiruvalluvar in Chennai.

## PDF 44 / printed p.43

Arrangements of this kind should be made throughout Tamil Nadu.

Because Kamban is said to have stayed at Nattarasankottai, there should be a memorial there; in nearby Karaikudi there is a memorial hall for Kamban. Likewise, Tamil Nadu has countless villages and towns where great poets lived, were born and moved about. Even if we cannot build expensive monuments everywhere, erecting smaller memorials in such places would help keep Tamil awakening evergreen in the hearts of the people.

Two years ago, Education Minister Dr. Navalar and I were travelling by car. We saw the village of Okkur, learned that Okkur Maasathiyar had been born there, and arranged for one of her poems to be inscribed on stone in a place where everyone could see it.

At least to that extent, memorials and memorial halls should be raised for the great poets of Tamil Nadu — the poets of the Akananuru and Purananuru, writers of Sangam literature, Ilango Adigal, Thiruvalluvar, Kamban and others like them.

## PDF 45 / printed p.44

My wish is that speaking at the Kambar Festival about Kamban's special qualities and the beauty of the Tamil epic he gave us should increasingly help the growth of Tamil.

The characters Kamban created for his Ramayana are Kamban's own creations. Between Valmiki's characters and Kamban's there are not merely differences; at times there are contradictions.

If Valmiki's work is accepted as the original Ramayana, then the characters as Valmiki conceived them would be the original characters. Later translations, or works based on Valmiki's Ramayana, reshape those characters according to each writer's power, circumstances, period and temperament. I know that neither supporters nor critics of the Kamba Ramayanam can deny this.

Many Tamil scholars praise Kamban's beautiful Tamil and analyse his characters in minute detail. A collection of essays by some of them is titled *Kambar Kaviyamutham*. I read it on my way here.

## PDF 46 / printed p.45

When the question arises what Kamban's Ayodhya was like, we must ask whether he was describing a country that actually existed in that form, or imagining the country as he believed it ought to be. Thiru G.K. Sundaram argued here that Ayodhya truly was as Kamban described it. No one should imagine that the views expressed here are meant merely to defeat one another in debate. The Kamba Ramayanam is a vast Tamil work that invites such criticism.

So when people put forward differing views, they should be understood as literary criticism of Kamban, not as an attempt to insult him.

G.K. Sundaram referred to Rajaji's observation that under Dasaratha's rule punishment was administered without distinguishing between rich and poor. Some describe Ayodhya through Kamban's poetic lines: “There was no generosity, because there was no poverty.” There were no benefactors because there was no one forced by poverty to stretch out a hand. And because of the absence of inequality, Kamban says in effect: “All attained every great wealth; there were neither those without nor those who possessed.”

## PDF 47 / printed p.46

But does that mean those conditions literally existed there? Or does it prove Kamban's dream that such conditions ought to exist? We must not forget to ask.

If we say that Ayodhya in the Ramayana age really had no poor people, Kamban himself contradicts us elsewhere. When the time is fixed for Rama's coronation and Kooni resolves to have Bharata crowned instead, she agitates Kaikeyi's mind. She tells Kaikeyi that if Rama is crowned, Kaikeyi will lose all influence and no one will seek her help. Then she asks what Kaikeyi will do when suffering people, driven by poverty, come to her begging.

Kamban gives Kooni the verse:

“தூண்டும் இன்னலும் வறுமையும்
தொடர்தரத், துயரால்
ஈண்டு வந்துனை இரப்பவர்க்கு,
இருநிதி அவளை
வேண்டி ஈதியோ? வெள்குதியோ?
விம்மல் நோயால்
மாண்டு போதியோ? மறுத்தியோ?
எங்ஙனம் வாழ்தி?”

If people can come begging to Kaikeyi because of poverty,

## PDF 48 / printed p.47

then that itself shows that there were people in want in Ayodhya.

Kamban therefore was not saying that the country of the Ramayana age actually existed in that ideal form. He was imagining how a country ought to be. The dream expressed in “there are none without; there are none who possess” is not a modern socialist slogan. Kamban voiced it as early as the ninth century.

Valluvar had voiced the idea before him, and Kamban expanded it. We should not deceive ourselves by saying the ideal had already been achieved. Kooni's words to Kaikeyi prove otherwise. Kooni is not speaking to us, but when Kooni speaks, it is Kamban who makes her speak.

In literature, characters speak through the writer who created them. That does not mean the writer personally owns every harsh word spoken by a character; but it is the writer's task to use characters to illuminate the conditions around them. In that sense, the poet-emperor Kamban

## PDF 49 / printed p.48

showed the world through his epic both how a country ought to be and how a country actually is.

Sa. Ganesan said that the Kamba Ramayanam was first staged in A.D. 886. In A.D. 887, at Srirangam, it was presented under the leadership of the great Vaishnavite acharya Nathamuni, and thousands gathered to hear Kamban expound each verse.

I read in *Kamban Kaviyamutham* that Nathamuni considered including the Kamba Ramayanam in the Divya Prabandham, but one issue prevented it.

Kamban never forgot Sadaiyappa Vallal, who supported and nurtured him, and praised him in many places in the epic. At the height of that gratitude he sang:

“அரியணை அனுமன் தாங்க,
அங்கதன் உடைவாள் ஏந்தப்
பரதன் வெண்குடை கவிக்க
இருவரும் கவரி வீச,
விரை செறி கமலத் தாள் சேர்
வெண்ணெய்மண் சடையன் தங்கள்
மரபுளோர் கொடுக்க வாங்கி
வசிட்டனே புனைந்தான் மெளலி”

In other words, even the crown used for Rama's coronation is poetically linked to the lineage of Sadaiyappa Vallal.

## PDF 50 / printed p.49

Where is Rama's Ayodhya, and where is Tamil Nadu, the land of Sadaiyappa Vallal's ancestors? Kamban joins the two. In doing so he also expresses the gratitude he owed Sadaiyappa Vallal.

Nathamuni is said to have told Kamban that a human being, Sadaiyappa Vallal, had been praised in the Ramayana and that such praise of a man should not have a place in a sacred work devoted to gods. If the lines praising Sadaiyappa were removed, the work could be included in the Divya Prabandham.

Kamban refused completely. Whether or not the work entered the Prabandham, he would not erase his gratitude. What he had sung about Sadaiyappa Vallal and his ancestors had to remain. Because Kamban stood firm, the work did not enter the Prabandham — and, fortunately, his gratitude remained in the poem.

## PDF 51 / printed p.50

But was that argument itself sound? Was praise of a human being truly an obstacle?

In the same Kamba Ramayanam, Vibhishana, though born among the asuras, joins Rama's side. Kamban can praise him. Elsewhere Kamban places gods, human beings and asuras in a hierarchy and sings that humanity has surpassed the other groups:

“ஆறுகொள் சடிலத் தானும்,
அயனும்நின் றிவர்கள் ஆதி
வேறுள குழுவை எல்லாம்
மானுடம் வென்ற யன்றே”

If humanity itself is praised so highly, why should a noble human benefactor not be praised? Songs about Vibhishana, born among the asuras, could supposedly be accepted, yet a song praising Sadaiyappa Vallal, a human being, could not. I mention this so that you will remember the kind of opposition the Kamba Ramayanam itself faced in that period.

## PDF 52 / printed p.51

Gods, humans, asuras — humans stand between the other two. Is it reasonable to say that a work cannot enter the Prabandham because it praises a human being? It could praise Vibhishana, an asura who first erred and later reformed, but could not praise Sadaiyappa Vallal, who gave generously without such fault. This is not an attack on Kamban; it is a criticism of the social conditions of Kamban's age.

Did that age end in A.D. 887, or has it continued? I leave the answer to your thought.

G.K. Sundaram also referred to the killing of Vali. Rajaji's defence, as quoted, was that Rama had taken a human incarnation and humans can make mistakes; therefore those mistakes should not be magnified. But if a god takes human form in order to reform human beings, can the incarnation be used instead to excuse wrongdoing?

## PDF 53 / printed p.52

On the Vali episode, therefore, no one really accepts Rama's conduct without difficulty. We must regard these figures as literary characters rather than surround them with additional imagination.

More importantly, rather than merely having me or others say that Rama's attack on Vali from concealment was wrong, Kamban makes Rama himself say it. Where? Not in the Vali episode. He says it after Ravana has died. Those who have read the Kamba Ramayanam may ask, “What new story is this? I do not remember him saying so.”

Ravana lies dead on the battlefield. Rama comes with Ravana's younger brother Vibhishana and looks at him. He sees a scar on Ravana's back. Rama grieves: “Alas! Did I kill a man who bore a scar on his back? Is it worthy of a warrior to kill one who had been wounded on the back? Did I kill a man marked on his back?”

Kamban gives the verse:

“மாண்டொழிந்து உலகில் நிற்கும்
வயங்கிசை முயங்க மாட்டாது,
ஊண் தொழில் உகந்து தெவ்வர்
முறுவலன் புகழை உண்ணப்
பூண் தொழில் உடைய மார்பா!
போர்ப்புறங் கொடுத்தோர் போன்ற
ஆண் தொழிலோனிற் பெற்ற
வெற்றியும் அவத்தம் என்றான்.”

## PDF 54 / printed p.53

Then Vibhishana says — or rather, Kamban makes Vibhishana say:

“That scar did not come from turning his back in battle, Rama! Once before, my elder brother Ravana fought an elephant. The elephant's tusks entered his chest, pierced through him and emerged above his back. That is how the scar was formed.”

Kamban then gives the verses:

“நாடுள தனையும் ஒடி
நண்ணலார்க் காண்கி லாமற்
பீடுள குன்றம் போலும்
பெருந்திசை எல்லை யானைக்
கோடுள தனையும் புக்குக்
கொடும் புழுத் தழுந்து புண்ணின்
பாடுளது அன்றித் தெவ்வர்
படைக்கலம் பட்டு என் செய்யும்?”

“அப்பனை அனைத்தும் மார்புக்கு
அணி எனக் கிடந்த; வீரக்
கைப்பணை முழங்க மேனாள்
அமரிடைக் கிடைத்த காலன
துப்பனை வயிர வாளி
விசையிலும், காலின் தோன்றல்
வெப்பனை குத்தினாலும்,
வெறிநடைப் போய அன்றே.”

Kamban thus makes Vibhishana bring out Ravana's valour. Rama himself is therefore troubled: we have killed a man who bore a scar upon his back —

## PDF 55 / printed p.54

and disgrace may come upon us! I say this so that those who argue for Kamban and for Rama may reflect on how long Rama, who lays down the code of valour, must have suffered mental anguish over having killed Vali from concealment.

Even here I am arguing on Rama's side. He suffers so deeply at the thought that he has killed a man bearing a scar on his back — that his own martial standing has been damaged and ruined. We must therefore also consider how much he must have regretted the act of killing Vali from concealment.

Characters should be treated as characters. Our difficulty begins when, instead of honouring the poets who create epics, we begin to give excessive reverence to the characters they create. Professor Sundaram Pillai wrote *Manonmaniam*. We remember him, but we have not turned Jeevakan, Purudothaman and Manonmani into deities to be worshipped.

In Kamban's case, however, a situation arose in which his characters received excessive reverence while the poet who created the epic was forgotten. Thirty-five years ago, our Sa. Ganesan began a new revolution in Karaikudi to change that tendency —

## PDF 56 / printed p.55

by holding Kambar Festivals on the principle that the person who created the epic is the hero and is the one worthy of praise, honour and appreciation. I congratulate and salute this festival for continuing to establish that the distinction belongs to Kamban.
