# Inauguration of Dr. Ambedkar Law University

Karunanidhi calls the day a long-awaited and happy one and welcomes President K. R. Narayanan on his first visit to Tamil Nadu after assuming the presidency. He says the occasion is additionally significant because Narayanan is inaugurating what Karunanidhi describes as India's first law university named after Ambedkar.

Karunanidhi praises Narayanan as a leader whose thoughts remain centred on ordinary, poor, middle-class, backward, most-backward and oppressed people even while occupying Rashtrapati Bhavan. He compares this quality with the remembered image of Lenin in the Kremlin and says Narayanan's presence gives the gathering great joy.

He then traces the development of government law colleges in Tamil Nadu: the first in Chennai in 1891, a second at Madurai in 1974 during his government, later institutions at Tiruchirappalli and Coimbatore in 1979, and another at Tirunelveli in 1996. Because the government law colleges had been affiliated to different universities, the new institution is intended to bring them under one university.

Karunanidhi says this is not the first time his government has honoured Ambedkar's name. During Ambedkar's centenary observances in 1990, the Chennai Law College was named after Ambedkar; an arts college started in Perambur in 1972 had also been given Ambedkar's name. He also recalls Tamil Nadu's support for the demand to name Marathwada University after Ambedkar, including a campaign of telegrams to the Maharashtra government and responses from Governor Alexander and Chief Minister Sharad Pawar.

He explains that the Dravidian movement's attachment to Ambedkar comes from the common principles taught by Periyar and Anna: opposition to caste and religious inequality, rejection of social hierarchy, and commitment to equality.

Karunanidhi then quotes Ambedkar extensively in English on caste. The first quotation argues that social progress is impossible without changing the social order and that anything built on caste foundations will crack rather than become a whole. A second quotation argues that the decisive remedy for caste is inter-marriage, because the fusion of blood creates a feeling of kinship capable of overcoming caste-created separateness. A third states that inter-dining and inter-marriage must become ordinary practice before caste can cease to operate as a social force.

He says these ideas remain relevant even during the golden-jubilee year of Indian independence. He recalls that in 1974 his government encouraged inter-caste marriages by awarding one-and-a-half sovereigns of gold to couples where one spouse belonged to an oppressed community. In 1989–90, support was announced at ₹5,000; after returning to office in 1996 it was raised to ₹10,000. He adds that at the recent anti-untouchability conference in Madurai he announced ₹20,000 in assistance where one spouse in an inter-caste marriage was an Adi Dravidar.

Karunanidhi also mentions `சமபந்தி` community meals taking place at temples and elsewhere, describing such measures as following the paths of Periyar and Anna, the ideas of Gandhi, and the principles of Ambedkar.

He then draws the President's attention to another initiative: the government's plan to establish **`சமத்துவபுரம்`** settlements in fifty locations across Tamil Nadu, with two hundred houses in each location for families belonging to different social communities to live together.

He concludes by again welcoming President Narayanan, the Governor and the other dignitaries and extends his heartfelt wishes to the university.
