Karunanidhi begins at the Government Arts College in Tiruvarur by joking about the unusually large crowd and the congestion around the stage. He says that in a normal college function he would have preferred to speak directly with students about their concerns rather than turn the occasion into a political public meeting.

He reflects on graduates leaving college without any guarantee of employment. Education should not be treated merely as a route to a salaried job, he argues, although India's economic conditions often force students to think that way. Graduates should also use their learning, research and skills to create work, serve society and improve the lives of others.

Karunanidhi then distinguishes exceptional public figures who had little formal education from the general need for students to study seriously. Kamaraj, Periyar and Karunanidhi himself cannot be used as excuses for neglecting education: “exceptions are not guides.”

The speech shifts sharply to caste conflict among students. Karunanidhi laments reports of caste violence in colleges and asks how caste prejudice could enter institutions that are supposed to cultivate new thought. This provides the setting for the title, drawn from the Tirukkural:

> “பிறப்பொக்கும் எல்லா உயிர்க்கும் சிறப்பொவ்வா
> செய்தொழில் வேற்றுமை யான்”

He interprets the couplet as declaring equality by birth. Differences in “சிறப்பு” arise not from birth but from the manner in which people act and perform their work. The speech illustrates this with administrators, musicians and teachers: people holding the same occupation do not all earn the same respect, because distinction comes from the quality and character of their conduct.

Karunanidhi connects this principle to reservation policy. Caste should ultimately disappear, he says, but so long as caste produces inequality and inherited disadvantage, reservation for backward and oppressed communities remains necessary. He cites the contemporary agitation in Gujarat and notes Indira Gandhi's assurance that reservation would continue as long as disadvantaged communities needed it.

He then contrasts demands for “merit and ability” in education and public employment with restrictions on temple priesthood. If qualification and competence are truly the principle, he argues, then any trained person, regardless of caste, should be able to perform temple worship. He recalls the DMK government's legislation intended to allow qualified people of all castes to become priests.

His conclusion is that reservation becomes unnecessary only in a genuinely casteless society. Until then, governments have a duty to compensate for entrenched inequality. Students, as the coming generation, must build a society without caste and religious division and without severe economic inequality.

## Separate source closing note

Speech delivered at the Government Arts College, Tiruvarur, on 4 April 1981.
