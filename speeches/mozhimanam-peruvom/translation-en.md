## Let Us Gain Linguistic Dignity

Today is the pleasant occasion on which college textbooks are being released. Coming immediately after Pongal, this function takes place on Thiruvalluvar Day. Navalar, our Education Minister who presides over the function, and others have explained its significance.

What surprises me is that in Tamil Nadu itself we must organise a ceremony to release Tamil textbooks and then appeal at that ceremony, “Please buy these Tamil books!”

We may say that it is special that this takes place on Thiruvalluvar Day. Yet even on such a day we are in a condition where books written in Tamil must be introduced and people must be asked to buy them. It was because of such a condition that, fifteen or twenty years ago, Perarignar Anna titled a major address at Annamalai University “O, Fallen Tamil Nadu!”

Because Tamil Nadu remains in such a fallen condition, we still have to ask people to buy Tamil textbooks; we still have to plead for a certain number of books to be bought each month,

## PDF 105 / printed p.104

and even say, “If not every month, at least set aside thirty rupees on Thiruvalluvar Day each year for these books.” This unhappy condition still exists. At least on this day we must ask whether it should continue and shape our practices accordingly.

Gathering in great halls, delivering stirring and eloquent speeches, seeing the audience rejoice and applaud, and ending the function there—these acts alone do not amount to service to Tamil or to Tamils.

I appeal not merely to those seated on the stage but to everyone before me, and to those who will read in the newspapers the ideas expressed here: those who have learned through Tamil must come forward to foster and protect Tamil. You must dedicate yourselves to putting this principle into practice.

The Tamil Nadu Textbook Corporation was established in 1969. In its early years it produced thirty-five books; subsequently, from 1969 to 1970, 231 books; from 1970 to 1973, 334 books;

## PDF 106 / printed p.105

and with the 166 books being released today, the total reaches six hundred. This is worthy of praise.

Under the policy that college books should be published in the languages of the respective States, Gujarat has published 104 books, Karnataka 160, Madhya Pradesh 77, and Andhra Pradesh 135. Other States have produced fewer.

We have published six hundred. That is something in which to take pride.

But if we extend the statistical comparison, another fact troubles us. Andhra Pradesh may have published only 135 books, yet in the last year alone sales reached seventy-six lakh rupees. In Tamil Nadu, by contrast, sales amounted to only about two lakh rupees. The Director mentioned this with regret. My consolation is that at least that much was sold! While we lament that people are not buying these books, we must also remember that there is a group actively saying that there is no need to buy them.

The most important point is that the failure to sell and the failure to buy are connected to a campaign telling people that they need not buy these books. We must not forget it. Does that campaign take place openly, from platforms,

## PDF 107 / printed p.106

before crowds of thousands, on the beach, on lawns, in halls or public forums? No. I need not explain how that campaign operates; you will understand it yourselves. We must overcome it. We must win.

In that victory lies the growth of Tamil and the growth of the Tamil people.

There is another distinction we must understand clearly: studying Tamil is one thing; studying through Tamil is another.

To study Tamil means to study its literature—the Sangam works, epics, poetry, Purananuru, Akananuru and other ancient texts. Studying Tamil is necessary.

To study through Tamil means to study history, geography, economics and the many other fields of knowledge in Tamil. We must recognise the difference between studying Tamil as a subject and studying other subjects through Tamil.

Studying Tamil is necessary; studying through Tamil is even more necessary. It is essential to every aspect of Tamil Nadu’s future development.

Does that mean English and other languages should be totally rejected? No. English, Hindi, Telugu or Bengali may be studied as languages where necessary.

## PDF 108 / printed p.107

But Tamil must become the language through which we study our subjects. Only then can the efforts we have undertaken succeed.

I am genuinely delighted that six hundred books have been published, as is our Education Minister. In the past we often felt pain because most textbooks were translations. One author wrote the original and another translated it. To understand which Tamil word corresponded to each expression in the source, one almost needed a separate dictionary. We regretted that so many books were appearing only in that translated form.

Our desire has been that most books—if possible, nearly all—should be written originally in Tamil by their authors. That desire is now being fulfilled to an encouraging extent.

Of these six hundred books, only 150 are translations, while 450 have been written originally in Tamil. That is a matter of pride.

Yet it is not enough for us to say that six hundred books were published and lakhs of rupees spent. Hearing the concern expressed here, I asked: “Are the people and students of Tamil Nadu not buying them?”

## PDF 109 / printed p.108

I was told: “They do buy—but not these books. They buy new sets of notes instead.”

Through this function I appeal to every student studying through Tamil. In other countries people possess linguistic dignity. Because they wish to live with a sense of honour, they care about developing their own languages. With great sorrow I say that we have failed to cultivate that linguistic dignity. We must acquire it.

To encourage students in the pre-university class to buy these books, the Government even gives fifty rupees. Yet students take that fifty rupees and buy “notes.” Should this condition change or not? Student friends, and all who want public life and education to function in Tamil, must think about it.

Navalar expressed another concern. He pointed out that more than twenty-two crore rupees are spent on spreading Hindi in India. In addition, under the appearance of spending on the development of each language, one crore may be allotted to Tamil, while Hindi—spoken across several States—benefits from separate allocations through those States as well. In this way many crores are spent year after year on the growth of Hindi.

## PDF 110 / printed p.109

An attitude seems to have arisen at the Centre as though India has only one language—Hindi—and only one State needing development—Uttar Pradesh. We must examine this not merely through a political lens but through the future of India itself. Even if others dismiss our concern as narrow-mindedness or self-interest, we must view it from the standpoint of the growth of Tamil Nadu and the Tamil language.

Two days ago a welcome announcement came from the Central Government.

While there was an elected government in Puducherry, that government had requested a university for Puducherry. The request remained for a long time as though stored in a pickle jar. Two days before President’s Rule was imposed, news came that a university would be established in Puducherry and that the Centre had approved it. I too believe Puducherry needs a university; everyone will agree, and we welcome it.

But a danger may accompany it. I say that the university should come only under circumstances in which that danger is prevented, because the people of Puducherry are Tamils and I therefore have a duty to speak.

Puducherry does not possess State status. It is under the direct relationship and authority of the Central Government.

## PDF 111 / printed p.110

Even when the Puducherry Government wished to give Bharathidasan’s family a monthly assistance of 250 rupees, it had to obtain permission from the Central Government. Therefore, in a territory under such direct Central authority, the university will function through the direct involvement of the Central Education Department. Under such circumstances Hindi could certainly be introduced as the medium of instruction.

Puducherry needs a university. But it must come with a firm assurance that Hindi will not be made the medium of instruction. Otherwise we ourselves may purchase a danger and tie it at our waist: on territory surrounding Tamil Nadu, at Tamil Nadu’s edge, Hindi may be installed as a base. Countries that seek to extend control over neighbouring territory establish military bases nearby; in the same way Puducherry could become a military base for the advance of Hindi domination. That must not happen. This is our deep concern.

Puducherry should have a university, with a guarantee that Hindi will not be imposed as its medium. One way to secure that is for Puducherry to obtain State status. If Puducherry gains State status, a means will exist to resist such an imposition. Keeping all this in view, the Central Government must proceed with great care and caution in establishing the university in Puducherry.

## PDF 112 / printed p.111

The Central Government does not need me to remind it again of 1938 and 1965. Tamils have suffered enough from the domination of other languages. They are not prepared to bear further and further forms of domination. As a Tamil, I regard it as my foremost duty to state that plainly, and with that I conclude.
