## Chennai - Aazhvargal Research Centre Function Speech

Today, with the affectionate greetings of all of you, I, elder Sivaraman, Dr. Silamboli Chellappan, and Dr. (Mrs.) Radha Thiagarajan have received awards on behalf of the Aazhvargal Research Centre. Quite apart from the distinction of the awards presented here, I feel that those who spoke about the awards and about those of us who received them have praised us very highly—and especially praised and extolled me.

Our People’s Leader Moopanar said here that these awards have been given not for what you have done in the past, but for what you still have to achieve. That is the truth. The beautiful Tamil words spoken here, the descriptive phrases, the descriptions Jagathrakshakan poured forth in abundance, and brother Valampuri John, with his distinctive command of Tamil, raining words in this hall—

## PDF 296 / printed p.295

all of them showered such a rain of affection that I wondered whether I might get soaked and submerged in it. I do not think all this was for the work I have done so far, or for the energy, ability, scholarship, or labour they found in me. Whether it was brother R.M.V. speaking, the others speaking in praise, or Moopanar himself speaking, I understand all of it to be nothing other than pats on the back intended to put me to work still more for this society.

To that extent I have been praised here. If I were to say more, I stand before you in something of a daze, not knowing what to say or how to begin speaking. To that extent I now appear before you like a fly fallen into a pot of honey—not a fly that fell in, but one that was lifted up and thrown into it.

When brother R.M.V. spoke about the Ramanujar Award given to me, he said: “We did not print in the invitation that we are presenting this to Kalaignar. We stated that he has agreed to receive it.” That is not something I can accept—not the award, but the words Veerappan used are what I cannot accept. Because once, the man described as “Kambanadippodi,”

## PDF 297 / printed p.296

Karaikudi Sa. Ganesan, invited me to the annual Kamban festival held in Karaikudi. I attended that function. Our Sivaraman, Silambuchelvar M. Po. Si., and many other eminent Tamil scholars were also present.

When I spoke there, I said: “No one should be surprised that Karunanidhi is taking part in a Kamban festival. Instead, be surprised in a different way. It is right for you to be surprised that they have invited Karunanidhi to a Kamban festival.”

Likewise, rather than saying that I agreed to receive the Ramanujar Award, I think you have created a situation in which others can look at you in astonishment and ask, “Did you give the Ramanujar Award to Karunanidhi?”

When it was announced that this award would be presented to me, I could tell from R.M.V.’s speech that he had not remained idle: he had carefully observed all the talk going on around us about this as well. You too would have understood that.

The movement to which I belong—the movement to which we belong—the movement in which we grew up holding the hand of Periyar—

## PDF 298 / printed p.297

and following the path of Anna—if anyone were to wonder whether this is consistent with that movement—they will not ask openly, because they know that if they ask, they will receive the proper answer—but if they do harbour such a doubt, I have only one explanation for them. When Ramanujar is one who, a thousand years ago, put into practice the very actions we wish to carry out in this country today and the very advances we wish to see blossom, I take pride in receiving this award. I would have been disappointed only if R.M.V. or the Aazhvargal Centre had decided that it should not be given to me. I take pride in their coming forward to present it. For I need not carry the doubt that I have been kept away from the Aazhvargal: there is pride for me in knowing that the Aazhvargal too value me, the Aazhvargal too like me, and the Aazhvargal too accept me.

Once, at the Karpagambal Marriage Hall in Mylapore, a conference of the Divine Forum was held jointly by the heads of the monasteries in Tamil Nadu. At the time I was serving as Chief Minister for the second time. One Aadheenam head who spoke there said, “Whether Kalaignar accepts God or not, still I say this—” and continued expressing those views.

## PDF 299 / printed p.298

When I spoke after him, I referred to the phrase used by that Aadheenam head who had spoken before me: “whether Kalaignar accepts God or not.” I told him: “The issue for me is not whether I accept God. The issue for me is whether I conduct myself in such a way that God accepts me.” The position is the same even now. R.M.V. illustrated that beautifully here as well.

As a crowning statement to all this, when Perarignar Anna founded this political movement, in pouring rain at Robinson Park in Chennai on 17 September 1946, he said: “One clan, one God.” He stated that this was our principle regarding God and our social principle.

When we, who follow that principle, find that one aspect among the many dimensions of Ramanujar is consistent with our own principle, why should we ignore it? Why should we hate and reject it? Why should we fail to make use of it? Therefore, when the principle proclaimed by people such as Ramanujar a thousand years ago has still not been fulfilled—

## PDF 300 / printed p.299

whether or not there is distinction in this award being given in his name, or whether or not there is distinction for me in receiving it, if the benefit and consequence of this award help bring about, many years from now, a situation in which Tamil Nadu becomes a land of equality and India becomes soil of equality, I am duty-bound to point out here that this award will have greater distinction on that future day than it has today.

Not only Ramanujar: the region where the first three of the twelve Aazhvargal appeared was the Chennai–Chengalpattu region itself. In one of those places, Sriperumbudur, Ramanujar was born in A.D. 1017. Everyone who spoke here said generally, “a thousand years ago.” Before coming to functions like this, I do not come without taking notes. (Applause) I do not come thinking, “I am the Chief Minister; whatever I say they will listen and leave, so I can say something and go.” From this morning I have read five books—books about Ramanujar—before coming here. When our Justice Reddiar himself referred to some of those points, I even wondered, “Ah! He is speaking about all the notes I brought,” though I was not upset. I mentioned even that to R.M.V. He replied, “We invited him to this function precisely so that he could explain all these matters.”

## PDF 301 / printed p.300

So, instead of saying approximately “a thousand years ago,” if one is to say it exactly, Ramanujar was born at Sriperumbudur 981 years ago, in A.D. 1017. The teacher whom he accepted as his guru was Yadava Prakasa, an Advaita teacher. But there were frequent disputes between guru and student. Ramanujar was a disciple who surpassed his guru. Present-day disciples should not ask, “If even Ramanujar was like that, what is wrong if we are?” (Laughter) By “a disciple who surpassed the guru,” I mean one who could be admired and praised by the guru himself, and who, even in his student days, was so capable that he could explain to the guru ideas and meanings the guru himself had not considered. Because of this, it came to be widely spoken of that disputes often arose between guru and disciple.

You know this. People today may not know it. Our People’s Leader Moopanar knows it well—he is from Thanjavur. R.M.V. also knows it well; he has long been in the world of arts. In earlier times, “guru and disciple” did not mean a teacher opening a school, sitting on a chair, seating all the students in front of him and conducting lessons. Especially whether it was the learning of the Vedas and mantras, or training

## PDF 302 / printed p.301

in an art, the old custom was for the student to stay in the guru’s own house, study and train there, and develop his skill. Accordingly, while Ramanujar was a disciple of this guru Yadava Prakasa, one day, as was customary, he had seated his guru and was rubbing oil on him. In those days, all this was a duty students had to perform for their guru.

While Ramanujar was applying oil to his guru, a dispute arose between the two. Even today, researchers who study this episode criticize the fact that the guru should not have spoken about such a major subject at such a moment. Suddenly the guru cited the phrase “Kapyasam—Pundari Kaatcham” and gave an explanation for it. It was a somewhat obscene explanation. While Ramanujar was rubbing oil on him, the guru looked at him and asked, “Disciple, do you know? Do you know what ‘Kapyasam, Pundari Kaatcham’ means?” Ramanujar said, “Tell me, Guru.”

The guru explained: “Kapyasam means kapi-asam. Kapi means monkey. Asam means the monkey’s backside.” He did not stop there. He said that when the Lord became angry, His eyes reddened, and explained “Kapyasam” as meaning that they looked like a monkey’s backside. Ramanujar could not bear it.

## PDF 303 / printed p.302

When speaking about the eyes of God, the eyes of the Lord whom he worshipped, and about how they had reddened in anger, Ramanujar thought: “How can the guru interpret them as looking like a monkey’s backside?” Tears began to stream down. When those tears fell on the guru’s body, the guru looked up and asked, “My boy, why are you crying?” Ramanujar expressed his anguish: “I am crying because of the explanation you gave.”

The guru asked angrily, “Then you tell me the explanation. What other explanation is there?” Ramanujar replied: “Sir, *kapi* means the sun; *asam* means the lotus flower. Just as a lotus that sees the sun blossoms red, the proper explanation is that the Lord’s eyes were like that.” The guru did not thank him for this. Jealousy arose. It is natural for some gurus to become jealous in this way. If a student conducts himself in a manner that goes beyond the guru, some gurus become jealous. Once that jealousy arose, would those around them remain quiet? The other students thought this was the right time to get rid of this student. Sitting with the guru, they said, “It would be good if he were removed. Otherwise even our religious duties may be in danger.” The guru too was of that mind, but he thought about the method. If they used open violence, it would become known outside. So they considered a less obvious way of getting rid of him. Finally, the guru himself said that he would take all the students

## PDF 304 / printed p.303

and say that he was going to Kashi. He would invite Ramanujar too; Ramanujar would come. There, in Kashi, at a suitable time while they were bathing in the Ganges, they could push him into the Ganges and later say that, while everyone was bathing, the current had carried him away. The guru himself proposed this plan and invited Ramanujar. Ramanujar agreed and went along, unaware of the conspiracy.

His younger mother’s son Govindan—who was also training under that guru—went with him. Halfway there, Govindan understood their plot. He took Ramanujar aside and said, “This is what they are planning to do. So you escape and run away. I alone will go back and manage by saying that you have gone somewhere.” He enabled Ramanujar to escape. Govindan alone returned to the guru and said, “He has gone somewhere.” The guru was delighted. “Where can he go in this forest? Wherever he goes, animals such as tigers and lions will kill him. Never mind,” he thought happily. He and the others went on to Kashi and returned only after many days.

On their return, when he saw Ramanujar coming toward them surrounded by disciples, while a large crowd praised and acclaimed him, the guru was stunned. This is

## PDF 305 / printed p.304

an event of great importance in the life history of Ramanujar.

The reason I mention this is that merely because he was born as Ramanujar, he did not have a life free of difficulty. Life was a problem for him too. His life too was a struggle, to the point that even his own guru thought he must somehow be brought down. He survived by escaping from that.

As Justice Reddiar mentioned, although Ramanujar had first accepted the guru I referred to as his teacher, after this the person he accepted as the teacher who would advise him was Thirukkachi Nambi. That Thirukkachi Nambi belonged to an oppressed community. Ramanujar was born into a high caste—or was regarded as having been born into a high caste. The person he accepted as the teacher who would advise him was Thirukkachi Nambi, who belonged to an oppressed community. After this event, the path he showed became the path Ramanujar followed. It is said that many of Ramanujar’s disciples belonged to communities then treated as untouchable. One of them was Uranga Vallidasar. When Ramanujar went to the bank of the Kaveri to bathe, he would go with his hand resting on the shoulder of a Brahmin; after bathing, on the way back, he would place his hand on the shoulder of this Uranga Vallidasar, who belonged to an oppressed community,

## PDF 306 / printed p.305

and walk back. The Brahmins who saw him asked, “What injustice is this? Can you behave like this?” Ramanujar replied with the principle that caste is not a barrier to following the path of devotion.

Not only that: there are three forms of pride—pride of learning, pride of wealth, and pride of caste. “You who ask me whether I may walk with my hand on his shoulder appear to me to be people without these three forms of pride. Even so, by asking this question you show that you despise a man in whom caste pride cannot exist. Society needs a condition in which these forms of pride have disappeared. Only if such a condition comes about can society progress and live.” That he explained this to them is a revolutionary page in his history.

Caste is not a barrier to taking part in the path of devotion. Ramanujar proclaimed that people of every caste—and why, even women—could reach God through the path of devotion. He removed the restriction that a guru might initiate only one student into a mantra and said that one could initiate any number of disciples; instead of judging a disciple’s qualification, one should look only at his eagerness and initiate him. He put this into practice too. Thus it becomes clear that *mukti*, liberation, the fruit of devotion, is not the property of a few: Ramanujar made it common property for all.

## PDF 307 / printed p.306

The Aazhvargal who lived before him had spoken of this. Nathamuni had thought on this basis. Uyyakondar, Alavandar, and others too had conducted themselves in this way to the extent possible to them. But the distinction of having carried this out as religious common ownership—as a movement itself—belongs to Ramanujar alone. That is why I read a weekly magazine today. In it, the fine writer Indira Parthasarathy has written an article about Ramanujar. He describes Ramanujar there as “the Periyar of that age.” If I said it, or if R.M.V. said it, it might not seem so fitting. In that long article in *Kanaiyazhi*, Indira Parthasarathy describes Ramanujar as “the Periyar of that age.” I mention this here precisely to point out that Indira Parthasarathy too has described him in that way.

To that extent, a thousand years ago itself we had a Periyar. But if we had continuously followed the social reform, equal justice, social justice, and equality that that Periyar preached, this Periyar would not have been needed today. There would have been no need to establish Samathuvapurams. For if everyone were equal, why would there be a need for a Samathuvapuram? For these thousand years, we did not accept and live by the ideas stated by people such as Ramanujar. We did not continuously

## PDF 308 / printed p.307

follow that path. That is why we have arrived at this situation, and I wish to say that here.

In Tamil Nadu he faced great opposition from some Tamil kings, and especially from Chola kings. That is the event that causes me pain. Moopanar and I are both from Chola country. But in those days a Chola king—his name was Kirumikanda Cholan—opposed Ramanujar. Unwilling to live under that king’s rule, Ramanujar went and lived in the Mysore region for twelve years. It was during those twelve years that, as mentioned here, at Melakottai Tirunarayanapuram he brought oppressed people with him and initiated the remarkable revolutionary act of temple entry.

Ramanujar was not only a man of stubborn determination in everything; the way he dealt with Thirukkottiyur Nambi is an example showing that he thought things through decisively and did not worry about the consequences, possessing the same firmness of character as Periyar.

He mounted seventeen or eighteen campaigns to learn the mantra from him. Only on the eighteenth occasion did he succeed. Then Thirukkottiyur Nambi whispered the mantra into his ear and said: “You must not reveal this to another person until the end. If at all you are to reveal it, only at the end of your life may you tell it into the ear of a single person. If you act against this, no good will come to you; only harm will. Give me your oath.”

## PDF 309 / printed p.308

Holding Thirukkottiyur Nambi’s feet, Ramanujar swore, “I will not tell anyone.” Then he came to Thirukkottiyur and climbed the temple tower. “Come, come,” he called to all the people of the town. Everyone gathered. Looking at them, he taught, in a way everyone could understand, the secret mantra that Thirukkottiyur Nambi had given him.

When he heard this news, Thirukkottiyur Nambi was shocked and enraged. He had Ramanujar brought before him and asked, “How could you swear an oath and then break it?” Ramanujar said: “If harm comes to the one who tells it and benefit comes to those who hear it, there are many hearers and I am only one speaker. If this one person suffers harm, never mind; many people will receive benefit. That is why I told it openly for many people to know.” On hearing this, that guru—unlike the earlier guru—embraced him and said, “You are the one who has shown me the way, my child.” This is a portion of his history worthy of being engraved in letters of gold.

It is with the principles of such a Ramanujar imprinted in our minds that today we are striving to create Samathuvapurams. I have spoken at length about Ramanujar after receiving the Ramanujar Award from the hands of elder Chengalvarayan, commander of the freedom struggle.

## PDF 310 / printed p.309

Here, our Sivaraman has received an award in the name of Anna. We all know how forcefully and emphatically Sivaraman conveyed words to the country through the printed page. Whenever a major issue gave rise to doubt, if we bought *Dinamani* to see what Sivaraman had written about it and understood the issue through him—not just today, but even in those days when all of us were students—then Sivaraman’s distinction becomes easy to understand. He declined to speak here. The reason is his advanced age. His hearing has become weak to the point that he cannot hear what all of us are saying. He declined to speak only because he felt he could speak only if he knew what we had said, and for no other reason.

A book titled *Anna’s Story* was published on behalf of *Dinamani Kathir*. It was published during the period when our friend Saavi worked at *Dinamani Kathir*. The *Dinamani* office asked that I should be the one to release the book, and I went to release it. The speech Sivaraman delivered then, and the ideas he impressed upon our hearts in that address—

## PDF 311 / printed p.310

remain ideas that still move like shadows through my memory. He is an elder with such depth of experience. He should live for many more years. I often ask those close to him how he is doing. His coming to this function and receiving the award given in Anna’s name are matters that give us pride.

Likewise, our respected Mrs. Radha Thiagarajan has received the award given in the name of my dear friend M.G.R. I know as a fact how learned she is in Tamil, how eager she is to study all the works of literature, and how she has not stopped with that eagerness but has now studied them all fully. Therefore, giving her the award in the name of my friend M.G.R. is highly appropriate.

The award in my name has been given to our Dr. Silamboli Chellappan. I do not know whether the award gains distinction from this or not. But Silamboli Chellappan, who has received it, is not merely a scholar with a doctorate; in his desire that Tamil Nadu should progress and that Tamil should grow and gain strength, he possesses learning enough to compete with anyone.

## PDF 312 / printed p.311

I have known such a Silamboli Chellappan for many years. He is one of those living for Tamil Nadu. Therefore, at this award function I am duty-bound to congratulate him too.

On behalf of all of you, I convey my heartfelt thanks to R.M.V. and to secretary Jagathrakshakan, who conduct this award-giving function every year on behalf of the Aazhvargal Centre.

They draped me with a golden shawl. They presented an award shield bearing the image of Ramanujar. Not stopping there, to mark my age of seventy-five, they also gave me seventy-five thousand rupees as a prize. Only then I thought: “It is only seventy-five; could it not have become one hundred?” (Laughter)

You know that generally I do not personally keep prize money given with awards of this kind. Among the awards given to me in many places, when our former Vice-President of India himself came to Thanjavur and presented the Rajaraja Cholan Award, I gave the one lakh rupees that came with it to that university and asked them to establish a trust in the names of my father and mother

## PDF 313 / printed p.312

and conduct many lectures under its auspices.

Jagathrakshakan should not think that I will simply give this away in the same manner here too. (Laughter) Nor should R.M.V. expect that. Moopanar and I have discussed this and arrived at a decision. Ramanujar worked for equality and put it into practice. Therefore, I am adding this seventy-five thousand rupees, given with an award in his name, to the government fund for the Samathuvapurams that I have launched on behalf of this government. (Loud applause) This seventy-five thousand rupees alone will not be enough for that. The government has funds and can spend them. But you know that more than half of the available funds go merely toward paying salaries. Therefore, to show that you too are interested in Samathuvapurams—and by “you” I do not mean only those present in this hall, but all the people of Tamil Nadu—and to show and prove that everyone has an interest in the ideal of equality found in the ideas of Periyar, Anna, the Revolutionary Poet, and Ramanujar, I request that from today onward each of you continue to contribute whatever funds you can.

Among those making such contributions, our Moopanar has today given one lakh rupees as the first contribution. (Loud applause) He has not yet

## PDF 314 / printed p.313

given it. Yet the reason I say that he has given it is that it is as good as given. He is a man worthy of that much trust, and that is why I say it has been given. Following him, I ask everyone to contribute their assistance to the Samathuvapuram scheme. I convey my heartfelt thanks to this Centre because this fine function, conducted on behalf of the Aazhvargal Centre under the leadership of R.M.V. and Jagathrakshakan, has served to open the way for that. With that, I conclude my speech.
