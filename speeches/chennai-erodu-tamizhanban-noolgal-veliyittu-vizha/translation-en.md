## Chennai - Erode Tamizhanban Books Release Function

I am greatly delighted to have the good opportunity to take part in this pleasant function at which two rare books by the poet Erode Tamizhanban are being released, and to release the books.

There is no need to introduce Tamizhanban to Tamil Nadu. For many years now, Tamil soil has been made aware of who Tamizhanban is. His poems have been travelling through Tamil Nadu. His fame and distinction continue to grow day by day. In such a setting, following his many earlier books, these books have been released today. The Professor who spoke here and other Tamil scholars have also spoken here about the strengths of the poems he has written.

Among the poems he has composed, I find in these books a quality that makes them all taste like honey and sugar candy, to such an extent that it is impossible to divide them into those that are excellent and those that are not

## PDF 373 / printed p.372

excellent.

Under the title “On What Date?” he asks:

“One caste to labour!
One caste to suck others dry and live!
On what date
will this
decrepit justice
be torn apart?”

That is what he asks.

Though those who spoke, praised and criticised have explained the meaning they found in each poem and set out the views they themselves held, what I was eager to see in Erode Tamizhanban was this: what does he say about today's society? What advice does he give this society through these books? These lines, which caught my eye immediately, moved me deeply. They are words that must be engraved in the hearts of the younger generation. They are lines of poetry.

Next:

“For humaneness,
in which one human
respects another,
to flower—

## PDF 374 / printed p.373

if religions stand in the way,
we shall abolish religions!”

He has written.

At a time when religions today come forward to destroy humaneness, if they become obstacles to humaneness flowering, there is no other way — we shall abolish the religions themselves. These lines of poetry, composed with great firmness and forceful Tamil words, stand forever as a challenge to us.

He did not say this out of a feeling that religions themselves must be abolished. He has expressed this idea through poetry in order to say with unmistakable emphasis that religions must not become obstacles to humaneness. I have heard his similes, his play with words, and the sweep of his expression at many poetry gatherings. You too have heard them. In one place he says:

“Even when shut inside a box,
let us carry with us
the nature of a matchstick
that does not become a corpse!”

Even when it is inside a box, a matchstick's power is not reduced merely because it has been confined within the box. Let us carry the nature of the matchstick that does not become a corpse even when boxed in. He says further:

## PDF 375 / printed p.374

“Water that washes away dirt
itself becomes dirty!
Fire that burns away grime
never becomes grime!”

The water that washes dirt becomes dirty by mixing with that dirt. When grime is burned by fire, the fire does not become grime; but the water that washes away dirt becomes dirty. These are the poetic lines he has used to show that we must be fire in every way.

If I may give one or two examples of his similes, the sun appears in the east in the morning. The novelty lies in how he describes it:

“Midnight,
drunk on illicit liquor,
kicks the belly
of the east
already gripped by labour pains.”

The east is already ready to give birth. It is seized by labour pains. He says that midnight, drunk on illicit liquor, kicks the belly of the east that is in labour.

Then he describes the evening:

## PDF 376 / printed p.375

“The sun,
biting into the neck of the day,
drags it away,
blood dripping and dripping,
and disappears
in the west.”

I do not think there could be another poet who has described the sky so beautifully. I believe our younger brother Erode Tamizhanban has planted the flag of victory there.

When he sings of the stars:

“When going to sleep,
the sun
shook out its bed...
star-dust.”

Next, drawing on an old mythological story, he gives a simile for the stars:

“Which Gautama
cursed the night?
Why is its body
all eyes?”

You all know the story of Ahalya and Gautama. It is a story involving Indra. Indra violates Ahalya. “She too consented,” was said not by me but by Va. Ra. Gautama the sage suddenly comes upon this and curses Indra. After telling Ahalya, “Become stone,” he curses Indra that his entire body shall become eyes

## PDF 377 / printed p.376

. That is what he uses here as a simile for the stars, asking: which Gautama cursed Indra so that the whole body is eyes?

Next are lines I greatly enjoyed:

“Twilight sowed the sun—
so many flowers already?
What a potent seed!”

Silamboli Sellappan, speaking here, took up a fine poem among these lines. They are lines written by our younger brother, the poet Erode Tamizhanban, about the Revolutionary Poet Bharathidasan. I would like to repeat them and add a few explanations.

“Bharathidasa!
When Kanakasabai wrote you,
you were
*Subramaniyar Thuthi Amudhu*!”

Bharathidasan's father's name was Kanakasabai. When Kanakasabai “wrote” him — when Kanakasabai wrote him in his mother's womb — and he stood as a son to his father, during his school days or youth he sang a work called *Subramaniyar Thuthi Amudhu*. Many of you may never have seen that book. What the Revolutionary Poet Bharathidasan wrote about Murugan in those days, when he was the devotee Bharathidasan, could hardly have been written in that fashion by any other devotee.

## PDF 378 / printed p.377

He sang *Subramaniyar Thuthi Amudhu* with such excellence.

“When the man of Ettayapuram wrote you,
you were
*Khadi Irattina Paattu*.”

A book by Bharathidasan was published under the title *Khadi Irattina Paattu*. About khadi and its excellence and greatness, even the poets of today's Congress Party could not write as Bharathidasan did in verse. Even Gandhiji, had he read it, would have been overwhelmed with wonder: “What a poet!”

“When Erode Periyar
wrote you,
you became
the Revolutionary Poet.”

That is what he says. When you think of this *Subramaniyar Thuthi Amudhu*, the khadi song, and later the Self-Respect songs, Dravidian movement songs and songs about Tamil society that he sang, one thing must be kept in mind.

A hundred years from now, or fifty years from now, some mischief-maker may say he is publishing the poems of Revolutionary Poet Bharathidasan, place the Self-Respect songs, the Dravida Nadu songs and the Tamil movement songs he wrote in the front portion of the book, and then even fabricate a story that Bharathidasan later reformed himself, became a believer,

## PDF 379 / printed p.378

and only then wrote *Subramaniyar Thuthi Amudhu*. I have a doubt: might something like this have happened in Bharathi's era too? It could have happened in Subramania Bharathi's era. For however revolutionary his songs were, even if some were suppressed, some poems that were published without being suppressed continue even today to circulate among the people.

“The age when the Brahmin
was called ‘Aiyar’ is gone!
The age when the white foreigner
was called ‘Durai’ is gone!”

Bharathi did not say this in sorrow. He said it because such a time had changed.

“O ignorant ones who wander
in search, saying
there are a thousand gods...”

says Bharathi. If all these were not placed properly in sequence but shuffled around, and if Bharathi was presented to the country entirely as a devotee and worshipper of the Goddess — without showing that he was a revolutionary in society and that he hated caste and religion at their roots — then the meaning is simply that his songs were not arranged in proper sequence.

## PDF 380 / printed p.379

That is why I say this. Even these songs may in the future be deliberately placed out of sequence by some people in an attempt to cast a stain upon Bharathidasan. For the works he wrote, *Subramaniyar Thuthi Amudhu* and the khadi song, are such fine songs; such collected volumes have indeed appeared.

When singing about Bharathidasan, our younger brother Tamizhanban speaks of the sweetness of that song:

“Having submitted its resignation,
the song left the flute
and met you!
At that meeting,
the song
grew a moustache.”

Having submitted its resignation, the song that left the flute met you. A song comes out of the flute. Music comes out. When air is blown into the flute, music enters it, they say. When it comes out, what is it like? It comes out after submitting a resignation and releasing the music. The song that left the flute after submitting its resignation met you. At that meeting the song grew a moustache. That song, they say, became a heroic song in the first part.

## PDF 381 / printed p.380

“On hearing
just one or two
of your sentences,
volcanoes came of age!”

Sellappan also said here:

“The four annas
that were the wages
of the comrades who planted seedlings—
your fire of anger melted them
and forged a sledgehammer.”

That is how Tamizhanban writes. Bharathidasan had sung it this way:

“For comrades who planted seedlings
to receive four annas as wages,
while the wealthy who do no bodily labour
rule and roam the world—
against the vile crowd
that declared this God's command,
let us sever the bond called God
and rouse the workers.”

That is what Tamizhanban refers to in this way. It is in the poem he has written under the title “What is the path to seek the human?” that I have a slight difference of opinion. I think younger brother Tamizhanban will not be hurt if I mention differences at this function of appreciation. I say this because I am an elder brother who has that right.

## PDF 382 / printed p.381

“Rama
joined hands
with the Ravanas.
In the flood of the Sarayu,
the corpses of dharma.”

That is what he writes.

The poem creates the impression that our younger brother Erode Tamizhanban's view is that if one joins the Ravanas, dharma becomes a corpse. I know that, in truth, this is not his view. Even so, the poem creates the impression that it might be his view.

I know which alliance he is referring to. I know which Rama he means. He intends to refer to the Babri Masjid. I have one concern. I do not know whether our poet Vairamuthu has published something like this; I have not seen many of his poems. Whether it is our Kavikko Abdul Rahman, Tamizhanban, or some other poets, whenever they refer to Ravana they have made it a habit to use him as a symbol of some bad quality. I cannot accept that. (Loud applause) For the Bharathidasan whom younger brother Tamizhanban has praised so greatly

## PDF 383 / printed p.382

— do you know what he said about Ravana? Even before Bharathidasan, the person who gave Bharathidasan and us the first step in this direction was Pandit Jawaharlal Nehru. In his *World History*, he said that the Ramayana was an Aryan-Dravidian struggle. Therefore there can be no doubt that Ravana was a Dravidian, a Dravidian king. Pandit Nehru himself gives testimony to this. On that basis we argue that Ravana was a Dravidian king. We keep telling that truth to the town and to the world.

To prove, “I am not a merciless demon,” Perarignar Anna wrote the play *Neethi Devan Mayakkam*, which could run for three hours and was built around the single question, “Am I a merciless demon?” He himself acted as Ravana, and *Neethi Devan Mayakkam* was staged throughout Tamil Nadu. In that play, the person who would repeatedly play Neethi Devan and faint and fall was our Professor Anbazhagan — I am duty-bound to remind you of that. (Loud applause) For, unable to answer Ravana's questions, all the great sages would be confounded. “You cursed Ahalya — did you have compassion then?” Ravana would ask one such question after another, tormenting all the sages, and finally he would win. “I am not a merciless demon; do you know who the merciless ones are?” he would ask, and Ravana would plant the flag of victory.

## PDF 384 / printed p.383

About that Ravana, Bharathidasan — whom younger brother Erode Tamizhanban has praised — says:

“I look toward the south;
what shall I say? My
whole mind, my shoulders, swell with pride!
The valiant Tamil who once ruled that Lanka,
who spread his fame in all ten directions!
With mighty shoulders that could lift mountains,
with a hand that gave generously!
Death to the pack that acts like cunning jackals!
Ancestor of my Tamils; lord of my Tamils—
behold Ravana! His name
the whole world knows!”

Following that Ravana sung by Revolutionary Poet Bharathidasan, I wrote in my poem *Thennavan Kaathai*:

“Like a lamp lit
in the southern land left in darkness,
the king of Lanka rose and stood.
If I were to tell his history...
every muscle
would dance! The lord of southern Lanka,
who won fame in all eight directions; ancestor of the Dravidians.
Our forefather who held fast to ancient Tamil;
the island of Lanka like the pupil within the eye,
the deep seas its dark iris! The great warrior
who brought fame to Tamil soil;
a lion in form — gold at heart — one who lived
without peer in the modes of the yazh,
a sacred man of Yalpanam! a mountain-Tamil!

## PDF 385 / printed p.384

There is an eyelid to protect the beautiful eye.
Just so was Ravana to Lanka!”

Erode Tamizhanban would have read this too. He would also have read the earlier poem on Ravana by the Revolutionary Poet. At least from now on, do not hurt Ravana. (Loud applause) Do not hurt him and thereby hurt me. If younger brother Vairamuthu has not written so until now, thanks to him! I ask him not to write so in future. I request those who have written so at least not to do so hereafter.

For, according to the evidence given by Nehru, and according to evidence given by great scholars such as Srinivasa Iyengar who wrote Indian history, Ravana was a Tamil king, a Dravidian. Do not depict him as a tyrant. There may have been mistakes in his life. For those mistakes do not portray him as a wrongdoer or unjust man. Since I have the right, in the midst of this function where all of us are praising Tamizhanban and showering him with honey and sugar candy, to offer a gentle, affectionate rebuke, I ask him to avoid going so far as to say that merely joining Ravana makes the corpses of dharma float.

In another place, as our Tamilkudimagan beautifully pointed out here, he brought together the Revolutionary Poet Bharathidasan's song about the Tamil movement and these lines used by our Erode Tamizhanban. With great anguish, as though driving a spear into our heart and mind, there is a poem in just four lines:

## PDF 386 / printed p.385

“With Tamil,
everything is possible;
but
with the Tamilian alone, nothing is possible!
The one mistake
Tamil made—
was that it became
the mother tongue of the Tamilian!”

(Loud applause)

I think Tamizhanban must have written this with tears mixed into it. For even as I read it, tears well in my eyes. I kept thinking and thinking about that single mistake Tamil made — becoming the Tamilian's mother tongue — and I marvelled, grieved and suffered.

One more thing. Though they are stories that move through the varied situations, customs and practices of village life, he has observed them closely and turned them into poems in this book. I want to give just one example.

We have heard a story in villages. There will be a village headman. That headman will be respected by everyone in the village;

## PDF 387 / printed p.386

at the same time, everyone will fear him. Whatever function takes place in anyone's house, he will not go. They will wait, expecting him to come and come. Eventually it becomes customary to assume he will not come. If there is a wedding in a house, he will send his walking stick. They will take the walking stick and place it there. The stick will be there in his place. Assuming he has come, the wedding proceedings will take place. Even if a bereavement occurs in a house, they will place that walking stick there. Assuming he has come, they will take the dead person's body away for the funeral rites. Whatever the function, this was the custom he followed. Finally, he himself died. When he died, the people of the house expected everyone in the village to come. No one came. A hundred walking sticks came to the house. (Loud applause)

Erode Tamizhanban has copied this matter and written a poem. He too is not a poet who never copies; he is indeed a poet who copies — and this poem is an example. He says:

“That day I had
important work, so I could not come
to your ‘wedding’—
the lie I wrote
bubbled in the nerves
of my writing,

## PDF 388 / printed p.387

surged and beat
in waves
through
my blood vessels.”

(He did not go to a wedding. “Important work; I couldn't come,” he says. The moment he writes it, his nerves tell him it is a lie. He writes again.)

“My health
is really not good;
otherwise,
would I fail to come
to your daughter's coming-of-age ceremony?…”

I dropped the letter
in the box and came home.
It
came back and fell
into my mind—with a different sentence:

“Don't lie; go.”

“I have admitted
my mother-in-law to hospital.
I cannot come
to your house-warming…”

Before my very eyes,
tablets climbed over
the letters
and trampled them.
The corpse of the letter
floated in the smell of medicine.

## PDF 389 / printed p.388

“That day I could not come
to your
mother's death…?
There was a major problem
at the office…”

The pen lunged forward
and scratched
the chest of truth.

Last week I died
of a heart attack—
in a dream!

Letters to my son,
in great numbers.

Every letter
that began
“I could not come that day…”
in my own handwriting…

I wake with a start!
Even then
I am writing:

“I could not come that day…”

Though this is a poem copied from that story, it has become a very beautiful poetic tale woven around a small story that all of us can enjoy.

Our dear friend Sirajul Millat Abdul Samad, who spoke here, said he had not read the entire poem and had only turned over one or two pages. The Professor has read all of it. The others too spoke only after reading it fully.

## PDF 390 / printed p.389

As Vairamuthu was speaking, I could tell how much he had read. He has read more than half. I will tell Tamizhanban this: I have read it in full. If you want proof, I will give you one example.

“That day I had
important
work,
so I could not come
to your ‘திருமணத்திற்கு’”

There is a printing error here. Instead of ‘உன் திருமணத்திற்கு’, it has been printed ‘உன் திருணமத்திற்கு’. From this, younger brother Tamizhanban should understand that I have read this book in full. With that, I take leave.
