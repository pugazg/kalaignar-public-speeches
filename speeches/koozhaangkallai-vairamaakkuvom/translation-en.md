## Let Us Turn Pebbles into Diamonds

Chancellor, Pro-Chancellors, Vice-Chancellors, respected professors, students!

I greet you in the spirit of Vallalar's pure path, and in ancient Thillai, where he lived and flourished, I address my dear brothers and sisters gathered at Annamalai University—an institution of learning that stands as delight to the learned and unlearned alike, an eye to those who see and those who do not, and a balanced centre to all.

## PDF 135 / printed p.134

I greet you through Tamil, which is sweeter than a carefully blended nectar.

Just as Alfred Nobel and Rockefeller strengthened learning through generous endowment, Raja Annamalai opened his hands to education. This University stands as a beautiful monument to that service. Its teachers and students, who keep his banner of fame flying high, deserve the praise of the Tamil world.

Today you walk proudly into the expanding world of knowledge and receive your degrees. I came to offer you congratulations, yet you have arranged a conferral for me too. In Sangam-age Tamil tradition, if a warrior died without a battle wound, a ceremonial wound was made before burial so that he might be counted among those who bore honourable scars. In something of that spirit, by giving me a degree today you make me a graduate too.

## PDF 136 / printed p.135

I accept this distinction as encouragement to work with still greater energy and firmness, and as an honour paid to Tamil feeling, love of art and cultivated rationalism. I dedicate it to my Tamil people and to the leader who shaped me.

At a convocation here, Arignar Anna said that it comforted him to know his own limits: he was not going to offer entirely new theories of his own invention, but to recall the wisdom of many scholars and view expert educational thought from the standpoint of the ordinary person. “This is the age of the common people,” he said; “I am their representative.”

I feel those words fit me too. The “Doctor” honour you confer should make me more humble.

Education in our country has made striking progress. It was once almost the private privilege of a few prosperous and socially dominant groups.

## PDF 137 / printed p.136

That age is changing. Ordinary people at the bottom of society, including those whose families for generations were denied entry into centres of learning, now gain the right and opportunity to study and shine.

Ability and merit are not monopolies of any one section. Given opportunity, facilities and encouragement, capable students can be found at every level of society.

Old doctrines have lost much of their authority, while new goals have not yet been fully established. We must turn social tests into achievements and create ways of life suited to our time. Students must lead the younger generation in combining innovation, revolutionary thought and concern for the public good.

A university should mirror national life, reflect social development and prepare a new generation for a changing world.

Youth is a springtime, but every moment age is slowly claiming it.

## PDF 138 / printed p.137

If youth is to remain immortal, it must move from the body into the mind. We must become mature in age and knowledge while remaining forever young in the struggle for truth.

Book learning is manure for a growing tree, but manure alone is not the tree. A true tree has branches, shoots, leaves, fruit and shade for the weary. Education must produce such living benefit.

School and university learning are of little value to the country if they do not serve others. Textbook knowledge alone is insufficient; independent intelligence must also be cultivated.

At this point the source reproduces in English A. L. Mudaliar's observation that examination success is not a reliable measure of future originality, that merely reproducing prescribed learning can turn a student into a “Text-Book Gramophone,” and that scientific spirit requires independent inquiry.

## PDF 139 / printed p.138

Education must turn pebbles into diamonds, not merely feed a system that leaves graduates asking, “Of what use is a diamond in a poor man's house?”

A degree should not reduce education to a hunt for salary. Employment is not the sole ideal of education, but unless employment opportunities expand, poverty will continue to dominate society.

Universities therefore need effective employment services, linked with public and private industry. Employers should use these links when selecting qualified graduates.

In other countries private employers approach colleges, identify strong students and offer employment even before graduation. That situation may take time to mature here, but a beginning must be made immediately.

## PDF 140 / printed p.139

University employment bureaux should include advisory groups of private industrialists. Universities in the South could also form a common coordinating body. Curricula should be adjusted so students are trained for the skills industry actually needs.

Five-Year Plans are expanding employment, but government and private establishments cannot immediately absorb every educated person. Graduates should therefore consider creating their own enterprises. Nationalised banks are now prepared to finance educated people who come forward with such initiatives.

Agriculture graduates should not hesitate to practise agriculture themselves. Engineering graduates can establish small workshops in villages, benefiting both themselves and society.

## PDF 141 / printed p.140

Development programmes have placed thousands of pumps, sprayers and modern agricultural implements in villages. Many now lie idle because there are no skilled local people to repair them. Engineering graduates can address this by establishing village workshops.

More students are choosing science, which is welcome, but science students often lose contact with history and the arts, while arts students lose contact with science. This division prevents complete intellectual development.

## PDF 142 / printed p.141

I therefore suggest that science students study at least one subject such as history, arts or economics throughout college, and that arts students study at least one science such as physics, chemistry or biology.

Some may regard this as an unfamiliar curricular reform. Yet students need both breadth and specialisation. The separation of scientific and humanistic knowledge contributes to weakness in education and even to difficulties in employment.

I ask educational experts not to reject the proposal merely because it is new, but to consider changes that give students fuller training in both spheres.

In countries such as America and England, movements encourage students to participate in social service.

## PDF 143 / printed p.142

We too need programmes that draw young people into work for social improvement. Service cannot simply be forced by rule, nor achieved by speeches telling students to go to villages. Conditions must be created so they come forward voluntarily, with financial help and practical concessions.

With that in mind, the Tamil Nadu Government has introduced a scheme encouraging two thousand educated young people to serve in rural areas. I urge idealistic and energetic youth to use the opportunity.

Employment and income must not turn the younger generation into machines concerned only with their monthly accounts and private families. The youth must lead the advance of the country as a whole.

## PDF 144 / printed p.143

Young people are the pioneers of progressive thought. Youth unrest is not merely an Indian problem; it is worldwide. Older generations have complained about younger ones since the time of Plato.

Differences between generations are natural, but the transition must carry constructive growth. Student populations today are immense, and in some young minds extremism overflows.

I am not an enemy of intensity or radical feeling. A blemish on the face should be treated at its cause; tearing it out with a fingernail may cause bleeding, scars and even dangerous infection. The method matters as much as the desire to remove the blemish.

## PDF 145 / printed p.144

There is no disagreement about removing evil. The dispute is about method. Means must be as good as ends.

On the road to the University stands the statue of student Rajendran, who gave his life to protect the language that nurtured him. Yet no ideal justifies harming or destroying others.

Bharathidasan called for ending the deeds of the cruel—not for cutting off the heads of the cruel. Attend to that distinction.

I see before me a lion-like assembly of youth and draw strength from it.

As the peacock dances at dark clouds and the lotus blooms at sunlight, I rejoice at the light in your eyes. I cannot truly take leave, yet I must part. Greetings.
