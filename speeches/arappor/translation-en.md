## Speech body

### PDF page 4 — printed page 3

#### Arappor

The struggle has begun, comrades—the struggle has begun. Against those with blinkered minds who, unable to sense the force of revolution, mistake a smoking volcano for a smiling waterfall; against those friends who once cried, “Cast your votes into the yellow box, and fullness will overflow in your lives,” and who today conduct a patched-up government; against the Vande Mataram heroes who proclaimed that language and education would flourish like the full moon, but have instead set out to raze Tamil to the ground; against the arrogant rulers who, once they climbed onto the seat of power, intoxicated by authority, began hurling repression upon repression—the struggle has begun, comrades! The struggle has begun.

With hearts hard as diamond, prepared to welcome one of two things—“life or death”—we have entered the field. We have not sung the war-song in haste, enslaved by anger, or without necessity. We convened conferences and pleaded. We pointed out to the ministers Maraimalai Adigal, the great Tamilian Thiru. Vi. Ka., the valiant Tamilian Sivagnanam, and the Tamil learning of those in rival parties. Only after realizing that those who came to rule would yield to nothing did we say: War! War!! War!!! The war has broken out. Before battle, we held talks. Periyar met the Premier. We did not deviate even slightly from the ethics of war. We who have placed in the field young bulls, maidens, heroes prepared even to become graves, and our beloved Anna as a sacrifice on the battlefield—

### PDF page 5 — printed page 4

—stand here wishing to explain the state of the struggle, to make its urgency understood by members of the public who have not grasped it, and to answer the campaign of falsehood that portrays those conducting the struggle as wicked. People! Who can deny that it was the Congress ministers who performed the inaugural rite for this war to erupt? Emboldened because law and authority were in their hands, were the arrows they shot at us few? Until now, on Periyar's orders, we tried to ward off those arrows with a shield. The shield broke; the arrows reached the point of crushing our ribs—our situation became desperate. That is why we beat the drum and announced that the struggle had begun. We who have now begun to resist do not resist with spear, bow, or lance. We resist by tearing out and hurling our broken rib bones. Yes—we are waging an *arappor*, a righteous struggle.

The first arrow they shot after power came into their hands was the banning order against the play *Porvaal* [War Sword], written by Comrade C. P. Chinnarasu. What offence did they find in that play? Does *Porvaal* contain the story of Nalayini carrying her leprosy-stricken husband to the courtesan's house? Does it contain the bundle of lies called the Nallathangal legend, in which she is said to have lit a stove with a banana sheath? Does it contain the crooked conduct of Gopala Krishna, stealing the saris of women bathing in a pond, sitting in a tree, playing his flute and making merry? Does it contain the disgraceful history of Maheswaran turning Maha Vishnu into the enchantress Mohini, coupling with that male, and begetting Ayyanar?

Was *Porvaal* banned merely because Chinnarasu's sword told people to think? “Can this befit rulers?” we asked. The rulers maintained silence—

### PDF page 6 — printed page 5

—they maintained silence. A ban on the sword of war; a ban on knowledge. “Periyar, what do you say?” we asked. “Be patient, my heart, be patient,” said Periyar; we waited.

Next—the Blackshirt Corps, which did not exist, was imagined into existence, and a banning order against the Blackshirt Corps began its dance. Minister Omandurar, Arcot Ramasamy, scholar Shanmugam, friend Kamaraj, and Subbarayan, who frequently changes parties—all of them jumped into the Aryan arena. Was it not to destroy this miserable condition that we donned the black uniform? “Even that is forbidden, Minister?” we asked, hearts aching.

“There is no military organization called a Blackshirt Corps; it has not engaged in any violent activity”—when the leader of the Dravidar Kazhagam, E. V. Ramasamy, had issued a statement to this effect, why did you impose the ban?

A barrage of such questions was raised in the Legislative Assembly. One of the ministers opened his precious mouth. We expected an answer.

He graciously declared that he “did not know” the reason for the banning order. He is now a former minister. Did we have time to rejoice at that caricature? Our hearts were wounded. “Periyar! What is the way out of this?” we cried loudly toward Erode. “Be patient, my heart, be patient,” he said. Even then we waited. The war did not begin. Then—

—the seat of power demanded a security deposit of 2,000 rupees from *Viduthalai*. We demanded the reason, aflame with anger. Would a reason ever be found in this wretched realm? What wrong had *Viduthalai* done? Was it a crime to state the truth that the universally revered Gandhi was killed by a Brahmin—

### PDF page 7 — printed page 6

—was that the crime? Was it a crime to bring Periyar Ramasamy's golden words to Dravidam? Was it a crime for its sharp needle to prick and correct those who muddy politics and those who make mischief in society? Publications that pour out lust; magazines that entice youth and reduce life to waste—why do none of these catch the ministers' eyes? After putting this question rather emphatically—passionately—we turned toward Periyar to hear what he would say. From there too came the calm refrain: “Be patient, my heart, be patient.” We subsided, regained calm; even then no date was fixed for war.

Then came Ravana: the broad-shouldered one who lifted a mountain; the hand that gave in generosity; the doom of the pack that acts like cunning foxes; my Tamil ancestor! My Tamil lord! A noble heart ashamed of the great stigma that the world might call him the elder brother of the treacherous Vibhishana; a poet who stroked the strings of the fine *yaazh* and gave the ear an abundance of musical nectar; a great man who, even when death approached in fierce battle, would not desire wrongdoing—the king of southern Lanka, Ravana. On his greatness, the poet V. S. Kuzhanthai composed an epic: *Ravana Kaviyam*. Yes, a garden of novelty. The sceptre-bearers set fire to that garden. They banned *Ravana Kaviyam*. “Do not sell it—do not buy it,” decreed the government's law. Could we refrain from asking why *Ravana Kaviyam* should be confiscated? Could our hearts refrain from turning into fire when we thought of it? We brought the young men into the garden of Kamba Ramayanam and showed them cobras writhing among its blossoming flowers. We warned them: the one bearing the sacred mark is not Rama—it is a good snake that has come to bite our people. “Listen to that rustling sound—”

### PDF page 8 — printed page 7

“—it is not a stream; it is a poisonous canal destroying our education,” we explained. Did we stop there? We showed Mithilapuri. In Mithila's palace we showed Janaka's daughter Janaki. We showed Sri Rama arriving with Viswamitra to break the bow and marry Sita. We showed the scene in which Sita's eyes and Rama's eyes became entwined. We showed Kambar's verse about Mother Earth, Sita Devi, swelling with rapture at the sight of Rama—unable to contain her delight. We read out the manner in which Kambar describes, in that verse, Sita's hidden place suddenly swelling because it could not contain its joy, causing the *mekala* ornament to snap and fall. We showed Kamban's debased mind in giving the incarnation of Mahalakshmi the crude description, “vaamamekalaiyil valarnthu alkule.” “Government that lacks the courage to ban this obscene Ramayana! Was it just, fair, or honourable for you to ban our *Ravana Kaviyam*?” we asked. With blood flashing in our eyes—and rage boiling in that blood—we roared and looked toward Erode. “Be patient, my heart... be patient,” said Periyar Ramasamy. The struggle was postponed.

After that we heard startling news that split the Dravidian heart. We saw our mother's throat being strangled. Hindi had entered—Hindi! Could even this be tolerated? “Periyar, we can bear no more. We are warriors, not cowards. Where is the order for battle?” we challenged. Even then, that troublesome old man said, “Be patient, my heart, be patient.” Our agitation gained a force we ourselves could no longer restrain. A conference was held. He conveyed the heart of Mother Tamil to the ministers. The ministers' hearts softened—
