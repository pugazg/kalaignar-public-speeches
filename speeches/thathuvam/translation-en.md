Karunanidhi opens at Pachaiyappa's College with humour about the crush of students greeting him, comparing the scene to ants swarming sugarcane and joking that he cannot tell whether he has come to a college or a sugarcane mill. The enthusiastic welcome, he says, must be matched by discipline; even energy and youthful passion need order if they are to produce useful results.

He then turns to the word “philosophy” itself. Philosophy, he argues, is not some inaccessible treasure like a mythical flower or magical object from a story. In ordinary Tamil it can be understood as `மெய்ப்பொருள் இயல்`—the inquiry into reality or truth. Difficult terminology should not make ordinary human questions appear mysterious.

This leads to a distinction between `உண்மை` and `வாய்மை`. Drawing on the Tirukkural—“பொய்மையும் வாய்மை யிடத்த புரைதீர்ந்த நன்மை பயக்கும் எனின்”—he explains that a statement may be literally false yet morally truthful if it prevents harm. He illustrates this with the story of Valluvar protecting a deer from a hunter by saying that it had not come his way.

Karunanidhi next recalls Periyar's insistence on “raw truth”: a clothed person may be called “man,” but what we see is already covered by social signs and conventions. Philosophy, in this sense, is an effort to strip away such coverings and examine reality directly.

The speech then moves into questions of life, death and the soul. Karunanidhi argues from a rationalist perspective that what people call “life leaving the body” can instead be understood as the cessation of bodily functions. He questions the consistency of simultaneously claiming that a soul is reborn in another body and that yearly rites must still be performed for that same soul in another world. He explicitly notes that Professor Muthuraman, who had given him a book on Saiva Siddhanta, may disagree, and presents such disagreement as legitimate philosophical debate.

Philosophy, he says, can be used in many directions—religious, rationalist, political or social—just as iron can be fashioned into different tools. Students should therefore gather ideas from many schools, like a bee gathering nectar from many flowers.

Karunanidhi then places philosophy in a larger account of human development: the evolution of life, the formation of families and societies, the emergence of social rules, political authority and the state. From this he turns to Western political thought and the European Renaissance, discussing the transition from a “Natural Condition” to a “Political Condition.”

He sketches the ideas of Hobbes, Locke and Rousseau. Hobbes is associated with strong monarchical authority; Locke with political authority derived from the people and limited by representative institutions; Rousseau with popular sovereignty. Modern parliamentary systems, he argues, contain elements of these historical debates.

The speech compares parliamentary democracy with dictatorship and cautions that elected rulers are not automatically good, just as autocrats are not automatically identical. Yet dictatorship is not the solution. Social unrest and violence, he argues, must be understood together with the poverty, unemployment and inequality that generate them. His analogy is that one cannot remove poisonous snakes while leaving the bushes and anthills in which they thrive.

He concludes that philosophy is not confined to religion, metaphysics or heaven. It is present in ordinary life—in the road, the shore, sunlight, wind, affection and even harsh social realities. Students of philosophy should therefore understand themselves as future custodians of Tamil Nadu and India.

## Separate source closing note

Speech delivered at a Pachaiyappa's College function in Chennai on 18 November 1980.
