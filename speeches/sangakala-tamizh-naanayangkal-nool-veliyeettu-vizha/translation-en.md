# Sangam-Age Tamil Coins — Book Release Function

Karunanidhi speaks at the release of a work by Dinamalar editor R. Krishnamurthy on Sangam-age Tamil coins. He calls the evening an opportunity to look again at ancient Tamilakam, its rulers and the historical evidence that should be preserved for younger generations.

He begins with humour about Krishnamurthy and Dinamalar, recalling a family remark that the editor's house was the “house opposite” or “house that opposed.” Karunanidhi says criticism can be useful when it points an administration toward genuine public shortcomings, and he recalls cases where Dinamalar reports led to action against officials.

He then remembers Krishnamurthy's father, Ramasubbu, as a newspaper founder who valued useful public-interest reporting over sensationalism and as a man associated with anti-caste and Adi-Dravidar social reform.

Karunanidhi responds to praise from scholars at the event by saying he does not claim expertise in every field. He tells the story of a Greek painter and a cobbler: the cobbler's criticism of a painted shoe is accepted, but when he goes beyond his expertise and criticises the woman's eyebrow, the painter tells him to stay with shoes. Karunanidhi uses the anecdote to reject exaggerated claims that he knows every field.

The central theme is the difficulty of reconstructing Tamil history. He says Tamil rulers were praised in poetry, but systematic historical writing was often absent. Literary sources such as the Akananuru, Purananuru and other Sangam works, inscriptions, copper plates and coins therefore become crucial evidence. He recalls the argument, associated in the speech with Vincent Smith, Manonmaniam Sundaram Pillai and Anna, that Indian history should be approached from the Cauvery region rather than only from the Ganga.

Karunanidhi says that when he became Chief Minister after Anna, he asked scholars including Mu. Varadarasanar to work toward a fuller history of Tamil Nadu. He also recalls speaking at the 1956 Tiruchi party conference on “Dravidian contacts with the world,” where coins, inscriptions and external evidence helped illuminate ancient commercial and cultural connections.

A substantial part of the speech praises Krishnamurthy's numismatic discoveries. Karunanidhi quotes his account of obtaining a coin in Madurai bearing Tamil-Brahmi writing, cleaning it, studying it with a magnifying glass and identifying the name `பெருவழுதி`. He connects this to the Pandyan ruler Palyagasalai Mudukudumi Peruvazhuthi.

He notes further discoveries of coins bearing a leaping tiger, understood as Chola coins, and copper coins from Karur bearing the bow-and-arrow emblem of the Cheras. These finds, he says, challenged the claim that coinage was absent from ancient Tamilakam.

Karunanidhi also mentions the earlier work of Walter Elliot and his 1886 publication on South Indian coins. He then praises Krishnamurthy's contributions to Tamil typesetting and computer typography, including a line-type/typesetting method and what the speech calls the “Krishnamurthy Design.”

He closes by saying the book has been created through serious research and excavation and can help build a new historical understanding from the evidence of the past.
