Karunanidhi opens at Yadava College by reflecting on student leadership and on the institution's growth. He then turns to the historical identity of the “Tamil citizen,” arguing that Tamil society was divided over time by caste labels that obscured a common social identity.

The speech invokes the Self-Respect movement, Periyar and Anna in challenging caste hierarchy. Karunanidhi distinguishes between a long-term ideal and immediate rights: the goal may be a society of “one community,” but until that goal is reached, historically disadvantaged communities must not lose the protections and representation needed to secure equality.

He discusses the struggle to permit qualified people of all castes to become temple priests, presenting this as a question of equal rights even for those who may hold a broader rationalist critique of temples themselves.

A substantial portion of the address traces communal representation and reservation policy. He refers to the Justice Party era, the Communal G.O., litigation in the courts, constitutional amendment, and later changes in reservation percentages for backward and Scheduled communities. He also cites Karnataka's Devaraj Urs as an example of creating a share for economically poor people across castes without taking away the established share of backward and oppressed communities.

Karunanidhi warns students against confusing the abolition of caste with the removal of safeguards before equality has actually been achieved. He urges office-holders to judge demands by their justice rather than by the political source from which they come, invoking the Tirukkural principle of discerning truth regardless of who speaks it.

The speech closes by asking students to think seriously about the future of Tamil society. He says his purpose is not to incite them but to make them think; thoughtful students, he argues, can help ensure that future generations do not inherit renewed inequality.
