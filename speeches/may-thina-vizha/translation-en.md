### May Day Celebration

I stand before you, having received the opportunity to convey May Day greetings and my heartfelt good wishes to all working people.

The guardians of our movement, the ministers, and the trade-union leaders who spoke here have explained all the special features of the May Day celebration and the extent to which the Labour Progressive Union Federation affiliated with the D.M.K. has both the duty and the right to conduct this function.

The first of May has not only been a day of the working class. In earlier times, in many countries in the northern part of Europe, it was also celebrated as a festival day. In Rome, during a period when the first of May had not been identified as a day for workers, they celebrated the first of May in worship of the goddess of flowers.

## PDF 129 / printed p.128

In England, on the first of May, boys and girls would gather in one place early in the morning carrying flowers, erect a pole, call it the “May Pole,” tie bunches of flowers and ribbons of many colours to it, and dance round and round that pole.

In America in those days, on this day in the United States of America, people made small paper baskets, filled them with flowers, hung them on the doors of their friends' houses, and rejoiced. Even today they take part in that enjoyment.

Thus, there have been periods when this day was celebrated in various countries as a joyful festival, like a holiday. But that period changed, and a day arose on which it came to be celebrated as the great day of the working class. As the friends who spoke before me have pointed out, on 7 October 1881 a federation of trade unions in America—not in Russia, not in China, but in America—met and decided to begin a struggle with the demand that “from the first of May 1886, an eight-hour working day must be made lawful.”

The decision was made in 1884. The year announced for the struggle to begin was

## PDF 130 / printed p.129

1886. After that, the influence of this struggle began to spread throughout the world. In particular, and especially in the city of Chicago, worker comrades became intensely involved in this struggle, were subjected to repression, and endured every suffering and sorrow, even to the point of kissing the hangman's noose. From 1889 onward, in a manner that honoured that memory, that kindled fire in the hearts of the working class while honouring that memory, that sought through that fire to create a revolution, and as a symbol that such a revolution must become a victorious revolution of the workers, it was declared that from the first of May 1889 this day should be celebrated throughout the world. The federation of American workers too announced this, and from that year this day began to be celebrated throughout the world.

As those who spoke here have pointed out, in India this May Day celebration was first held in our Tamil Nadu. As brother Rahman mentioned here, the May Day function held on the Chennai seashore on the first of May 1923 took place under the leadership of M. Singaravelar, who went about as a sun of rationalism and a sculptor of thought.

It was at that meeting that Singaravelar made the demand: “On May Day, workers should be given a paid

## PDF 131 / printed p.130

holiday.” That demand was fulfilled 47 years later, during the period when this humble person had assumed office as Chief Minister. Those who spoke before me have pointed out how closely the Dravidian movement is connected with May Day—I say “May Day” merely by way of identification—and with the working class. More particularly, not only was the May Day celebration first held in India here in Tamil Nadu; even the first trade union in India was begun in Tamil Nadu, and that too in the city of Chennai.

You know that our great leader Thanthai Periyar was the first from India to travel to and tour the land of Russia, which stood as a garden of common ownership, was attracted by those principles, brought them to Tamil Nadu, and proclaimed them, and that this movement and Tamil Nadu have that distinction.

In 1929, a Self-Respect Conference was held at Chengalpattu. Many progressive resolutions were passed at that conference. You will remember—I have already pointed this out in the Legislative Assembly. The resolution that women, like men, should have equal rights in property was passed at the Self-Respect Conference held at Chengalpattu in 1929. Even that resolution, 60 years later, in 1989, was made into law, and that opportunity

## PDF 132 / printed p.131

came to me—to this government—you know. (Applause)

At that same Chengalpattu conference, a revolutionary resolution was passed for workers. It was not merely the resolution that “workers must receive wages according to the work they perform and according to their needs, and those wages must be paid to them.” At the Self-Respect Movement conference of that time, a resolution was also passed supporting the then-ongoing South Indian Railway workers' strike and condemning all the repressive measures carried out to suppress that strike. Such a resolution was passed at the 1929 Chengalpattu Self-Respect Conference.

Before this, there was an event in 1921. It was spoken about here. Buckingham Mill. That mill had approximately 13,000 workers. The leader of that trade union was Thamizh Thendral Thiru. Vi. Ka.—a breeze for Tamil, but one who could become a storm for workers. Under the leadership of that Thamizh Thendral Thiru. Vi. Ka., all 13,000 working people carried on a struggle for six months. They fought against the mill management.

Lord Wellington was the Governor then. It was a time when Lord Wellington sat as the guardian of British imperialism. He not only filed a case against that struggle, but on that

## PDF 133 / printed p.132

basis issued an order exiling Thiru. Vi. Ka., the field commander of that struggle. The government then in office was the Justice Party government. To put it plainly, it was a government of the Dravidian movement. The Raja of Panagal was the Chief Minister of the day. In those days the title was “Premier”—Prime Minister.

You see the large building constructed here, “Panagal Maligai,” at the entrance of this Saidapet constituency—the person to whom that name Panagal Maligai belongs, that Raja of Panagal, was then the head of the government.

Lord Wellington ordered that Thiru. Vi. Ka. be exiled. The leaders of the Dravidian movement—its successors and pioneers, Natesanar and Thyagarayar—met the head of the government, the Raja of Panagal, and said: “Go immediately to Lord Wellington and tell him firmly that if Thamizh Thendral Thiru. Vi. Ka. is exiled, the Cabinet will resign.” Saying this, they sent him to Wellington's residence.

Accordingly, the Raja of Panagal—the leader of the Justice Party, the head of the Justice Party government—accepted the request of Thyagarayar and the request of Dr. Natesanar and met Lord Wellington. He said, “Thiru. Vi. Ka. must not be exiled for the labour struggle, for having led it.” Wellington refused. “If you refuse, we will resign from office,” said the Raja of Panagal.

## PDF 134 / printed p.133

The order was immediately withdrawn, and British imperialism yielded that day, saying, “You remain in office. I will not exile Thamizh Thendral Thiru. Vi. Ka.” The reason was the Justice Party government of that time. The leaders who stood at the forefront of that government were people such as Thyagarayar and Natesanar. The movement that came in that line is the Dravidian movement, the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam.

Those who spoke here have listed how much concern the D.M.K., after coming to power, showed for workers' welfare and what kinds of schemes it implemented. I wish to set them out briefly, one by one.

It was under the D.M.K. government in 1968 that we implemented medical schemes in hill plantations under the Plantation Establishments Act.

It was the D.M.K. government that, in 1969, for the first time appointed a minister for the Labour Welfare Department.

As all the friends have pointed out, it was the D.M.K. government that granted a paid holiday for this very day. (Applause)

Not stopping at the state level, we stressed that this holiday was needed throughout the whole of India. During the period when V. P. Singh was Prime Minister, we made a request to him; he immediately accepted it and announced that “throughout India, ‘May’ Day is a holiday.” That too was because of the effort we undertook.

## PDF 135 / printed p.134

For the welfare of workers, it was the Kazhagam government that created a Labour Welfare Fund and, in 1971, established a Labour Welfare Board to administer it.

It was the D.M.K. government that made provision in law to provide free medical treatment to plantation workers and their families.

For the welfare of workers, from 1972 onward it was the D.M.K. government that introduced a scheme to provide gratuity to workers upon retirement, as is available to government employees.

It was the D.M.K. government that, as early as 1972, created an Industrial Accident Relief Fund so that workers affected by accidents, drivers of lorries, autos, private cars, taxis and buses, and those engaged in building construction could receive appropriate relief and the suffering of their families could thereby be eased.

It was the Kazhagam government that, as early as 1972, started welfare centres in Chennai, Madurai and Coimbatore with tailoring-training classes for women workers and workers' wives, and kindergarten classes for their children.

For the purpose of creating good union leaders, providing training to workers and individual officers, and providing refresher training to Labour Department officials, it was the Kazhagam government that established the Tamil Nadu Institute of Labour Education in 1973.

## PDF 136 / printed p.135

For the welfare of contract workers, it was the Kazhagam government that enacted a separate law in 1971. In 1975, by framing some additional rules under that law, it was the Kazhagam government that protected the welfare of contract workers.

Accepting the request made in the Legislative Assembly by comrade Varadarajan of the Marxist Party, it was the D.M.K. government that, immediately within a single week, created the May Day Park and erected the May Day memorial there.

Beyond all these things, as our trade-union leaders and the Labour Welfare Minister here have pointed out, workers who are not organized do not receive many workers' welfare schemes. In order that these benefits may reach them, committees were set up in the 1997–98 Budget to study the problems of these workers.

As recommended by those committees, the government has decided to establish an Unorganized Workers Welfare Board this year. Who are the workers who are to benefit through this Board? load-carrying workers, match-industry workers, handloom workers without unions, power-loom workers, brick-manufacturing workers, workers engaged in dyeing, cashew-shelling workers, rope-making workers, pandal-erecting workers, appalam-making workers,

## PDF 137 / printed p.136

synthetic-diamond polishing workers, goldsmiths, barbers, laundry workers, potters, printing workers, cooking and hotel workers, people employed in private shops, tailoring workers, cycle-rickshaw drivers, auto-rickshaw drivers, hired-car drivers, salt-pan workers, and others—workers in 43 kinds of unorganized occupations, approximately 25 lakh people in all. I wish to tell you that it is the D.M.K. government that is establishing and announcing a Board from which they can benefit and is going to put it into operation.

Through all that I have set out, not only through government in Tamil Nadu, and not only through the party: during the 1969–70 period when I assumed responsibility for government, a resolution was passed at the D.M.K. General Council held in Tiruchirappalli that there should be a trade-union federation affiliated with the D.M.K. On that basis, our movement's stalwarts travelled throughout Tamil Nadu and established the Labour Progressive Union Federation. All the predictions—“Will this survive? Will it advance? Does it have a future?”—were treated as trivial; it sprouted and grew, and today, having put down aerial roots, the movement called the Labour Progressive Union Federation stands in Tamil Nadu like a banyan tree. (Loud applause)

On this important day, on behalf of this movement, although I am the leader of the D.M.K.,

## PDF 138 / printed p.137

I speak here before you in relation to its affiliated auxiliary organization, the T.Mu.S., and voice not a resolution, but an opinion. I ask that this be accepted as the resolution of this May Day public meeting.

One-party rule in India is no more.

The position has now become firmly established that henceforth a Central Government can be formed only with the participation of state parties such as the D.M.K.

In this situation, in every state a central trade-union organization has been formed and is functioning like the T.Mu.S. Federation.

When there was one-party rule, organizations such as INTUC, AITUC and CITU at the Centre were recognized by the Central Government.

If state-level organizations such as the T.Mu.S. Federation, which contain a large section of workers, are not recognized, then when decisions are taken and have to be implemented, those decisions cannot reflect the feelings of the workers in the respective states.

Therefore, I ask all of you not merely to applaud the resolution that, through a vote, the Central Government should recognize the organization that represents the majority of workers in each state, but to raise your hands and endorse it. (Loud applause)

## PDF 139 / printed p.138

(Everyone in the gathering raised their hands, applauded, and endorsed it.)

I convey my appreciation and greetings to brother Kittu, who arranged these fine May Day celebration events, and to area secretary Murugan.

You may have come expecting some political views in connection with May Day. Not only you—the journalists too have come. So, a few political views.

At this time when May Day is being celebrated, there are some other parties too that have always had a rightful claim to it. Whether it is the C.P.I., the C.P.M., or the broader communist movements, they are in a position legitimately to claim that right.

It was spoken about here—brother Duraimurugan mentioned the S.N.P. struggle, the Nangavaram struggle, the Pettai Vaythalai struggle, and so on. Some creatures who do not even know whether all these were black or red are celebrating May Day today. Let them celebrate; I am not saying they should not.

But while celebrating in that way, they deliberately engage in throwing dust and abuse at the D.M.K. and the D.M.K. government; that is what is amusing.

Madam Jayalalithaa, while speaking today at a wedding function held in connection with May Day,

## PDF 140 / printed p.139

said that Karunanidhi does not care about the poor at all, that the poor will never respect Karunanidhi, and that the poor are “not prepared to trust Karunanidhi.” This “friend of the poor”—whether she is a friend of the poor or an owner of seven bungalows (Applause)—says this. The newspapers have printed in huge letters Jayalalithaa's words: “‘The poor are not prepared to trust Karunanidhi.’ I am the guardian of the poor.” I read them.

Do we not know about this “friend of the poor”?

E. V. A. Vallimuthu of Kovilpatti was the presidium chairman of the A.D.M.K. both while the A.D.M.K. was in power and while that party had influence. A dear friend, he had held the responsibility of presidium chairman of the A.D.M.K. from the time of M.G.R. He suddenly passed away. He had lived a long life. From the early period of the Dravidian movement, through the times of Thanthai Periyar and Anna, he associated with all of us and helped build this movement. He had been made presidium chairman of the A.D.M.K. by M.G.R. He died in Kovilpatti while serving as presidium chairman of the A.D.M.K. in Jayalalithaa's time. Did this lady who toils for the poor go there? Was he someone holding an ordinary position in that party? He was the party's presidium chairman. She did not even go for his death.

## PDF 141 / printed p.140

In contrast, a few weeks later, a millionaire named Subburam Reddy died over here in Andhra. As soon as she heard of his death, Jayalalithaa, who was then Chief Minister, boarded the special aircraft that had been purchased for the government—the private plane costing 20 crore rupees—and she and her “not-born-with-her...?”—the two of them—went to Nellore or some other town in Andhra, paid homage to Subburam Reddy, and returned on that aircraft.

Poor Subburam Reddy. A millionaire poor man. So, it was possible to go there by aircraft. But Vallimuthu, who had been the party's presidium chairman, died in Kovilpatti. She had no mind to go there.

Why stop there? Nearby in Alandur, our dear friend Mohanarangam had been the leader of the A.D.M.K. members in the Rajya Sabha during M.G.R.'s time. Jayalalithaa too was then a member there. He died of a heart attack. Everyone went. It was only nearby Alandar. M.G.R. had even named a building after that Mohanarangam. That was how much affection M.G.R. had for him. In the period after M.G.R. was gone—after his death—Mohanarangam died of a heart attack.

Is Alandar in America? Yet this

## PDF 142 / printed p.141

“friend of the poor,” who could travel to Andhra and place a garland on the body of that millionaire lord, could not go to Alandur and pay homage to Mohanarangam. Why stop there? She did not go to Anna's memorial this year, did she? Even for that, did she not send proxy Nedunchezhiyan? Was not Navalar Nedunchezhiyan used as a proxy for that too?

What kind of speech is that Navalar Nedunchezhiyan making? A man who received wonderful titles from that lady! He stands as the very illustration of all those rare expressions that “blossomed” from Madam Jayalalithaa's sacred mouth. “Fallen. . .” You complete the rest. (Applause) Street. . . You complete the remainder. After receiving titles like these, Navalar Nedunchezhiyan is now cursing: “Are you filing a case against me? Will you prosper?” There is a case against Navalar Nedunchezhiyan, but he has not been arrested. If he goes to court and proves himself innocent, we will be happy.

What case? Others may have the T.V. case, the TANSI case, the coal case, the bungalow case, the asset-accumulation case; his case is the shoe case. (Loud laughter) I will say it respectfully: the footwear case. That too is a case concerning the purchase of footwear for children on behalf of the government. It is a corruption case. When Indira Kumari, who committed that corruption, said that 4 lakh shoes should be bought, the case is that the Justice Minister signed saying, “Buy 9 lakh,” because there would not be enough scope for greater corruption with only that amount.

## PDF 143 / printed p.142

He asks, “A case against me? Will you prosper?” When I was young, carrying the Tamil flag in my hand, I marched through the streets of Tiruvarur saying, “O Hindi woman who came running here, listen; this is not the land of cowards you came seeking.” All my close relatives then said, “This fellow will never amount to anything.” You know whether I amounted to anything or not. (Applause)

Now Navalar Nedunchezhiyan says: “This fellow will never amount to anything. Will his party amount to anything?” Am I claiming that I am someone built of stone and fixed here forever? Everyone, at some time, has to go. Even such an Ayya has gone. Even such an Anna is no more. Even such a revolutionary poet Bharathidasan is no more. Even such Gandhi Adigal is no more. Even such Nehru is no more. How much longer are you and I going to remain? I ask Navalar Nedunchezhiyan: is cursing people what politics has become today? Make your predictions; that is your wish. We have already held responsibility for government four times. They say they will dismiss this government. If you ask why, they say everything has gone bad.

Anna used to tell a story often. A humorous story. There was a flood in a river, and it carried away a fox. That fox, while being swept along, kept shouting, “The world is gone! The world is gone!” The people on the bank turned, saw it, immediately ran over, and

## PDF 144 / printed p.143

pulled the fox ashore. “You kept saying, ‘The world is gone! The world is gone!’ Where has it gone?” they asked. The fox replied, “If you had not saved me, by this time the world would have been gone—it would have been gone as far as I am concerned.”

In the same way, like the fox that cried, “The world is gone! The world is gone!”, Jayalalithaa says, “The law is gone! The law is gone!” The law will not disappear. The law will remain. It will remain permanently. For a little while, you may try entering and playing in lanes and by-lanes. The arguments of some lawyers may help with that. But justice will not tilt. Even if it tilts, just as it straightened itself in Madurai, just as justice, after tilting, was made upright in Madurai, justice will be made upright. At this revolutionary May Day celebration, I issue this as a warning to Jayalalithaa.
