## PDF 26

I have spoken at so many meetings in Kanchipuram. I have seen meetings at which I spoke in obedience to Anna's command, and processions that I conducted. I have participated in meetings organized by our dear brother C. V. M. Annamalai when he was district secretary. I have seen—and participated in—meetings held here by the late Madurantakam Arumugam when he was district Kazhagam secretary. I have also seen meetings organized by people such as K. D. S. Mani and Cantonment Shanmugam when they were office-bearers of the Kazhagam.

But today's meeting answers the question, "What would it be like if all those people came together and conducted one?" Kanchipuram is holding a gathering like a sea **(loud applause)**, and when I see—

## PDF 27

—before me all of you seated here as drums of uprising, like the army of Kanchi's ruler Narasimhavarman when he set out in battle array to defeat Pulikesi **(loud applause)**, I behold this sight.

Under the arrangements of our younger brother T. M. Anbarasan, when I see all the decorations, electric lights and fireworks put up by our Kazhagam warriors and administrators, I stand before you with the full confidence that even though we lost power, even though we were defeated, the people are still on our side—that they will not abandon not only me, but this Kazhagam under my leadership.

This day is Veera Vanakkam Day! For whom? It is the day on which we pay homage to our beloved Udanpirappugal who set out to protect the language and gave their precious lives. In 1965: Singa Tamizhan Chinnasamy of Keezhapazhuvur in Tiruchi district; Sivalingam and Aranganathan of Chennai Virugambakkam; Veerappan of Tiruchi Ayyampalayam; Muthu of Sathyamangalam; the Mayavaram student Sarangapani; Shanmugam of Viralimalai; Muthu of Keeranur; Rajendran of Sivagangai; and Dandapani of Coimbatore Peelamedu.

Then, in 1986, those who gave their lives opposing compulsory Hindi: Bodi Murugan, Allur Kalimuthu, Melur Jegajeevan Ram, Ariyalur Pugazhendi, Ulundurpet Panneer—

## PDF 28

—Selvam, Needamangalam Govindarajan, Kulithalai Srinivasan, Coimbatore Bala Subramaniam, Guduvancheri Subramaniam, Kattumannarkoil Kaliya Perumal, Salem Arunachalam, Marudur Mookkan, Kalaignar Pithan, Aruppukkottai Mariyappan, Sundarapatti Vedamanickam and Mullipattu Natarajan. Thinking of how they cast their lives into the embers to protect Tamil, became gold of principle, and became one with us as Udanpirappugal mingled with our very lives, from amidst these many thousands gathered in Kanchi I offer all of them my bowed homage **(loud applause)**.

### The Dravidian race — descendants of the Cheras, Cholas and Pandyas!

They did not leap into fire, bury themselves in the earth, or die by taking poison in the expectation that one day a meeting would be held in Kanchipuram with Karunanidhi, and that at that meeting the people would offer greetings as a way of showing gratitude. If one asks why they did it—why they wrapped the sacrificial fire around their bodies and gave up their lives—it was to stop the danger that had come, and could come, to Tamil, so that Tamil would not disappear from our Tamil soil;

## PDF 29

he is one who came in the Pandyan lineage—a kinsman of the Chola—a sibling of the Chera; these were the three crowned kings. Three flags, "three Tamil," and this Dravidian land surrounded by three seas were ruled by them. To tell today's Tamils, who are their descendants, that their language—I say, their language—must be changed is to say that their life, their breath, their very respiration **(loud applause)** must be brought to a stop; pushing Tamil behind with that aim, the Hindi language stepped in here in 1938.

As the friends who spoke here pointed out, I was then a fourteen-year-old school student. In those days I sang this *parani*:

"O Hindi maiden who came running, listen—
this is not the land of cowards you came seeking!
In the land where heroic Tamil is cherished,
we will not accept flavourless words in our homes!"

I wrote that *parani* and gave it to the students' procession. **(Applause)**

But if my age today is eighty-eight **(loud applause)**, I have been fighting for the language alone for nearly seventy-four years. I fought holding Periyar's hand. I fought holding Anna's hand. I fought as one among the ranks of commanders of the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam. All those struggles went on and on, ended and ended; yet, as a friend here pointed out today, if you ask whether we have reached the complete goal, the answer is no. To fight for the language—

## PDF 30

### Members of all parties have the right!

I will not say that opposition to Hindi domination and the protection of Tamil are policies belonging only to the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam, as someone here said. Speaking of people in other parties, our friends said: we fought; we writhed like worms; we were imprisoned; we were subjected to torture; after making so many sacrifices, we have the right today to pay homage to the heroes of the language struggle.

They spoke as though people belonging to other parties did not have this right. I say this: if people of every party claim this right for themselves and join us, if at least on this one issue we set aside party differences and accept this one truth—that we are all Tamils, members of Dravidian society, people nurtured by Periyar and people who came in Anna's path—and if the entire Tamil race enters the language struggle today, our ultimate goals will certainly be fulfilled.

What does the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam say? All the languages of the states in India must be made official languages at the Centre. All state languages, including Tamil, must become official languages of India. What is the difficulty? What do those who ask, "How is that possible?" say?

## PDF 31

They ask: if all the official languages of India's states are made official languages at the Centre, how can communication take place? Do the Telugu people accept this? Has Karnataka accepted it? Have the people of Gujarat accepted it? Have the Marathi people and Malayalis accepted it? If they ask these questions, we will wait until they accept it. But until they reach that point of acceptance, as the first language and as the first step, let us seat the Tamil language upon the throne as an official language at the Centre. **(Loud applause)**

That feeling will come to the Marathi people. That feeling will come to the Malayalis. The Malayalis may even come forward with stronger feeling than we have, because we see the speed they show and the intensity they maintain over Mullai Periyar. Therefore, first make Tamil—the language endowed with every richness: grammatical richness, artistic richness, civilization, culture, all such riches—an official language at the Centre. After that, if you wish, if others want themselves added to that line, add all of them. Do not waste time on the pretext of asking why the Tamil language must be further enriched.

Our language is an ancient language of the world. No language can be spoken of as equal to this language. No—

## PDF 32

—language is equal to this language. Therefore make Tamil an official language at the Centre of India. The struggle that I began when I was fourteen has not ended today. It continues. I began that struggle holding Father Periyar's hand; I began it receiving the blessings of Arignar Anna. Today Periyar is no more; Anna is no more. They are in my heart. **(Loud applause)**

Touching that heart, I say this: what is the duty that you, I, and the Tamil people must perform for them? Think about it. When this struggle took place in 1938, Rajaji was then seated as Chief Minister in the Tamil Nadu Legislative Assembly. What did he say?

### It was the cry of Periyar and Anna that changed Rajaji himself, who had supported Hindi!

"Who opposes Hindi? Only one E. V. R. and one Somasundara Bharathi oppose Hindi. There is nobody else," he said. At that time Pannir Selvam was seated in the opposition ranks. The Pannir Selvam I am speaking of is a different Pannir Selvam. **(Loud applause)** I say this only so that you can identify him: Sir A. T. Pannir Selvam.

## PDF 33

That Pannir Selvam—who disappeared after an aircraft fell into the Oman Sea, leaving Tamils in anguish—was seated in the opposition ranks of the Tamil Nadu Legislative Assembly in 1938. It was looking at them that Mutharignar Rajaji—Rajagopalachari—said: "You oppose Hindi. But you Hindi opponents do not have a majority. There is just one E. V. Ramasamy Periyar and one Somasundara Bharathi. These two alone oppose Hindi."

"So you are the minority," he said. Immediately A. T. Pannir Selvam rose and said: "Honourable Chief Minister, remember what I tell you. You mock us by saying that only two people oppose Hindi. But two people oppose it. You alone are the person bringing in that Hindi. If we ask which is greater—two or one—you are the minority. Two people say they do not want it; they are the majority." **(Loud applause)**

All these are matters recorded in documents. They are strongly recorded in historical notes. The reason I recount all this is that Rajagopalachari introduced compulsory Hindi. The very same Achariyar who brought compulsory Hindi to Tamil Nadu—

## PDF 34

—after hearing Anna's cry and Periyar E. V. R.'s cry, himself said: "Hindi will not do for Tamil Nadu. I oppose Hindi being made our language, a compulsory language." Rajagopalachari himself became an opponent of Hindi.

Why do I say this? When I was a fourteen-year-old youth—or boy—Rajagopalachari introduced Hindi. When I had reached fifty years of age, if he himself said, "Hindi is not wanted; I will not accept Hindi," I remind the Brahmin friends in Kanchipuram of this. Hindi that Rajaji himself could not accept—Rajaji immediately made preparations to oppose it on the ground that if Hindi came, Tamils would become third-class citizens. This is itself something that proclaims to the world the victory of the Dravidian movement.

When we say such things, some people do not like it. But only after seeing it through experience do they feel, "What they said was right after all." It is like this: when we say that we are all Dravidians, some say, "We will not accept that we are Dravidians." Even this morning I listened to a television discussion.

In it, our younger brother Suba. Veerapandian debated with another comrade. In that debate, the person seated opposite Suba. Veerapandian argued, "I will not accept the word Dravidian—"

## PDF 35

"—at all." Suba. Veerapandian asked, "Why will you not accept it?" The other person said, "We have passed a resolution in our party."

He said, "Even the word 'Dravida' must not exist!" Why? Is "Dravida" not Tamil? Is it a word a Tamil will not accept? Is it a word a Tamil has rejected? Scholars speak of the greatness of Dravida; history speaks of it!

It was our leader Anna, born in this very town, who started the weekly newspaper *Dravida Nadu*. **(Loud applause)** The friends of the Justice Party accepted the word "Dravida" on documentary grounds. If anyone says there is no such word as Dravida—that it was merely a word uttered by Periyar Ramasamy, or a word brought in by Arignar Anna—then please let them listen once to India's national song, or read it:

*Jana gana mana adhinayaka jaya he*

### Bharata bhagya vidhata  
### Punjab Sindhu Gujarat Maratha  
### Dravida Utkala Vanga.

Where did the word "Dravida" appearing here come from? This is India's national song. In that national anthem—

## PDF 36

—the words *Dravida Utkala Vanga* occur. If people who accept this, stand up, salute it and pay respect to it, then say, "We will not accept Dravida," please think about this: are those who hear it mad, or are those who say it mad?

White priests who came from foreign countries to Tamil Nadu and learned Tamil—people such as Caldwell—accepted the word "Dravida." They were great scholars who researched and understood the depth, breadth and height of Dravida. History proves it. Historical record says so. Can it be forgotten?

To those who say, "We will not accept even the word Dravida. If a party has the name Dravida in it, we will not join them," I say: please do not stop with this national song. Walk a little along Chennai's seafront. There is a college there on the beach—Presidency College, the State College. At the entrance of that college there is a statue. If you look closely at the statue, on its pedestal the name is written: Dr. U. Ve. Saminathaiyar. Do you know how it is written? "Dravida Vidyabhushana Dr. U. Ve. Saminathaiyar."

## PDF 37

How did "Dravida" come there? If it can come with Saminathaiyar, can it not come with the ordinary man Karunanidhi? It can. Because Dravida is a culture. A race. The culture of a race. All these together explain the word "Dravida." Dravidians are the indigenous inhabitants of this country.

To go further, there is history saying that they lived in India even before the Aryans came; that the Aryans entered here and drove the Dravidians away. Even the histories in schoolbooks state this very clearly.

Therefore, names such as the Dravidian movement and the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam are not used because the word was somehow invented by Periyar, nor because it was invented by Anna. It is a word we have been accustomed to from the time of our great-grandfathers. The name of a race. The name of a race with honour. We must not forget it. Any movement or event conducted on behalf of that race should take pride in the circulation and use of "Dravida" within it.

### The Dravidian cry is the foundation and taproot of the Tamil race!

There was a time when we raised the cry, "Dravida Nadu for the Dravidians!" Now, taking into account India's—

## PDF 38

—unity and integrity, after Anna's deep reflection, there was that book written by Anna in his own hand, a book carrying his handwriting. The whole book was not typeset; Anna himself wrote it and it was reproduced directly as a block from his handwriting. In it, Anna did not abandon Dravida Nadu. He did not abandon the Dravidian race. Even though we gave up the demand "Dravida Nadu for the Dravidians," Anna himself stated emphatically that we belong to that race. That is what I remind you of.

Dravida is not an imaginary word. Dravida is the foundation and taproot of our awakening, the honour of our race, the robust principles we hold to protect that honour, and our firm ideals—that cry called Dravida, that word, that expression. **(Applause)** Whoever attaches "Dravida" to whatever movement, I appreciate them. I respect them. But by using the word Dravida, do not deceive not only non-Dravidians—do not deceive Dravidians themselves. I ask you, Dravidians, you Tamil people, to remain vigilant so that no one is deceived merely by believing, "They too say Dravida."

## PDF 39

As I look at all of you now, I ask myself how such an upsurge—an upsurge that was not there before the election **(applause)**—has come now. I understand what you are saying without saying it: "Only when one is struck does one understand." **(Applause)**

Here in this same Kanchipuram, when Anna stood in an election and came house to house and street to street asking for votes, I was one of those who came with him. In many places Anna would fold his hands in greeting; through that gesture he would indicate that he was asking them to vote for him. I saw it. A scholar whom the world could praise, an incomparable jewel beyond comparison—such a scholar! When he came through these streets of Kanchipuram asking for votes, with us following behind him, I knew people who, when he folded his hands and asked for their vote, turned aside as though they had not seen him.

After seeing that, Anna said, "Come, Karunanidhi! Let us go and sit somewhere a little apart." I understood. Because Anna knew what result awaited him in that election, he felt like saying that we should go somewhere away from these people.

But the very next year that same Anna came and sat as Chief Minister of Tamil Nadu. It was that same Anna who changed the name "Madras State," which we had asked to change, into "Tamil Nadu." **(Applause)**

## PDF 40

It was that same Anna who changed the situation in which Hindi had to be compulsorily studied and declared: "From now on in Tamil Nadu there will be only two languages—Tamil and English. There is no place for Hindi." The election defeat was not the Kazhagam's—

### It was the voters'!

In this very Kanchipuram, where people once insulted him by not even returning his greeting when he bowed to them, you people of Kanchipuram forgot the pain of that one occasion then and there.

Today, at the very time we meet you, you are seated here in your thousands, as I said at the beginning, like drums of uprising. Your slogans all along the route, the decorations you made all along the route—if one is to read the meaning of all these, the defeat suffered today is not ours; it is yours! **(Applause)** A defeat you went looking for! A defeat you yourselves brought upon yourselves! **(Applause)**

When you board a bus, you will know the weight of that defeat! When you go to a shop to buy goods, you will know the heaviness of that defeat! Therefore, it was not the Kazhagam that lost—the losers were you, the voters.

## PDF 41

Those who realize that they have made a mistake will thereafter never make another mistake for the rest of their lives. Such a lesson has come not only to you in Kanchipuram, but to the people throughout Tamil Nadu. Having learned that lesson, respect it and do not forget it. At least in the future, with the firm resolve that "we will not again make the mistake we have made now," protect yourselves and protect the future generations of this society.

I see all of you as Tamils, as Dravidians, as rationalists, as Anna's younger brothers, as Udanpirappugal dearer to me than my own life. Asking this of all of you, thanking you for the welcome you gave me all along the route, and assuring you that whether you strike me or embrace me, I will not forget the people of Kanchipuram, I say: I am one among you. We are all one race. Let us all work together for the advancement of this race. With these words, I take my leave.

> **Translator note:** The frozen Tamil begins this sentence with the unusual shift `அவர்களையெல்லாம் ... உங்களையெல்லாம்`. E2 keeps the Tamil frozen and renders the direct-address sense as "all of you" without treating the source wording as a transcription error.
