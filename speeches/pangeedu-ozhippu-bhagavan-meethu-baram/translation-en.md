### PDF page 52 — printed page 51

#### Abolition of ‘Rationing’!
#### The Burden on Bhagavan!

At the time the great Second World War ended, in the countries of the world that had been battlefields, that had been made battlefields, did not India too experience food shortage and a scarcity of the basic necessities of life?

Scarcity! Food, clothing, shelter, and many, many other things needed for life could not be obtained in the required quantity, at the required time, by those who needed them—by those who needed them with the greatest necessity and urgency. That helpless condition did indeed begin in the Indian subcontinent as a result of the Second World War!

The prices of essential commodities—indeed, even their very distribution—were controlled by the rulers!

Though control helped prevent price rises and the wasteful hoarding of goods, cunning, selfish groups that sought to exploit famine-time and pile up money—

### PDF page 53 — printed page 52

—did grow fat and prosper by it, to some extent!

Even so, at that time control did satisfy the people's needs to some extent!

Stone-hearted men unwilling to submit to control, hard-hearted traders who, taking advantage of the time, had been selling at whatever prices they wished, together with idlers and middlemen, could no longer show their guise openly and entered the black market.

They hid the stocks in their hands in secret warehouses and buildings and, instead of selling at a fair price, sold them illicitly at whatever prices they pleased, taking handfuls of money. Whenever such black-marketeers were discovered, whenever they were caught red-handed, appropriate punishment was imposed.

Countless troubles did arise then, amid many good benefits.

Under that system, suited to the need of that time, there arose—there was created—a good measure that brought benefit: ‘rationing’, the system of ‘food rationing’.

### PDF page 54 — printed page 53

In some parts of the country there was a good harvest, while in certain other parts there was no harvest. Not only did foodstuffs needed by the people of the unproductive regions become unavailable at a fair price, the country's total production itself was below what the people of the country required.

At this time, among the first necessities needed for people's life in the country, the most important necessity—food—fell into famine, and famine began to dance with its hair unbound!

Taking account of the famine that had begun to dance with its hair unbound and of the horror of hunger, the rulers apportioned and distributed the goods that existed, that were available and that could be obtained, according to the total number of people. The rulers themselves accepted the great responsibility of distributing them!

On lands that produced food, they prevented the production of other things!

From landowners, after leaving what they needed for themselves—according to the rationing allowance—and what was needed for seed, the Government itself bought the surplus produce for a price, stored and safeguarded it, and rationed it to the people!

When necessary, the Government also imported foodstuffs from foreign countries to the extent possible and continued to ration them!

### PDF page 55 — printed page 54

A poor worker who had done daily-wage labour and returned home with some little money in his hand could go straight to the shop and easily and reliably obtain the ration rice available to suit his family, and could live with at least some measure of peace.

Before the ‘rationing’ system, in famine-time, when money came into one's hand, rice would not be available in the shop; even if it was available, it would not be available at a fair price! Even if one paid above the fair price, the small quantities that the poor bought—day after day, indeed meal after meal—could be obtained only amid the abuse of having to search, wander, tire oneself out and endure great, endless, unceasing trouble! At some times, even that was simply not available!

In such a distress-filled and dangerous time, only those with means could store food and live contentedly, while others were trapped and writhing in a cruel, inescapable whirlpool of suffering. Food control—the rationing system—brought that condition under control!

‘I have seventy acres. I shall eat as I please! Even if it does not rain for seven or eight years, I have no worry. I have stored it away.’ And while neighbours, though they had money in hand, because they were poor—

### PDF page 56 — printed page 55

—were labourers with nothing of their own, landless workers who nevertheless cultivated and divided the landlord's land and made green paddy grow, and while they withered from hunger without food or foodstuffs, those useless fellows, those human beasts, could live without concern watching them suffer. Everyone knows that the ‘rationing’ system appeared to such people as a lesson—indeed, even like a cobra dancing with its hood spread.

‘Property gathered with so much hardship! How much manure I poured in, cultivated and raised the crop. Some fellow from somewhere has come to measure it out and give it away,’ the farmers, owners of many kanis of land, those living proudly with mitta and mirasu, wearied and sighed whenever food-rationing officers came to procure foodstuffs!

Food rationing had been functioning on a large scale as a useful and established system.

Rationing has existed in this province for many years!

Over time, under the Congress Government that claimed, ‘We are the people endowed with the country's mitta, mirasu and patta rights, and we are the ones entitled to rule the country,’ rationing became like the story of ‘the donkey wearing down—

### PDF page 57 — printed page 56

—into an ant’: not only did it fall from sixteen ounces to below six ounces, but the apostles of non-violence (!) who went about as food ministers even preached to human beings, ‘Live by eating cottonseed, tamarind seed, and even the oil-cake eaten by cattle,’ and the system collapsed, disintegrated, decayed and was ruined into such a degraded state!

There were natural failures too! The monsoon failed; for many years there was no rain; the fertility of the soil declined; produce fell to a certain level. I state at the outset that we speak while taking all these things into account.

If we consider the produce that is grown, the goods that are needed, the goods procured by the Government, and the goods that somehow escape procurement in between—if we look even at the statistics supplied by the Government itself—can it fail to become apparent that the obstacles behind the food shortage are the Government's failure to procure properly, transport, the safeguarding of foodstuffs, and the lack of attention to the scale of losses occurring in between—

### PDF page 58 — printed page 57

—can we really be placed in a position where it is possible to think otherwise?

Why this condition! This degraded condition! A condition of having nothing, when everything exists! Question marks, the voices of the people's mental anguish, their sobs, keep rising toward those who have come to rule, while the country groans.

Is even the small quantity of food that is available wholesome and fit for use? No!

Ministers! Manickavelars who go about with your chests held high saying that you are the representatives of the people! Achariyar who is praised as a great man and as a great sage! Think in your hearts—not once, but a thousand times, think and think—and do you have the courage wholeheartedly to deny this? Will it come? Will you come forward? Why will you not?

Caught in the alluring net cast by mitta and mirasu, attached and devoted to the polished words of farm proprietors, remembering the useful support given by the rich during election times, and thinking that the office obtained must somehow be preserved, it does not appear that anything—any cry at all of the poor—is properly, fully and calmly considered—

### PDF page 59 — printed page 58

—may we not ask this of the rulers who rule the country by the votes of its people? Speak with courage in your hearts! Is it an offence? Why is it an offence? What is the offence, and how is it an offence?

I ask you to consider carefully that these questions, these question marks, arise from the desire that the voices of people crying ‘hunger, hunger’, with nothing to eat and finding even the food they obtain useless, should reach your ears, and from the thought that good results should thereby be produced.

What is the condition of the country today? Are the people of the country living a good life? Why not? Because of what?

On all four sides of the country, the great flood of tears of unemployed handloom weavers is overflowing beyond control!

On one side are those who suffer unemployed because of prohibition; on another side is the pitiable multitude of farm labourers suffering without rain, looking to their honour, fearing for their honour, their very lives tormented;

(Factory workers who, even after working, suffer without the required facilities or wages proportionate to their labour, and still other workers! Between them, neither this nor that—neither poor nor rich—those living month after month on some small amount—

### PDF page 60 — printed page 59

—but on one fixed, restricted amount of income: the worn-down condition of the middle class!

Is this not how the country is withering into a wasteland today—a large part of it, the greater part of this province? Are most people living a life? No!

They are not living. Leave aside a prosperous life: is there even a way to live with self-respect to the extent of merely keeping life in the body?

For people who, caught in the whirl of life, without means, without resources, without a way, were withering, wasting away, stagnating and suffering, there came sudden news as though a mountain had rolled down upon their heads. Yes, friends! Yes! A startling piece of news, news that made them suffer as though stung by a scorpion, came as an order of those who had come to rule!

The excellent system, the scheme called rationing—food rationing—has been abolished, removed, cast aside as unnecessary!

Why? For what reason? Has the production of foodstuffs in the country suddenly increased? No, no! Then for what reason?

### PDF page 61 — printed page 60

Have the people's state of mind and the fertility of the soil grown and matured to such an extent, and have order and regulation become so firmly established, that one can say rationing is no longer needed in the country?

Will prices no longer rise? Will produce no longer stagnate in vain? Was the abolition of rationing a good decision born of thinking through all this and arriving at a conclusion?

None of these is the reason; none was shown as the reason, nor was any stated!

It is all the work of Achariyar, who rules by placing the burden on Bhagavan; a sudden divine play! A sacred service!

Achariyar announced, ‘I have abolished the food-rationing system by placing the burden on Bhagavan.’

About the rationing system there were defects, offences and corruptions so unceasing, unending and unresolved that one might think them impossible to solve—not because the system itself was wrong, but because of disorderly methods that could not carry the system out. Many people began to speak about them, condemn them, point them out and question them. Was the thought of that the reason? Fear? The act of a helpless coward? What is the true reason for the abolition of rationing!

The burden is on Bhagavan, they say. For the abolition of rationing!

### PDF page 62 — printed page 61

Rationing—from the person who has it. After leaving him what he too needs, treating and distributing it as with everyone else, the Government itself buys produce for a price and directly rations it to the people at a fair price: a good, honest and civilized system. Is it said today to be unnecessary not because the condition of things has made that clear, but because administrative deficiency and disorder caused agitation among the people of the country, because the grievance expressed through their mental distress could not be avoided—and not only could not be avoided, but because there was no intention even to avoid it? Will the people not ask this when they once again suffer under the former famine condition, unable to bear the burden of crushing high prices?

They removed the rationing system without thinking! From the very next moment, did prices—the prices of foodstuffs—not begin to rise? Why the silence?

‘Of course one must pay a higher price for good rice,’ another piece of sophistry is spoken, like driving a spear into a festering wound!

Where did good rice come from only now? Where had it been all these days, when under the rulers' good administration only stinking stuff had been supplied? ‘Why?’ the people ask—

### PDF page 63 — printed page 62

—the people ask! Is it for this that you place the burden on Bhagavan!

Bhagavan! Bhagavan is there to bear the burden, they say. We are supposed to put up with it!

Intelligence, ability, inquiry, experience, education, degrees, Parliament, the legislature, ministers seated on ornamental chairs and receiving thousands in the people's money as monthly salaries—and for the head of a civilized Government that speaks of acting democratically, in the position of being responsible for its office—did this alone appear to be the proper reason?

You could simply keep up a blind bhajan, saying, ‘the path shown by Kannan’, instead of speaking of democracy and holding ministerial office!

A bhajan hall would be enough to preach such doctrine! After studying and obtaining degrees, accepting ministerial office, and then, instead of relieving the people's grievances, speaking of ‘the burden on Bhagavan, poor thing’—is that good? These are questions, question marks, that must be thought through. We must not only ask them of ourselves; we must ask questions that touch the good sense of the people of the country and obtain answers to them!

### PDF page 64 — printed page 63

When questions are asked, memories that startle the heart come and torment us.

On one side, the rulers open gruel centres in the country to sustain the battalions of the starving!

At the same time, ‘Do you want good rice? Pay a higher price and buy it. Do you want rotten rice? Go to the ration shop’—without saying this directly, is this not the roundabout, planned and controlled system, the condition in which the people are held after rationing was abolished?

‘Why not give sixteen ounces? Procure properly. Do not lean, bend and bow toward mitta, mirasu and the farm proprietor’—is this abolition of rationing the action taken in answer to the voices the people raised in this way?

‘Do not ask me. Why should I have this trouble? Go to ruin somehow,’—is this the relief given to those without work, withering without a way or means? Is it a treacherous scheme for destruction?

Does this not make things convenient only for those who live ostentatiously and as the rich?

Where is the poor man to go to buy rice at a higher price!

### PDF page 65 — printed page 64

Put a question, put a question mark, and ask for what reason, with what inner intention, this talk of placing the burden on Bhagavan is being spoken!

I believe that the ever-new problems, thoughts and answers stirred up by questions, by question marks, will help open up the hearts of the people!
