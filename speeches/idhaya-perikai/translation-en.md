#### A Small Drop, a Great Flood.

A storm of anguish arose. It devastated the flower garden called affection that had existed between us and our leader. The burden of condemning our leader fell upon our hearts. Because of the marriage episode which he calls very, very ordinary, our bewilderment and tears became futile, and in our condition there came about a “regrettable change.”

In the very first issue of the *Maalai Mani* newspaper, Anna wrote the editorial in these words:

**A regrettable change has occurred.**

That sentence must be considered with care.

Even with thousands upon thousands of younger brothers who have studied history—who bear the marks of daily sacrifice in international politics—who, through speech, writing and acting, work with enough ability to turn everyone from great scholars to ordinary people toward their point of view; even with them, working so that opponents are stunned, enemies enraged, doubters delighted, and those who cursed become unsettled; even with tens of thousands of genuine workers who toil without rest like the great waves of the sea—

### PDF page 5 — printed page 4

—even with all of them at hand, if scholar Anna, who possesses the maturity of mind and strength of will to conduct a great political movement, had to write in anguish of a “regrettable change,” how can that situation possibly be described?

When gold is being brought out of a mine and a rock suddenly rolls down, the effort to escape that rock and the effort we made to save our principle were one and the same.

How we trembled! How utterly we wilted! How many tears we shed! The sound of our choking sobs shook the whole Tamil land! For a few days we became people consumed by anxiety—statues that did not know what to do.

When we remember those few days—alas—the prayer that leaps in our hearts is surely: may those days never return.

On one side was the thunderbolt that we were separating. Yet after the separation, greater than the sorrow that we had separated was the realization that we who had parted had now become people charged with a great future; that we had become bullocks yoked to the noble chariot called public service. How were we going to achieve our ideals?

### PDF page 6 — printed page 5

Could we do it?.... Driven by such feelings of responsibility, we stood bewildered. That condition itself was terribly frightening.

More frightening still were the weapons of abuse hurled at us—the shafts of ridicule, the arrows of mockery, the darts of slander.

“Can these people run a movement!”

“These things are short-lived.”

“Will cultivation entrusted to a little child ever make it home?”

Such taunts danced about.

The doubt, “Can these people run a movement?” changed, and the answer arrived: these are precisely the people who can.

The prophecy “short-lived” disappeared, and the pet name “immortals” was bestowed upon us.

The proverbs about “a child’s farming” were put to shame. We can now see young bulls roaring proudly: Behold the yield the little children have produced—see the harvest—see the stacks piled high!

The talk that it would all end in the selfish interests of a few was shattered. Front-line fighters, proclaiming that every breath is for the country, now advance with graceful gait like brothers of a battalion—

### PDF page 7 — printed page 6

—the fruit of our effort has become such a camp.

No one else discovered and gave us the new name “Tear-Drops.” We gave ourselves that sorrowful name. There is no disgrace in a poor person calling himself poor.

There is nothing laughable in one who toils calling himself a labourer. We who shed tears and cried aloud do not lose our honour by calling ourselves Tear-Drops. On the contrary, we take pride—we swell with dignity—we walk with a proud stride. Why?

What riddled the Tsar’s rule—and the trickster Rasputin—with holes like a sieve? A teardrop.

What struck the napes of the devilish aristocrats of France? A teardrop.

What made the “Chinese island” become Red China? A teardrop.

A teardrop has brought down dictators. In many places a teardrop has summoned drops of blood. Those who treated tears with contempt ended up giving their heads freedom from their necks.

‘The tear shed by the poor is a sharp sword.’

The answer is not a question mark. Here stands our growing strength; this is the true power contained in the idealist’s tear.

If we have grown, has it been in an ordinary way? Without discipline, like the swelling of a wild river? With alphabet primers whose very organization is unknown?......No, no........

We become embodiments of exhilaration. Our hearts leap with the joy of how honourably we Tear-Drops have performed our duty.

### PDF page 10 — printed page 9

Thus, as a volcano that opposition cannot approach; as a pleasure-grove for the young bulls of ideals; as a honeyed song sounding ceaselessly across Dravidam; as an enduring beauty that plays in victorious smiles and heroic stride—there rises the upper pavilion of the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam, built by workers.

In this pavilion there will be no song of excitement from the pleasure-seeker. There will be the victorious folk-song of the worker. There will be no trumpet of one floating in luxury, surrounded by servants and attendants. There will be tigers who set out bearing the spear of ideals—so that the poor may live and the deceiver may fall.

There will be no conspiracy of moss-covered minds asking, “Shall we pounce upon the city of money?..... Shall we besiege the fortress of office?” in this pavilion!

Waves of affection will keep surging here: let us cherish those who work—let us place our trust in those who do not desire office.

There will be no disguise; there will be affection. There will be no petty thought of “work for wages”; there will be the broad thought of “action for principle.”

It is in such a pavilion that the righteous-struggle fighters of the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam move about.

Righteous-struggle fighters! Ah! How joy overflows within us!........... Those whose eyes once held tears—

### PDF page 11 — printed page 10

—now stand with spears in their hands. We must think of what a magnificent task they have accomplished even after their hearts had been broken.

One day—yes, on September 17, 1949, in beautiful Chennai, Commander Anna shed tears at the inauguration of the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam—before he shed words!

Anna’s tears—were they merely tears that he himself poured out? Were they not a gathering of great rain-drops, released when the burning hearts of the younger brothers turned blood into vapour and sent it streaming through the sky called the eyes?

“A small drop, a great flood”—there it is, the great flood rolling in waves! A flood of tears? Or a flood of rejoicing?.......

### PDF page 12 — printed page 11

#### The Delusion of the Street-Gods.

We told individuals what we thought; they flared up without pausing. We took it to meetings and said it there. To those who had filled their stomachs by addressing “Mr. President” and reciting the Lord’s auspicious wedding virtues, our platform looked like a danger signal; they cried out and writhed.

We did not stop with speaking. We showed our thoughts in writing. To all those who had piled up Agraharam stories and essays in *ammami* Tamil, our writing became a warning. They burned with rage.

Those who ought to have trembled because of us—because of our thought, speech and writing—did tremble.

But when the very people for whom we undertook that work—the people who ought to have been happy, the people who ought to have taken pride—are the ones crying out and writhing, that is when astonishment arises in us.

We did not stop with thought, speech and writing. We set out to express our ideas in dramatic form. Ideas that had hitherto been kept covered in this country tore through the curtain and emerged. Ideas created awakening. The wicked, seeing the awakening, grew furious. Their fury is natural.

### PDF page 13 — printed page 12

Those who ought to benefit from the ideas have begun roaming about with eyes that spit fire. This does not even look like hatred of the idea itself. It looks more like the ash of resentment from a heart that has been burnt black, flying in the wind.

#### The Poompuhar Conference.

The condition in which people declared that the *Kamba Ramayanam* alone was the honey-river of poetry and that Kamban alone was emperor of poets has now begun to erode. Those who delighted in garlanding Kamban alone have begun to show respect to Ilango Adigal too. Those who regarded a Kamban conference alone as a poetry conference have broadened their outlook enough to conduct a *Silappathikaram* conference.

No one can forget that we, the people of the rationalist movement, brought revolution to Dravidam not only in politics and society but also in literature. Our criticism of the *Kamba Ramayanam* broke the superstition that had grown so mature that people built a temple to Kamban and worshipped him as a god. When we criticized Kamban, the arrows hurled at us were not few. We were worshipped with cruel words. We were called people without artistic feeling. We were struck with the burning phrase, “beasts who know the taste of the mouth but not the taste of the ear.” Yet victory was ours. Our purpose was not to make the *Kamba Ramayanam* itself impossible to obtain in the country. Our aim was that everyone in the country should understand Kamban’s error—

### PDF page 18 — printed page 17

—that is our objective. The *Kamba Ramayanam* is a spider’s web that portrays Dravidians as demons and monkeys. Therefore, we said, it must be hated. That is the fundamental source of our opposition: Kamban made the Aryan Rama into a god.

We condemned that baseness. We explained that for such a book to be an object of worship for Dravidians was an act devoid of self-respect. The devotees of Kamban said that the ideas in the *Kamba Ramayanam* must simply be allowed for the sake of the age in which it was written. We did not object to those things. Our principal charge was that Kamban had betrayed the Dravidian by depicting an entire people in a degrading manner. Many scholars fought against precisely that. Even at that time we said: we are not enemies of literature. Works such as the *Silappathikaram*, though they contain many ideas of their own age, do not insult the Dravidian; indeed, they elevate the Dravidian, and so we do not hate them. When Tamil treasures such as the *Silappathikaram* lie buried, we have asked why foolish men hold festivals for the *Kamba Ramayanam*, which calls the Tamil a demon. We did not ask this because we had acquired devotional faith in the *Silappathikaram*, nor from religious feeling, nor because of its excessively imaginative stories. It was because it shows how the life of the Tamils was in the age of the *Silappathikaram*—

### PDF page 19 — printed page 18

—because it gives us a book through which to understand that life; because it displays the glory of Dravidian warriors who humbled the crowned heads of the north; because it speaks of the greatness of Dravidian kings who lived for justice; because, even if we do not believe that the character Kannagi performed deeds beyond imagination, it offers us an opportunity to understand how the courage of women of that age was portrayed; and because it is a rare book from which details of the rule of the Chera, Chola and Pandya can be learned. These, and nothing else, are why we support the *Silappathikaram*.

Why do we honour the *Thirukkural*? Because Thiruvalluvar is a divine poet? No. We hold festivals for the Kural because it is a good picture of the Tamil country of that age and because it records Dravidian principles of justice. The Kural is not without defects. Those defects are absorbed by the single fact that it is a work written in its own age. In times to come, even the revolutionary ideas written today may be called old-fashioned.

In general, works such as the Kural and the *Silappathikaram* make the greatness of the Dravidian shine; therefore we praise them. Kamban made the Dravidian into a degraded people; therefore we opposed him—
