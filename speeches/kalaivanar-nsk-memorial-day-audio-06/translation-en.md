## [00:42–03:22] Opening humour, Chinna Annamalai, Kannadasan and Sivaji

At a function for Kalaivanar, there ought not to be any shortage of humour. In that spirit, many of the friends who spoke before me spoke with so much humour that this entire hall resounded with laughter. It would be no exaggeration to say that they placed here an offering worthy of Kalaivanar.

When Chinna Annamalai spoke, he said something: that he had told Kalaivanar something no one else knew and thereby earned Kalaivanar’s praise. Whether even that was something none of us knew, I cannot understand.

I do not know whether even that piece of imagination arose only after he came onto the stage.

We do not know whether some “Tolkappiyar Conference” of this sort ever took place. We do not know whether Kalaivanar and Chinna Annamalai went there. If Kalaivanar were alive, we could go and ask him whether it was true. Taking courage from the fact that certain people are no longer here, many people say all sorts of things and take their jabs.

“I know Periyar; I spoke with him for twenty hours. I spoke with Andavudu for half an hour. Anna told me that all these things should be done exactly this way. I was closely acquainted with Gandhiji”—I think Chinna Annamalai adopted this method precisely to answer, through proper humour, people who go about speaking like this.

My dear friend, the poet Kannadasan, made a point. He noted that he and I had both been close to Kalaivanar. When Nadigar Thilagam Sivaji spoke about Kannadasan, Sivaji said of him: “Even if he abuses me and scolds me, he is the one poet I love.” I am obliged only to tell Sivaji, by way of competition, that this beloved has many lovers.

## [03:22–06:00] The crossed-telephone anecdote

Once, during a period when I was in the opposition, I was staying alone in a hotel in Chennai, writing dialogue for a film. It would have been around eleven in the morning. At that time poet Kannadasan, together with another friend, had broken away from Arignar Anna, started another party, and was attacking me very sharply in his speeches. Hostility between us had grown to the point where, even if we met each other, we would not speak.

At that time, from the hotel where I was staying, I tried to telephone a friend at the Mekala film company and began speaking on that number. While I was talking, I could hear my friend’s voice, and in between I could hear another familiar voice.

You know that such cross-connections often occur on telephones; and sometimes, if the interruption is interesting, those of you who have telephones may listen for a little while and only then put the phone down. That may well be something telephone users know from experience.

When I heard that familiar voice, I stopped the conversation with the manager of the Mekala company who was speaking with me and listened. Kannadasan had been trying to telephone someone else, and the two calls had somehow become connected.

“What, man?” he asked me.

“Who is this—Kannadasan?” I asked.

“Yes,” he said.

“How did you get onto this phone?” I asked.

“I phoned someone else,” he said.

“Never mind. At least let us talk on the phone,” I said, and we spoke for ten or fifteen minutes—at a time, ten years ago, when hostility between us was intense.

Then he asked me: “You read in the newspapers everything I say in my speeches. What do you feel?”

I told him: “You scold very well. Since you scold in Tamil—and that too in sweet Tamil—I enjoy it. I do not feel hurt by it.”

## [06:00–11:20] Tamil art, Anna, Kalaivanar, Ilangovan and the movement’s artists

Our Tamil has the power to transform even such hostility; Tamil art has that power. It was because Perarignar Anna understood the greatness and value of that art that he undertook the task of using art to carry good ideas to the people.

Who was the great figure of the art world who served as an impetus for Anna to undertake that task? It was Kalaivanar N. S. Krishnan, whose portrait is displayed today in this great assembly hall.

As Lakshiya Nadigar Rajendran pointed out, even before Anna began taking revolutionary and innovative ideas, Self-Respect Movement ideas, and ideas needed by Tamil society into cinema and theatre, Kalaivanar N. S. K. had already been presenting such ideas.

The late great writer, Ilangovan—Thanigachalam—who remains worthy of honour and praise even today, turned the Kannagi epic into a cinematic painting. Anna wrote a long article about that film in the journal *Dravida Nadu*, which he conducted. The title of that article was *Injippaththare Mel*.

What was the purpose and opening of that article? Anna first praised Ilangovan in terms such as: “Ilangovan is a powerful writer. Tamil dances in his pen. Ilangovan has created a new era in dialogue writing.”

Then Anna asked: “But did Ilangovan have to write *Silappathikaram* in the old hereditary-story manner, in the manner of mythology? Is it not a work of literature? Is it not the history of Tamil Nadu? Is it not a work that explains the distinction of the Chera, Chola and Pandya kings? Did such a great epic have to be given a mythological finish and ruined?”

Then, when speaking of Kalaivanar’s role in the film, Anna said that Kalaivanar N. S. K., appearing as Injippaththan, repeatedly buys gold, turns it into brass, gives the brass away saying that it is gold, steals the gold, and cheats others by giving them brass while calling it gold.

“What does this show?” Anna asked. Through his acting, Kalaivanar N. S. K. had shown that Ilangovan had hidden the gold called *Silappathikaram*, polished up brass, and handed that to us instead. Therefore, Anna had written that in the story of *Silappathikaram*, this Injippaththar was better than Vanjippaththan.

Kalaivanar possessed the skill of giving political, economic and social ideas to the people the way medicine is mixed into honey and given to someone.

After Kalaivanar came K. R. Ramasamy, a scholar of acting and music; Nadigar Vel M. R. Radha, who could present Periyar’s ideas in revolutionary form; the Muthamizh Kala Vidwarathna T. K. S. brothers, who brought social-reform dramas to the country and won distinction; our Lakshiya Nadigar Rajendran, who, from within their company, fought for the need for still more revolutionary stories; Nadigamani T. V. Narayanasamy; and my friend Sivaji Ganesan, who first gave a lion’s roar in the conviction that revolutionary ideas would gain strength only when spoken by this tongue.

All of them belonged to that line of people who made the ideas of Periyar and Perarignar Anna echo on theatre stages and in cinema halls.

When Anna called, they would run to him at once. They had such respect for Anna that, without even asking, “What play? What role?”, they were ready to say whatever he asked them to say. They were not people who, even after Anna called them and even after their participation had been advertised, ran away because they feared what would happen to their profession if they acted.

## [11:20–14:15] Kannadasan, Krishna and the Gopalapuram house

I have so many memories, so many fresh thoughts.

The great people of the art world seated here are here to carry the movement’s ideas through art. Today poet Kannadasan may belong to another movement, or he may be speaking for another policy. He may recommend that I praise Krishna. But I do not think Sri Krishna’s condition is quite so pitiable.

Nor do I understand Paramatma Krishna to be in such a pitiable state that Kannadasan must recommend to me, “You accept Krishna.”

He himself said: “I have built my house near the Krishna Paramatma temple.” Is that not enough for our opponents?

I did not build a house. I bought an existing house twenty years ago.

Because if I say I built a house, tomorrow the newspapers will write: “Did you know? Karunanidhi’s friend Kannadasan has exposed him! Karunanidhi built a house in Gopalapuram only after becoming Chief Minister. This is information revealed by Kannadasan himself. The mystery is exposed!” They would publish even that.

> **Translator note:** The frozen Tamil mock-headline uses the unusual phrase `குட்டை உடைத்து விட்டார்`. E1 renders its rhetorical function as “has exposed him” rather than silently substituting a different Tamil idiom.

That house in Gopalapuram was bought before I ever imagined that we might become ministers—before I even imagined that I might become a Member of the Legislative Assembly.

Our director Panju knows this very well. Gopalapuram was a large *agraharam*. The man who came forward to sell me the house was a retired very senior officer of the Postal Department—an elderly, seasoned man, what the source calls a “ripe fruit.”

When I tried to buy that house, all the people in the *agraharam* asked: “Karunanidhi? To him? Is he going to come and live on our street?” They had never seen me until then.

Later they said they would decide whether the house could be sold after seeing me. So the old gentleman cleverly invited me to his house in the evening, as though to give me a feast, so that all the others could see me. I did not even have the build I have now.

After seeing me they said: “We were so frightened by the name; but when we see the man, he looks mild enough!”

Only after all the people in that *agraharam* agreed did I buy that house there, twenty years ago.

## [14:15–17:15] Sivaji, benefit performances, and art in politics

Why am I telling you all this? From the time when we tried to carry our ideas through the art world down to the present, even though many of us gained no benefit from it, and even though many today no longer remain in the same place or hold to the same policy, the policy itself still remains, does it not?

Even if those who worked for it are elsewhere now, I am in a position to thank those who worked for it. That is why I mention this.

Sivaji acted with me in so many plays. One could say it that way then. If today I were to say, “He acted with me,” it would sound amusing. In today’s circumstances it would be more appropriate to say, “I acted with Sivaji.”

But in those days, plays were staged on the billing “Karunanidhi and Sivaji Ganesan acting.” In those plays our friend Sivaji was an actor who came and performed without taking payment.

For cyclone-relief funds, flood-relief funds, drought-relief funds, Kazhagam election funds, and such causes, Sivaji acted in plays together with me. Our Lakshiya Nadigar, T. V. N., K. R. Ramasamy, and often the poet too, took on roles.

All of us worked to expand the art world, but we were not people who thought that art itself should be turned into politics.

Politics needs art. N. S. K. made that the aim of his life; Anna did too. Art is indeed needed to carry political ideas.

Why do we say that, rather than state everything baldly, there should be beauty in words? Why do we say there should be beauty in speech? Why do we say there should be beauty in writing?

Only when there is flavour in expression does it enter another person’s heart. That flavour is what we call art.

Writing beautifully is art. If speech is delivered in a way that attracts others, there is art there. In the same way, art is needed to carry political ideas.

Politics needs art; but art itself is not politics.

Women need jewellery; but jewellery itself is not women.

## [17:15–21:04] The Tirukkural, children, and what remains after a person

Therefore, as Sivaji pointed out, art may be used for good purposes, and it may also be used for evil purposes. But only art used for a good purpose can truly be art. That art must live; that art must be nurtured. The late great genius and towering figure of the art world, Kalaivanar N. S. K., held to that noble aim.

“Whether one is worthy or unworthy is known by what remains after them”—that is the Tirukkural.

> **Translator note:** The frozen Tamil quotation begins `தற்கார் தகவிலர்`, an unusual source form. The English gives the contextual sense argued by the speaker while preserving the exact frozen Tamil here rather than silently normalizing it.

Valluvar said that whether a person is good or not good, worthy or unworthy, can be judged by what remains behind after that person.

Some scholars say that a person may be judged by his children. I cannot accept that. If whether a person is good or bad is to be judged by his children, that does not appear to accord with our own experience.

So I believe Valluvar certainly would not have said that a person must be judged by his children.

What remains is not the children. What is it that remains after them?

Eighteen years after Kalaivanar’s death, what is it that still remains? His fame; the ideas he fashioned; the thoughts he gave this country; the Self-Respect ideas he gathered and gave; the artistic work he performed in those days for the abolition of caste; the compassion he had for helping the poor and the humble.

When we consider that these are the things that remain even after eighteen years, and will continue to remain for many more years, the line “whether one is worthy or unworthy is known by what remains after them” cannot mean “known by one’s children.”

Even if we speak according to what believers in the Puranas believe, you cannot judge Hiranya by Prahlada. Prahlada’s principles are different; Hiranya’s principles are different. You cannot say that because Prahlada is one way, Hiranya must be the same.

Whether we speak in the terms believed by Puranic believers or in the terms believed by politicians, you cannot judge Mahatma Gandhi by Abdullah Gandhi.

So “known by what remains” does not mean “known by one’s children, by the offspring one has produced.”

Valluvar would not have written with that intention. People who came later—commentators who came later—may have wished to insert that idea into people’s minds.

What remains means the fame that remains after them; the wealth they leave behind; intellectual wealth; the fame they accumulate and leave behind; the ideas and clusters of thought they give to the people of the country. These are what establish a person’s worth today.

Viewed that way, after Kalaivanar, our gathering here to praise him and honour him, and our recounting how his rare ideas were expressed through humour—these too are things he left behind.

## [21:04–22:52] Avadi, *Vilaangu Manithan*, and the 1947 radio episode

As an example of the kinds of people found even in politics, he was invited to the Congress conference held at Avadi. At that enormous conference at Avadi, Kalaivanar was invited to stage a play.

There he performed a play called *Vilaangu Manithan*.

A *vilaangu*, he explained, is a kind of aquatic creature that shows its head to the snake and its tail to the fish.

In the play titled *Vilaangu Manithan*, Kalaivanar appears as a hotel-keeper. If he sees that people in khadi shirts are coming, he takes out a picture of Gandhi or Kamaraj and puts it up. Once they have eaten and left, if he sees people in black shirts coming, he takes out a picture of Periyar or Anna and puts that up instead.

Through that play, even at a Congress Party conference, he showed the world that such *vilaangu* men exist in this country.

As Lakshiya Nadigar pointed out, when the Indian subcontinent obtained independence in 1947, when the flag of freedom flew brilliantly and rose to touch the clouds, the radio authorities invited Kalaivanar and asked him to sing a song of independence.

Kalaivanar said: “I will sing only if you allow me to sing Periyar’s name too.”

It is that courage, that boldness, that sense of self-respect that is praised today.

## [22:52–24:46] Political friendships, election campaigns, affection and laughter

Nadigar Thilagam Sivaji made a very beautiful point. He said that if actors and artists enjoy respect today, the pioneer who rose like a morning star and made that respect possible was Kalaivanar.

People from every party praised Kalaivanar: Jeevanandam of the Communist Party; Silambu Selvar M. Po. Si. of the Tamil Arasu Kazhagam; leaders of the Congress Party; leaders of many different parties.

Yet Kalaivanar’s own thinking was this: even if he was personally friendly with others, when the moment came for victories to be won, those victories should be victories for the Dravidian movement.

That is why, at election time, he wanted Anna to win, Lakshiya Nadigar Rajendran to win, me to win, and Kazhagam comrades to win. There was no place he did not travel to for that purpose; there was no place where his *villuppaattu* did not take place.

When Navalar lost his chance of victory in Salem even after Kalaivanar had campaigned for him, Kalaivanar N. S. K. wept and wailed.

That was how much family affection Kalaivanar had stored within this movement.

He treated suffering as play. He would utter the maxim that a human being must laugh. He would say that laughter is good.

Even in our own experience, when anger comes, we feel the blood constricting and we see our body heat up. At the same time, when we praise others, when we praise them happily, when we praise them wholeheartedly, we ourselves feel our heart expanding and our body seeming cool.

Thus Kalaivanar N. S. K. was also a good physician who told the country that laughter is a great medicine.

## [24:46–26:22.080] The card game, Kalaivanar’s circle of friends, and farewell

One incident comes to mind.

Kalaivanar, I, our friend the poet Kannadasan, and one other person would sometimes sit together and play cards for recreation. There was not a single day on which Kannadasan could be said to have won.

That DMK song which M. S. R. mentions—a song written for Kalaivanar—was indeed written by the poet. He would take the money from Kalaivanar for writing the song, gamble with it right there, and hand it back to him before leaving.

At first Kalaivanar would give him one hundred rupees. Kannadasan would take it, sit down and play, and lose either to me or to Kalaivanar. When he had no money left, Kalaivanar would say, “No money? Get up, man!” and Kannadasan would get up and sit on a chair nearby.

We would all keep playing. Kannadasan’s mouth would begin to hum:

“Where shall I search for money? Where shall I search?”

Whether there was any proper *raga*, *shruti*, or *tala* did not matter to the poet; he would simply keep singing.

“All right, come on then,” Kalaivanar would say, and give him another hundred rupees. Kannadasan would come back, sit down and play again.

After two rounds or perhaps three, once again the song would begin: “Where shall I search...?”

Thus Kalaivanar would keep friends gathered around him. He created an atmosphere in which he could not remain alone without ten or so friends around him, and he was the great man who nurtured a large family of artists.

Saying, “Long live the fame of that Kalaivanar! Long live! Long live!”, I take my leave.

Thank you. Vanakkam.
