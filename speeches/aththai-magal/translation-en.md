### PDF page 19 — printed page 19

#### The Aunt's Daughter

Beloved leaders! Comrades! Elders! Mothers!

First of all, I convey my greetings, mingled with gratitude, for the opportunity I have had to take part in this great public meeting of the Madhavaram Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam, and to the friends who gave me that good opportunity.

My friend Thennarasu, who spoke before me, said that he was eager to know what reply I was going to give to Nehru for saying that all of us were madmen. Nehru did indeed dare to call us madmen. True! But what reply am I going to give? All of you saw that, just a little while ago, a Petromax lamp hanging in the pavilion—

### PDF page 20 — printed page 20

—suddenly began to shine far too brightly. That lamp created fear and agitation that it might set the whole pavilion on fire. But after a little while its light diminished, and now you can see it hanging there. The Light of Asia shines today just like this lamp! Other than this, I myself do not know what other reply I could give—briefly, tastefully and with dignity.

These days Nehru frequently makes triumphal tours through Dravida. Every time he comes in that fashion, he abuses us in one way or another! Why? Nehru, who once never even thought of the South—who never thought of turning to look at Dravida—why has he now begun to parade here so often?

When Mother Nature performed a terrible dance in the form of a cyclone in the South, he did not come! When deeply painful events took place in the Thanjavur land and Tiruchirappalli district, when the pitiful condition of thousands being forced to beg had spread, when many were staggering and struggling after losing their homes, cattle and carts, jobs, and even lakhs of rupees—he did not come!

But he did come to one single region. He came to Travancore, one of the beautiful lands of the world, prosperous and undiminished in wealth! He heard the song of Kerala!

### PDF page 21 — printed page 21

He was satisfied! He returned! Forgetting the places filled with distress, he went away enjoying nature's beauty!

Why, then, does such a Nehru now make these frequent “triumphal tours”? What are the basic reasons contained in this? It is neither a difficult question nor an incomprehensible riddle! What other answer can there be except that the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam has made Nehru turn and look back?

When Dravida was a forest of suffering, a land of wailing, trapped and tormented in a storm of poverty, Nehru could not be seen. Yet today, all of a sudden, he visits again and again with a great commotion! We see it! There must surely be a good reason for it.

We are engaged in the great task of recovering the homeland. We have plunged into the effort to break the chains of slavery of Mother Dravida! It is precisely in order to destroy and crush our hold on that ideal—this question of our very life—that the hero of Allahabad, the Light of Asia, comes here so often. While he conducts affairs of state, while he discusses education with Abul Kalam Azad, while he argues with an opposition-party man, while he relaxes happily with his family, and while he is immersed in the political question, “What does Pakistan say about American military aid?”—

### PDF page 22 — printed page 22

—and while he is caught up in the train of thought, “What is America's outlook? What is Russia's line of thought?”—indeed, while Nehru reads, sleeps, eats and strolls, what has been placed before him as his thought and his dream is Dravidian consciousness! Our uprising! That is why he comes so often; that is why he speaks about us!

In earlier days Nehru was afraid even to call this the South, to say “Dravida,” or to mention the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam, out of the vain thought that we might thereby gain publicity. But today, wherever he goes, he breaks into derision—his mind reels—he hurls words that should not be uttered—because he sees that the feeling for separation has taken full form in the South! He watches to see where that uprising for rights—that war of liberation—will end. But we, to safeguard the ideal of the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam, wait to see whether Anna's pointing finger will move—whether he will give even a sign with his eyes—whether he will command, “Younger brothers, set out!”

A student comrade who spoke before me said that I should speak more about students. The Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam has close ties not only with students, but with the people of the great Dravidian community, with heroic mothers, and with labourers who toil and suffer. Therefore—

### PDF page 23 — printed page 23

—with feeling, with a sense of right, and with a character filled with firmness, it grows and marches toward the happy future conclusion: “We shall surely fulfil the ideal.”

Elders! All that the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam asks of you is that you turn back a few pages of your ancient history! When you were born, the white man ruled our land of happiness. For years and years you lived as slaves. You suffered under the rule of an unwanted foreigner. Not only you; those before you—those who gave birth to you—lay bound by Aryan illusion. But I ask you to think for a moment. How did the Dravidians before them live? In the Dravida ruled by the Chera, Chola and Pandya kings—in free “Thiruvidam”—they lived with rights. But what is the condition in which we, who came in their lineage, live today? We wander as slaves—as servile dependants—as insignificant insects. Why this condition? Whoever it may be comes here—says whatever he pleases—unleashes repression too—why this degraded state?

Elders! Yours is an age nearing death. Old age, after youth has passed! You have married off your son—seen his comfortable life—and held your charming grandson in your arms! There may even be a few among you who have had the fortune of seeing that grandson married too—

—perhaps. From now on, only the earth belongs to you.

You may live only another five or six years—at most ten or fifteen. What service are you going to render in that short span of life? You were born in a land of slavery—should you not die in a free land? Should not the soil in which your corpse is buried be sacred soil—free soil? This is what the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam asks you, looking at your faces thick with the lines of experience!

Mothers! Even though we have plunged with speed into the struggle for liberation, the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam has not forgotten you. Can we forget you, who come in the lineage of heroic women who smiled when war was announced and sent bull-like sons to the battlefield by the lakhs? Is it even possible to forget you?

On the first day of the war, the father; on the next day, the wedded husband; then the only brother born with her—thus the members of the family were submerged in a rain of blood. The sole treasure left is the beloved son—the twelve-year-old boy—the male child of the family! Even then the war-drum sounds from the battlefield! The mother's heart does not falter. The sense of duty that one person from every family must go to the field drives her onward—

### PDF page 25 — printed page 25

—she calls to her side that boy from whom the smell of milk has not yet faded. She places a spear in his hand and sends him off saying, “Go toward the face of battle.” The Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam asks you, who come from such a community of mothers—from the lineage of mothers praised in the Purananuru—to give, without holding back, one hero from every home to the liberation struggle!

And you too, Dravidian worker-comrades! Working and working until your bodies are worn down, turning blood into sweat, toiling labouring class! The Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam looks to you too! It asks: your body aching, your heart sorrowing, you labour until you perish—what reward has this country given you? What value has it given your labour? Is it not merely half a stomach of gruel and a life without consolation? Think, people of honest hearts! People of the great Dravidian community! Should not your labour bring joy? Should not your life blossom? That is why the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam calls to you: “Dravidian workers! Unite! In rivalry with the voice of Red China—in rivalry with the voice of Russia—not in tone, but in principle—let us raise a mighty voice! Come, come!”

Student comrades! You to whom the future belongs!

### PDF page 26 — printed page 26

The Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam calls you too. How? By telling you to leap into the field? No! Watch carefully—but do not merge into it completely! Why? Yours is the age for study. It is the time to mature.

Not only the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam; you may think about other parties too, their practical actions and political events; you may observe them; and not only that—you must think about them, and think while standing a little apart! For your studies must not be ruined! They must not become water poured upon weeds.

A country's liberation struggle does not need only an army of heroes. It also needs an army of discerning people. The good work of recovering the country belongs to the army of heroes; those who must safeguard the country once gained are the army of the discerning. In Dravida today, the army of heroes stands in formation! Therefore, student comrades, you must grow into the future army of the discerning.

It was the Congress that conducted the freedom struggle. It had an army of heroes. But it had no army of discerning people. That is why it now suffers, unable to safeguard the freedom it obtained. That is why it places as finance ministers a Deshmukh who was not in the Congress, an R. K. Shanmukham who stood against the Congress, and John Mathai. Should our movement too become—

### PDF page 27 — printed page 27

—a useless crowd without such discerning people, a fallen fortress? It must not, comrades! It must not!

If Dravida is recovered, can we simply decide, “This man has spoken at many meetings; let us send him as Dravida's ambassador to China”? We certainly cannot. If someone is to be an ambassador of a country, should he not have the particular qualifications and ability required for it? Therefore an army is needed for that too! That is the army of the discerning! Come as that army; grow together with the movement's forces!

For students, politics is an unripe fruit! A maiden who has not yet come of age!

An unripe fruit may look good; but if eaten, it will either be sour or bitter! The proper course—the path that yields benefit—is to eat it only when it has matured, when it is fully ripe.

So too with a maiden who has not yet come of age! She may have grown with a fresh appearance pleasing to the eye! She may even still be an aunt's daughter! With the right of saying “aunt's daughter,” one may circle around her and talk to her! But she must not be touched!

Students must understand this truth. The Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam does not expect shields from you! Nor is it waiting for swords from you—

### PDF page 28 — printed page 28

—it does not ask you to lift and hand over guns either. What it expects from you is only future discernment and the quality of growing into wise people capable of protecting the country.

That is why, student comrades, the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam asks you: confine yourselves to observing politics—but do not remain without observing it either!

In this way the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam casts its ideas and ideals before elders, mothers, worker-comrades and student friends alike!

Madhavaram is only a small town on the edge of Chennai! Even so, I am delighted to see such a vast gathering here, your smiling faces, and the new radiance that plays across them.

We are gathered here at a time when we are conducting a three-pronged agitation against Congress rule gripped by imperialist frenzy and wearing the garland of victory. It is a time when we raise high the cry of rights to behold the dawn of the future Dravida!
