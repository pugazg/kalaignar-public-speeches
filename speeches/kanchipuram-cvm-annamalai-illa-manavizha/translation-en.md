## Kanchipuram - C.V.M. Annamalai House Wedding Function

With the warm good wishes of all of us, the wedding ceremony has now taken place here between young Seervendhan, grandson of Senthamizh Selvar C.V.M. Annamalai and younger son of Kamalakkannan and Manimekalai, and Kokila, daughter of Sabapathi and Usharani of the Kothila Theatre in Tiruttani.

Everyone who spoke at this function expressed their views with the feeling that this was a function in our own family. One of the families with a long tradition in the Dravidian movement is the family of C.V.M. Annamalai. From Annamalai's time until today, this family has continued along the same path he left behind, the path shown by Thanthai Periyar and Perarignar Anna. That is something to be proud of, but not something to be surprised by.

## PDF 409 / printed p.408

For C.V.M. Annamalai was a man who laboured tirelessly with the conviction that not merely his own household, but this entire region — indeed, Tamil land itself — should be made a place where Self-Respect flourished. The period in which I moved closely with him was a period of pride, a period that can always be remembered with happiness, a period that forever casts its shadow across my heart.

Annamalai was so close to Perarignar Anna that, whatever the function in Kanchipuram, Anna would say, “Call Annamalai.” As our younger brother Parimalam pointed out here, saying that he himself grew up on Annamalai's lap, it was not Parimalam alone. No one can deny that the rationalist movement shaped by Arignar Anna, the Self-Respect movement founded by Thanthai Periyar, and the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam founded by Anna all crawled and grew on C.V.M. Annamalai's lap.

Among the backward — indeed, the most backward — communities, C.V.M. Annamalai shone as a bright flame. Many rises and falls came in his life. But he regarded both alike and reflected on them with equanimity; whether there was advancement

## PDF 410 / printed p.409

that brought great joy or decline that could have brought sorrow, he treated both alike and worked for this movement. Senthamizh Selvar C.V.M. Annamalai was such a man of awakening.

It is only natural that all of us should participate in a function taking place in his family. It is not only this function; I have attended many functions in this family. To put it another way, all of you in the Kanchi region know well that I have been connected with this family to the extent that hardly any function takes place in C.V.M. Annamalai's house without me.

I wish to share with you some memories that still remain vivid in my mind. The Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam was founded by Anna in 1949. When its first State Conference was held in Chennai, Anna said that funds had to be raised for the expenses of that conference and entrusted that task to me. At that time I made a request to the party organisations: if I was to come and speak at a meeting in your town, you should contribute a substantial amount as a donation to the party's State Conference in Chennai. If you ask how much that “substantial amount” was in 1952, it was one hundred rupees,

## PDF 411 / printed p.410

two hundred rupees, and at most five hundred rupees. At a meeting held in Kanchipuram in more recent times, district party secretary Cantonment Shanmugam gave five lakh rupees on behalf of this district. In periods when we raised funds, even fifty lakh rupees has been given. But in those days the maximum was five hundred rupees. For a flag-hoisting function, the contribution would be fixed at fifty rupees or a hundred rupees. In that way they would conduct meetings throughout this district, night after night until dawn, invite me, organise them very well, and after I spoke at seven or eight meetings we would collect one thousand or two thousand rupees. C.V.M. and I would then take whatever had been collected and travel by car to Chennai.

The conference was at the S.I.A. grounds in Chennai. Work for the conference, including the erection of the pandal, would be going on. Anna and the party's frontline leaders would be sitting on the sand piled there. When we reached there with this money at two or three in the morning, they would be waiting eagerly for us — not for us, but to find out how much money we had brought for the conference expenses.

C.V.M. was one of those who had the distinction of lifting up and nurturing the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam in such a period. At the wedding function taking place in his house,

## PDF 412 / printed p.411

I was delighted to see in this invitation how many academic degrees the bridegroom Seervendhan has obtained.

Fifty years ago, it would not have been possible for a young man or woman from this community to obtain such degrees. But you all know that, because the Dravidian movement arose and because of the efforts made by its leaders, reservation has to this day remained a great boon for the suppressed and oppressed.

That is why our friend and former Member of Parliament Krishnasamy, even though he was in the Congress movement, could not deny the importance of reservation. Recognising it, he spoke here in appreciation. When people of this community raised their voice saying, “It is not enough for us merely to be classified as backward; only if we are declared most backward will this community receive greater benefit,” that voice may for a time have gone unheard. But when I assumed office for the third time in 1989, even when that voice was not being raised, I took the initiative and announced that twenty per cent would henceforth be provided for the Most Backward communities. Krishnasamy referred to that here with a deep sense of gratitude.

## PDF 413 / printed p.412

I did not do this expecting the gratitude of this community. Indeed, I myself belong to a Most Backward community. I do not deny that, in the present system of different reservations for the Scheduled and oppressed, the Most Backward, the Backward, and the forward communities — intended both to ensure that Most Backward people receive this benefit and that society as a whole benefits — certain adverse effects sometimes arise.

When medicine is given to cure an illness, there can be what is called a “Side Effect” — a side effect. Some other harmful effects can result from it. Even a good medicine can produce certain side effects in the body. In the same way, because of reservation, caste differences and caste divisions have today raised their heads in the country. But one cannot refuse to give a good medicine to cure the disease for that reason. One cannot neglect the disease and withhold good medicine merely because this medicine may have a side effect. If a side effect occurs, we must think about how to face it, how to deal with it, and still give the good medicine.

## PDF 414 / printed p.413

That is why reservation is the rare medicine discovered by the Dravidian movement even before 1920. And if there is one town in Tamil Nadu that holds a historic place in reservation — in communal representation — it is Kanchipuram.

It was here in Kanchipuram, at the Madras Presidency Conference of the Congress Party held in 1925 under the leadership of Tamil Thendral Thiru.Vi.Ka., that Thanthai Periyar, who was then the Congress Party's southern commander, insisted that the communal representation introduced by the Justice Party must be accepted, and that a resolution must be adopted accepting that reservation scheme. But at that conference held in Kanchipuram, the resolution was not accepted. Thanthai Periyar immediately shook out his towel, placed it on his shoulder and declared: “I am leaving the Congress movement. I will not remain in a party that gives no place to social justice — to communal representation.” He took that vow and walked out here in Kanchipuram. We must not forget that.

Therefore, it is no surprise that in Kanchipuram, which has such an important and historic place in the history of reservation, commanding leaders such as C.V.M. Annamalai should arise. That is why C.V.M. Annamalai arose here, and following him

## PDF 415 / printed p.414

many frontline workers are serving in the Dravidian movement today.

You heard the vote of thanks delivered by our younger brother Ponmozhi, who works with C.V.M.'s principles firmly fixed in his heart. You all saw the firmness of principle contained in that speech. In such a family, Seervendhan and Kokila stand before us today as bridegroom and bride.

The bridegroom's name is Seervendhan. He should indeed remain “Seervendhan”; he should not become a vendhan — a king — who asks for excessive *seer*, wedding gifts. (Loud applause) It is because of such demands that quarrels arise in many families and all kinds of things happen. Giving no room for any of that, I ask that Seervendhan live up to his name as a well-ordered vendhan; and merely because the father-in-law's house has earned a reputation for generosity, he should not expect excessive *seer-varisai* but should lead a balanced life. With that request to Seervendhan and Kokila, I take my leave. Long live the newlyweds.
