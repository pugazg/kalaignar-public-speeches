### Speech at Kavikko Abdul Raguman's Sixtieth-Birthday Celebration

I take great pride and immense delight in joining this pleasant sixtieth-birthday celebration that has been under way since this morning, and in congratulating and praising poet Abdul Raguman. Three books have been released. A special souvenir about the poet has also been published in connection with the sixtieth-birthday celebration. A trust has also been started in the poet's name.

At a time when we reflect on the fact that, on behalf of the present government, the work begun by governments of the past but left without continuation by administrations that came in between is now being carried out extensively and rapidly, with enthusiasm and concern, in order to develop Tamil further on Tamil soil, poet Abdul Raguman's sixtieth-birthday celebration is taking place. I have joined this celebration in the confidence that the enthusiasm and encouragement this function gives will certainly

## PDF 229 / printed p.228

reach this government, which is working to develop Tamil further and to give Tamil its rightful place.

In the development of Tamil, our concern in the situation before us is that our Tamil people hesitate even to write in Tamil on signboards. At such a grand function this may seem like an ordinary matter. But this is the big matter: a Tamil hesitates even to write the name of his business establishment in Tamil. Some people have a doubt. If the name of a business establishment is suddenly changed, will all the publicity it has already gained not diminish? Will those who had earlier dealt with that establishment not find themselves unable to make contact because the name has changed? Some people have this doubt.

For example, suppose a commercial establishment bears a name in Sanskrit. If that Sanskrit name is changed and the commercial establishment is made Tamil, how will others contact it? How will they remember the establishment? It is natural that such a reasonable doubt should arise. That is why I have already said this: if the name of a commercial establishment is “Jayalakshmi Silk House,” there is no need to change the name Jayalakshmi. It may be changed to “Jayalakshmi Cloth Shop” or “Jayalakshmi Silk Mansion” and the name may be displayed that way.

The place where we have gathered here is Rajaji Mandapam. Yet even though the organising committee includes Tamil scholars and Tamil

## PDF 230 / printed p.229

people, instead of printing on the invitation that this function is being held at Rajaji Mandapam, they have printed that it will be held at Rajaji Hall. I do not say this in order to find fault. I say it so that others may correct themselves in future. If we say Rajaji Mandapam, everyone will understand. For many years that is how we have printed the name at functions we conduct. Rajaji's name need not change. It is enough if “Hall” becomes “Mandapam.” That is the basic objective of Tamil development today.

I want to tell you an amusing incident. When Rajaji was alive, Murasoli used to print his name as “Rasasi.” One day, in a letter Rajaji wrote to me in his own hand, he said: “Dear Karunanidhi, in your Murasoli newspaper you print my name as Rasasi. But when you print M.G.R. and Rajendran, the Sanskrit ‘ji’ is there; what sin have I alone committed?” I immediately telephoned Murasoli and instructed them to print Rajaji's name henceforth only as Rajaji. I also wrote back to Rajaji, stating that from then on Murasoli would publish his name as Rajaji. He wrote back thanking me.

## PDF 231 / printed p.230

The reason I say this is not that if a person has a Sanskrit name it must somehow be changed. But where things can be changed, if “Hall,” “House,” and “Company” can be changed into Tamil, they should be changed. Because the poet who holds that feeling and who performs the incomparable task of instilling that feeling among Tamil people—who stands in the line of good Tamils, in the line of enthusiastic Tamils—is my dear friend Abdul Raguman, I consider it especially appropriate to say this at this function.

Three books were released here. Using the phrase that deeply moved me, “The One Who Fell Like a Seed,” a book has been published collecting poems Raguman wrote on many occasions. I have mentioned this even in the foreword I gave that book. When poet Raguman points to the passing of Arignar Anna, my heart swells with emotion. Even in that state, when I saw the phrase in which he said that Anna “fell like a seed,” I noted there that, for a moment, I forgot even that grief and sighed again and again, thinking of this wonderful image, this turn of phrase.

Such a rare book has been released, and Dr. Nannan presented its review here. While speaking,

## PDF 232 / printed p.231

he said that when a speaker speaks he must think of everyone in front of him as fools. At that moment the Professor, Raguman and I spoke among ourselves and consoled ourselves: fortunately, we are sitting behind him! (Loud laughter, applause.) What Nannan meant was that speakers themselves may even be fools, but while speaking, only if they think of those seated before them as less capable than themselves can they speak without fear. Nannan placed that point here, explained all the views with which he agreed, praised the poet greatly, delicately expressed one or two points, and concluded his address.

The person who reviewed the book published under the title “Pitthan” (“The Madman”) was Dr. K. Chellappan. I have known Chellappan for a long time. He has fine command of English. Many will remember that he translated my book “Kuraloviyam” into English and that it was published five or six years ago. Dr. Chellappan, a scholar with such command of English, has reviewed the book published under the title “Pitthan.”

## PDF 233 / printed p.232

Younger brother Vairamuthu—Kaviyarasu Vairamuthu—has renounced the title Kaviyarasu; and because, when I speak, there is greater sweetness in calling him “my younger brother” than in calling him Kaviyarasu, my younger brother Vairamuthu reviewed the poetry book written by our friend Raguman under the title “The Address of Muthamizh.” They say that “the address of Muthamizh” refers to me. Even if the address is correct now, because of the postal workers' strike, it does not reach properly. (Laughter.) In such a time, what does it matter whether there is an address or not? The poems he wrote praising me on many occasions and at many poetry gatherings have been published as a collection. On that, younger brother Vairamuthu delivered his emotion-filled address here.

Our Inamana Perasiriyar explained here all the ties Abdul Raguman has with this movement and with Tamil society, and praised his fine qualities and his simple Tamil.

I read these poetry books deeply. Some people who come to felicitation functions merely stroke the covers of the books. As for me, I

## PDF 234 / printed p.233

sit down for a day or two, read those books at leisure, and only then come. As Nannan said, I too have come after underlining a few lines. But I am not in a condition to speak for long. Abdul Raguman knows that. Since I have not been well from this morning, I have nevertheless come to this function with a determination that, whatever happens, I must take part in it.

I consider it a privilege to present to you a few lines of poetry from Raguman's book “Pitthan.”

The Madman is tearing his clothes. Seeing what he is doing, Raguman asks him:

“Why are you  
tearing your shirt?” I asked.  
Then the Madman says—  
“If one wants  
to read the body,  
must one not tear  
the covering?”  
he said. (Applause.)

Without my having to say it, you can understand how fine a philosophy is contained in this. The Madman continues.

## PDF 235 / printed p.234

“Flowers and birds  
do not wear clothes, do they?  
You  
became obscene;  
therefore you hid yourselves  
with clothes.”

Humans, it seems, became obscene—flowers and birds are without obscenity. That is why, it seems, they do not wear clothes. Having said this, he continues:

“Only stagnant water  
wears  
moss.”

Moss forms on stagnant water; through the Madman's mouth the poet calls that clothing.

“Only an extinguished fire  
wears  
ash.  
The snake knows  
that skin too is clothing;  
you do not.” (Applause.)

It seems the snake knows that even skin is clothing. This word “clothing”—as I mentioned first, stagnant water wears moss. In the same way, if milk is heated well and allowed to cool, a skin forms on top; in Tamil that too is called “adai,” a covering. Poet Abdul Raguman brings that “adai” and presses it onto stagnant water: it too wears moss.

## PDF 236 / printed p.235

If you read this entire poetry collection called “Pitthan,” the thought that occurs to me is that, as he himself says in the foreword, these are things stated with a resolve to say everything in the negative. They are said negatively within a negative affirmation. We say “positive” and “negative” in English; only when they are not separate but come together does light itself emerge. (Applause.) First the negative is produced. Even in photography, what is produced first is the negative; afterwards it becomes an image as a positive. (Applause.) If you read with that in mind, all of the Madman's poems appear in the positive itself—in that approach.

Under the title “Orphan”:

“Who is that  
orphan?”  
I asked.

The poet asks. The Madman replies:

“Truth,” he said.  
(The name of that orphan, it seems, is Truth.)  
“In the marketplace  
it was crying  
as an orphan.  
No one  
recognized it,”  
he said.

## PDF 237 / printed p.236

(Truth, it seems, was crying as an orphan in the marketplace. No one recognized it.)

“Why?” I asked.  
“On its forehead  
there was neither sacred ash nor a namam;  
on its chest  
there was no cross;  
on its head  
there was no cap.  
Therefore  
no one  
recognized it,”  
he said. (Applause.)

If they had recognized it, would not the Ayodhya problem arise? (Applause.) Therefore this is a negative approach—through a negative approach poet Abdul Raguman gives us a positive message, in order to establish that even if all these marks exist, everyone is one.

Look even now. Among the functions I have attended in this one week, the first was Thirunavukkarasu's golden-jubilee celebration; yesterday I attended our Christian Bishop Ezra Sargunam's sixtieth-birthday celebration; today the function I am attending is poet Abdul Raguman's sixtieth-birthday celebration. (Applause.) Hindu,

## PDF 238 / printed p.237

Christian, Muslim—since I am taking part in all three of these celebrations (applause), even though the identities are known, the very fact that I participate in all of them shows that all are one. Whether Hindu, Muslim or Christian, there is that song saying, “Hindu Muslim sikki saayi, aapas pe hai bhai, bhai” (laughter). To bring out the feeling contained in that song, poet Abdul Raguman gives us a positive message through a negative approach.

There is another title, “Pakkavaatham” (“Paralysis”). In it is a fine philosophy:

“You call  
one thing good;  
you call another bad.  
Is this not a blind judgment?”

the Madman asks. You call one thing good and another bad; is this not a blind judgment? he asks.

“Your judgment  
is in truth  
not a judgment,  
but a one-sided argument  
(‘one-side argument,’ he says. In Tamil, if ‘one-sided argument’ is run together, it becomes ‘pakkavaatham’—paralysis.)  
A blanket  
you call good  
in winter;  
in summer

## PDF 239 / printed p.238

you call it bad.  
Good and bad  
are not in the blanket.  
A fan  
you like  
in summer;  
in winter  
you dislike it.  
Like and dislike  
are not in the fan.

When rain falls,  
the farmer praises it;  
the potter curses it.  
There is no partiality  
in the rain.  
The line you draw  
to divide  
this as good  
and this as bad—  
is it not a line  
cut into running water?”

See what a wonderful image this is! Between the two words good and bad—between these two qualities—there is not a great gap. The poet wanted to say that it is a very small gap, a thread-thin interval. He did not call this interval a great trench; he did not call it a small channel; he did not call it the Kaveri, a river one must cross. Then what does he say? To divide good

## PDF 240 / printed p.239

and bad, is not the line you draw a line cut into running water? If you draw a line in running water, how long will it last? Even as you draw it, the two become one. He says that is how fine the interval between these two qualities, good and bad, really is.

There is another thing. Whatever we do not relish, the Madman relishes. Is it the Madman who relishes it? Under the name of the Madman, Abdul Raguman relishes it. He relishes “wounds.”

“I am enjoying  
the wounds.  
On the body of darkness  
these  
star-wounds—  
how beautiful  
they are,” he said.

(The conscience called the Madman speaks thus to Raguman.)

Here is another beautiful and fine thought, worthy of reflection and repeated appreciation:

“Only the palm leaf that is wounded  
becomes a manuscript (applause),  
wounded by the stylus.  
Only the stone that is wounded  
becomes a sculpture  
by the chisel.  
Only the bamboo that is wounded  
sings a melody.” (Applause.)

This is how he argues the case for wounds.

## PDF 241 / printed p.240

Next, “Burden.” Everyone dislikes a burden. If someone says, “Carry this burden and go,” irritation arises. But the Madman says:

“You say  
laying down a burden  
is joyful;  
but  
at death,  
why do you cry?” (Applause.)

We say life is a burden; we say the body is a load. Very well: you who call this a great burden, why do you cry the moment death comes? Is not the load then being set down?

Poet, after reading and reading the Madman's similes, poems and philosophies like this, I announce here in Rajaji Mandapam that I myself have become a madman of Abdul Raguman! (Loud applause.)

In the book about Anna titled “The One Who Fell Like a Seed,” in the poem titled “The Tears of a Blind Man”:

“What is buried here  
is not merely a human body,  
but our  
chest of history.  
We are blind.  
The treasure we received

## PDF 242 / printed p.241

we have buried again.  
We are blind.”

(Because we have buried again the treasure that came to us.)

Next, Nannan touched on and then left aside the phrase “Let us see God in the smile of the poor.” But what Raguman has written is:

“Anna, now  
I understand  
what you meant when you said,  
‘Let us see God  
in the smile of the poor.’  
You said it that way, did you not,  
because no one  
can see God? (Applause.)  
Even if  
we cannot see  
God,  
let it be so.  
Let us create a world  
without poor people.”

Thus he points to the policy, the ideal and the goal.

When he sings about Anna under the title “Anna Salai”:

“When we walked  
on the road,  
we removed and threw away  
the sandals  
that bit our feet.”

(Think politically about how deep the meaning is. When we walked on Anna Salai, it seems, we removed and threw away the sandals that bit our feet.)

“Some sandals  
whose straps had snapped  
slipped off by themselves  
and fell on the way.  
For publicity value,  
some commercial avenues  
and some commercial streets  
took on Anna's name.  
Does everyone  
who walks on Anna Salai  
walk in Anna's path? (Applause.)  
Anna,  
you said  
even the jasmine  
in another man's garden

## PDF 244 / printed p.243

has fragrance.  
But now some say  
only the jasmine  
in another man's garden  
has fragrance.”

(Anna said that even the jasmine in another man's garden has fragrance. But now some say that only the jasmine in another man's garden has fragrance.)

“They say the jasmine  
in our garden  
has no fragrance.  
Some others say  
even the other man's  
paper flower  
has fragrance.  
Some who grew because of you  
have grown beyond you.  
They say  
even the sewage  
at the other man's house  
smells sweet.  
They have begun  
to go and bathe in it.  
Still others

## PDF 245 / printed p.244

became intoxicated  
by the jasmine  
in the other man's garden  
and went there  
and settled down.” (Applause.)

All this anguish has been pointed out by Abdul Raguman in these heart-touching lines of poetry that praise Anna and mourn Anna.

For this pleasant function, the organisers had already sent me all the books. This afternoon they sent me the hardbound books and the souvenir, together with an article that appeared in Dinamani this morning. It was an article written by our friend, poet Meera. In it he has made a request.

He points out that, at one time, Bharathiyar sent to the Sudesamithran newspaper a poem written by revolutionary poet Bharathidasan, saying, “This is a poem by Bharathidasan, then known as Kanakasubburathinam, belonging to Sriman Subramania Bharati's Poetry Circle; publish it,” and that it was published in that newspaper. He then says that the Poetry Circle referred to by Bharathiyar no longer exists, and asks that, in connection with Abdul Raguman's sixtieth-birthday celebration, the Government of Tamil Nadu create that Poetry Circle.

## PDF 246 / printed p.245

I found one error in that too. Where does he say it should be created? He has written that it should be created on Mahabalipuram Road. It is not Mahabalipuram Road. It is Mamallapuram Road (applause), I tell younger brother Meera. Even if there is an error, since the idea is a good one, I proclaim at the sixtieth-birthday celebration of my dear friend Abdul Raguman that the Government of Tamil Nadu will undertake every possible effort to create the Poetry Circle (loud applause). I once again appreciate everyone who participated and spoke at this function, and thank them all.
