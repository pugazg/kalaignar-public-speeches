# We Are One Caste — the Tamil Caste! — English translation

Karunanidhi speaks at the Government Arts College in Villupuram and begins by insisting that a college function should remain an educational forum rather than become a partisan political meeting. Students, he says, are future builders of the country whether or not they ever hold office.

He reviews the expansion of educational opportunity in Tamil Nadu: the reservation struggles of earlier social-justice movements, Kamaraj's expansion of schools and free education, and the later extension of free education under the DMK government. Colleges, in his formulation, are training grounds for future intellectuals, leaders and social architects.

The speech then becomes autobiographical. Karunanidhi says he never had the opportunity to attend college because his political and Tamil commitments drew him away from formal study. But he warns students not to imitate such exceptions. Periyar, Kamaraj or Karunanidhi himself cannot be treated as models for neglecting education: **exceptions are not guides**. Formal learning and intelligence are distinct, but when they join, each strengthens the other.

He rejects belief in another birth as a reason for moral conduct and argues instead that the important question is not how many years one lives, but what one does for the country, society and language during the life one actually has. Bharati's short life is given as an example of enduring achievement.

Recalling Anna's Annamalai University speeches, Karunanidhi invokes the argument of Manonmaniam Sundaram Pillai that Indian history should be written from the Cauvery region rather than beginning only from the Ganga. This leads into a lament that students know too little of the history connected with the Cauvery, Karikala, Cheran Senguttuvan and the ancient Tamil dynasties.

The central warning is against caste division among students. Karunanidhi recalls the Cheranmahadevi Gurukulam controversy, caste-segregated water pots in Kumbakonam, and the struggles that removed such practices. He distinguishes reservation—needed to remedy historical disadvantage—from what he calls “reservation of the heart”: caste must not become an emotional wall between students.

He invokes Vallalar's criticism of caste and religion, Bharati's “சாதிகள் இல்லையடி பாப்பா,” and Tamil historical memory to ask students to see themselves not as mutually hostile caste groups but as **தமிழ்ச்சாதி**, one Tamil community.

Karikala's Kallanai, Cheran Senguttuvan's northern campaign, the Pandyan Nedunchezhiyan episode and the later Cholas are presented as shared cultural inheritance. Karunanidhi contrasts that history with what he sees as declining language and ethnic consciousness among students and rising caste consciousness.

He accepts reservation for backward and oppressed communities for as long as structural inequality persists, but argues that those communities should not fight among themselves over caste subdivisions. His satire of people supposedly born from different toes of the same divine foot underscores the absurdity of such internal hierarchy.

The closing appeal is to students and elders alike: reject caste and religious conflict, recover Tamil historical consciousness, cultivate rationalism, and create an egalitarian society. He recalls Bharathidasan's poem written for the Tamil Students' Association and asks whether that earlier spirit can again take root among students.

## Separate source closing note

Speech delivered at the Government Arts College, Villupuram, on **11 April 1982**.
