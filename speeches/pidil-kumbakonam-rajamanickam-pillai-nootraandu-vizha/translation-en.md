## Pidil Kumbakonam Rajamanickam Pillai Centenary Function

I take great pride and immense delight in participating in this special function, meeting all of you, and joining you all in honouring the fame of Kumbakonam fiddle maestro Rajamanickam Pillai.

I must say that taking part in this event gives me great *anandam* — joy. There is a reason for saying *anandam* rather than merely happiness. I see here, on this stage and before me, many people steeped in music, people who have fully understood the grammar of music, people who have created musical traditions, and people who even today continue to soak the hearts of Tamil people in the rain of their music. That is why I say that I feel *anandam*. In Semmangudi's language, one might say not merely *anandam* but even *brahmanandam* — supreme joy. With such

## PDF 417 / printed p.416

a feeling I stand before you.

We heard the speeches of Semmangudi, Pattammal Ammaiyar and Lalgudi Jayaraman. All of them studied music systematically, mastered its grammar and created many successors in their traditions. Listening to the speeches of such people — and especially to the speech of the elder Semmangudi — a thought came to me: he speaks of so many events from long ago; if only I had the time, if only I had the leisure, how wonderful it would be to sit with him for an entire day (Applause) and listen to and savour all these stories from the world of music! Where am I going to find leisure? Where am I going to find the time?

But I ask this of Semmangudi not for myself alone, but on behalf of everyone in Tamil Nadu. You spoke here of many such matters; similarly, at the function for the late great Nadaswaram Chakravarthi T.N. Rajarathinam Pillai, you spoke at length of many matters. In the same way, if you yourself were to publish as a book all the many honey-like stories still steeping and maturing within your heart (Loud applause), it would be of great use not only to the present

## PDF 418 / printed p.417

but also to the musical world of the future.

You may ask: “Where am I to write? What is my age? Can I write? Is my eyesight sound? Can my fingers write?” If you will speak, I am ready to appoint, on behalf of the Government itself, an assistant to listen and write it down. (Loud applause) I place this request before the elder Semmangudi because I have a deep desire to draw out such a great treasure from within him and give it to the world of music. I believe he will not refuse it. This is not a Government Order. It is a request from the Government — no, not even a Government request. I wish to tell him that it is Karunanidhi's appeal, Karunanidhi's plea.

It is only at functions like this that we are able to hear such matters from him. We all heard the many stories he scattered so generously in his speech about that elder, Kumbakonam violin Rajamanickam Pillai, who though departed has not disappeared.

The word *isai* does not mean music alone. It also means fame. Valluvar has said, “Give, and live so as to earn *isai*.” There, “living with *isai*” means living with fame. It is in that sense that *isai*

## PDF 419 / printed p.418

appears there. Kumbakonam Rajamanickam Pillai lived as one worthy of such *isai* — of fame — and, though he has passed away, continues to fill all our hearts.

The instrument he handled was the fiddle. We have heard a story about this fiddle: that when the city of Rome was burning, Nero was playing the fiddle. Only now do I understand it. Perhaps the saying that Nero played the fiddle while Rome burned arose to show that, however tragic an event may be, if one keeps playing the fiddle, the sorrow disappears!

In 1948, Kumbakonam Rajamanickam Pillai was given the title “Sangeetha Kalanidhi” by the Chennai Sangeetha Vidwat Sabha. Musicians gathered in many towns and held felicitation functions for him. In those days, at Tiruvarur, at the junction of South Street and East Street, the Isai Vellalar Sangam held a function to celebrate Rajamanickam Pillai receiving the Sangeetha Kalanidhi title. I still vividly remember standing there as a young man and speaking.

I said then: “It is not Rajamanickam Pillai who gains honour from this title; the Chennai Sangeetha Vidwat Sabha that gave him this title has carried away the honour for itself.”

## PDF 420 / printed p.419

There are many stories about him. Semmangudi mentioned here the Mysore violin vidwan Chowdiah.

Once in Karaikudi there were two functions — Rajamanickam Pillai had gone to one and Chowdiah to the other. Music enthusiasts in Karaikudi met both Rajamanickam Pillai and Chowdiah and asked them: “You must both play the fiddle on the same stage. We are ready to give a large prize to whoever wins.”

I wish to point out the reply Rajamanickam Pillai gave with his characteristic generosity. He said, “Ask whether he agrees.” When they went and asked Chowdiah, he said, “If Rajamanickam Pillai agrees, I too agree.” Rajamanickam Pillai then said: “Very well, I too agree. But on one condition. Collect fifteen thousand rupees — you yourselves can calculate what fifteen thousand rupees in those days would be worth today — give that bundle of money to an adjudicator and seat him on the stage. The two of us will play the violin. Whichever one of us loses, give that fifteen thousand rupees to him and send him back to his town. After he has gone home,

## PDF 421 / printed p.420

call back the one who wins and honour him every year.” He is said to have added: “But the one who loses after receiving the fifteen thousand rupees must never come to Karaikudi again. You must invite only the winner.”

What did that mean? The people of Karaikudi understood the meaning. It was his subtle way of saying: “The two of us are equals. Do not make one man the winner and the other the loser and thereby deprive the Karaikudi region of one man's music. So let us not have this competition.”

It was mentioned that the princely state of Travancore presented him with an elephant. He was also given the distinction of being an *Asthana Vidwan* of the Travancore court. When they asked him, “What gift do you want?” he asked for an elephant. After he received it, Muthiah Bhagavathar, who was close to him, looked at Rajamanickam Pillai and asked: “Man, have you no sense? Of all things, are you going to tie up an elephant and feed it?” Rajamanickam Pillai replied: “Did I ask for the elephant for myself? Did I not ask for it for the Kumbakonam Kumbeswarar Swami temple?”

## PDF 422 / printed p.421

He then handed the elephant over to the temple. That too is one of the notable episodes in his history.

I am not fully qualified to explain how supportive he was to music vidwans — to singers — how, as Pattammal Ammaiyar said, he stood beside them like a shadow. The music masters who have spoken here are the people fully qualified to speak of that.

Whether the accompaniment is a fiddle, a mridangam or a kanjira, and how an accompanist should cooperate with the singer — as for all that, I know only how to enjoy songs. Moopanar can understand and appreciate them far more than I can. I am not someone who can appreciate them with such technical understanding. In my youth, when I was sent to learn music, I refused for other reasons. At first I even disliked music. At musical functions such as this, I now experience feelings that make me regret why I disliked it then.

So, not to show how accompaniment should be, but to show how it should *not* be, Kalaivanar N.S.K. created a scene in a film.

In that film Kalaivanar N.S.K. plays a great Bhagavathar. He is invited to a concert,

## PDF 423 / printed p.422

but suddenly the mridangam player does not arrive. They search throughout the town and cannot find anyone. He says, “Catch hold of somebody and bring him.” They find a man walking along the road beating a *thappattam* drum and bring him in. “All right, seat him as the accompanist,” he says, and tells him: “I will sing. At least beat that drum properly in place of the mridangam.”

N.S.K. sings, “Rama... Nee... Sama.” But the new man does not know how to accompany even that properly; he beats, “Dangu — dangu — dangu.” Five or six times N.S.K. repeatedly sings it correctly for him, but the accompaniment does not cooperate.

Finally N.S.K. himself adjusts to the accompaniment and begins singing, “Rama... Nee... Sama, Rama... Nee... Sama,” to suit it. (Loud applause)

Why was that scene created? Not to show how accompaniment should be, but how it should not be. Kalaivanar N.S.K. created that scene in a film for that purpose. As an example of how accompaniment *should* be, and as a guide to others, stood the late elder Rajamanickam Pillai.

Though I did not have a very close personal association with him, I had opportunities to meet him many times. Once, Arignar Anna

## PDF 424 / printed p.423

and I went to Rajamanickam Pillai's house in Kudanthai and spent time speaking with him. Later, after I became a minister, I went to him once again to receive his good wishes and spoke with him.

In his family, among the lamps of his lineage, our younger brother Thiagu today shines as one such lamp, honouring the fame of his grandfather and shining brightly in the film world. Through his devoted efforts he has brought together here an assembly in which all these music vidwans have gathered. I too take part in this function, which adds to the fame of his beloved grandfather, Rajamanickam Pillai, who fills all our hearts, and I honour and praise him.
