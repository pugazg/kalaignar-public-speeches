## Ilango Adigal (1)

At today's Ilango Adigal function, I am grateful for the opportunity to release Silambu Chelvar's valuable book, *Was Kovalan Guilty?*

Kannagi had already asked the Pandyan court whether Kovalan was guilty, and the king himself ultimately accepted that he was not. So when I first received the book this evening I wondered why Silambu Chelvar was reopening the question. Once I began reading, my own conscience reminded me not to jump to conclusions.

I started the book at home and finished it while the other speakers were speaking here. They should not think I ignored them: I followed their speeches too and even spoke intermittently with Silambu Chelvar.

## PDF 156 / printed p.155

I have read the book closely enough, I say, to answer questions about its contents.

Silambu Chelvar notes in his preface that literary debates have spread even to small villages and that this shows a literary renaissance.

His question “Was Kovalan guilty?” is not treated as a re-trial before the Pandyan court. He does not simply speak for Kannagi, the king, or the goldsmith responsible for Kovalan's death. Instead he asks why Kovalan left Kannagi for Madhavi and examines Kovalan's artistic sensitivity, literary taste, linguistic knowledge and courage. He argues that Kovalan's relationship with Madhavi should not be reduced to crude lust or to the idea that he merely sought from another woman a pleasure denied by Kannagi.

## PDF 157 / printed p.156

The book also answers accusations based on the fact that Madhavi lived in a district associated with courtesans, or that Kovalan passed through streets associated with them in Madurai. Silambu Chelvar argues vigorously that these details do not justify branding Kovalan a libertine or saying he deceived Kavundhi Adigal.

I then explain why I felt such affection for this argument. Earlier that day, preparing to speak at the Ilango Adigal function, I had obtained another book from my friend Anantham: a volume of lectures by Thirikooda Sundaram under the title *Silappathikaram — Sornammal Memorial Lectures*. I finished that book and reached home at about a quarter to six, where my private secretary handed me *Was Kovalan Guilty?*

## PDF 158 / printed p.157

The earlier volume had disturbed me. I had been asking how a book could disparage Kovalan, Tamil history and Tamil society and yet be published as a Tamil publication of Annamalai University. Silambu Chelvar's book came to me almost as an answer to that distress.

Thirikooda Sundaram had argued that the prosperous people of Kaveripoompattinam spent their wealth in sensual indulgence and had treated their morality disparagingly. He also discussed Madhavi's rewards after her debut performance and treated the grant of 1,008 *kalanju* of gold as evidence of degradation.

## PDF 159 / printed p.158

He quoted Ilango's line saying that, under the prescribed rule, Madhavi received 1,008 *kalanju* and interpreted it as though the king were paying that price for her body rather than rewarding artistic performance.

I reject that hostile interpretation. The line can naturally mean that dancers were rewarded by an established convention and that Madhavi received the prescribed amount. To use it to degrade the Chola king, Madhavi and, by implication, Ilango himself is deeply unjust.

Everyone has a right to express an opinion. But when an interpretation demeans Ilango, Kannagi, Kovalan, Madhavi and the whole of *Silappathikaram*—

## PDF 160 / printed p.159

the great treasury through which we still see the Chera, Chola and Pandya worlds—it damages one of the richest surviving windows into Tamil history.

Silambu Chelvar's small book removes that anxiety. In places it appears to answer such hostile interpretations indirectly, gathering together Kovalan's virtues, civilisation, literary learning and artistic sensibility.

A. P. Nagarajan observed that we do not know Ilango Adigal's birthday or the day he died. I say Ilango's true birth was the day he completed *Silappathikaram*. One who remains immortal through such a work has no real day of death.

Nagarajan also reminded me that I had said at the previous year's function that an Ilango statue would be unveiled this year.

## PDF 161 / printed p.160

I reply that the statue has already been ready for seven days and that I have personally seen it. It is being made on what was once called Mount Road and is now Anna Salai, by the sculptor who made the Bharati statue. I shall show it to Silambu Chelvar soon. I do not intend to leave a promise unfulfilled, and I expect the statue can be unveiled within a month.

Likewise, at the lost city of Kaveripoompattinam, a foundation stone has been laid for a *Silappathikaram* art gallery. Arrangements are under way to carve the entire story of *Silappathikaram* in sculpture on its walls, and I am happy to say that the work is expected to reach completion within the year.
