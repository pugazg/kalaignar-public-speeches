## Freedom of Opinion

It is especially appropriate that this function is being held on Republic Day. During the three years from 1969 to 1972, forty-two books were published. When that is compared with the sixty-six books published in the two years since 1972, I am delighted to see how much more vigorous the work of the University has become.

On behalf not only of those gathered here but also of all who will benefit from these books, I congratulate the professors who have produced them.

At first we read many things in books; later we live long enough to read the world itself as a book. No one can deny the need for books such as these to serve as a bridge between those two stages.

Research works, histories, literature, works from other languages, and books that make known the greatness of Tamil Nadu are among the many kinds being released today.

## PDF 114 / printed p.113

Our Vice-Chancellor gave us the happy news that in these four years alone twenty-four books have been published by the Tamil Department. We can all take pride that twenty-four of the 108 books are from that department.

The happiness we express today will, I believe, encourage the Tamil Department to bring out still more books in future.

These books have generally been issued with freedom for their authors to express their views. Freedom of opinion is certainly necessary. Yet when we have cherished certain values for a long time and thereby helped create a new condition in the country, freedom should not become a means of losing what has been achieved; nor should facts be allowed to disappear.

Without pointing to any particular work, I would therefore welcome a process in which, before lectures delivered under various endowments are published as books, a University committee has an opportunity to read them, point out deficiencies to the authors, and enable appropriate corrections.

## PDF 115 / printed p.114

I regard such a process as desirable and ask that it be considered.

For example, a friend who read an English book about writers of Tamil Nadu told me—whether he is right or wrong I do not know—that Anna's name had been omitted from its list. Perarignar Anna has been acclaimed as one of Tamil Nadu's foremost writers. A foreign reader might otherwise assume that the book's list is the complete account of Tamil Nadu's writers.

I am not saying that every writer must be named. I only say that leading writers should not be omitted in a way that creates false information. Nor do I wish to interfere with or dominate another person's freedom to write. An author may insist on retaining a view; another may accept an explanation that a mistake has occurred and correct it. The University should think about such questions.

The University is autonomous. Interference in that autonomy would itself harm the autonomy we seek. That is precisely why I ask the University itself to consider the matter.

## PDF 116 / printed p.115

When research books are being published, it is also worth considering what researchers choose to study. We place Kattabomman among the freedom fighters of Tamil Nadu. Silambu Chelvar M. P. Sivagnanam in particular wrote of Kattabomman as an early freedom fighter against British rule and helped establish that image deeply in the minds of Tamil people.

Plays and films followed. If, in the name of research, someone now says that Kattabomman was merely a palaiyakkarar chief or robber who quarrelled with foreigners because he refused tax, and not a freedom fighter, we should ask who benefits. More than any benefit, how great may be the loss? A sense of freedom nurtured over many years could be shattered by such research. I therefore believe researchers should be cautious about entering such fields in ways that undermine it.

Research should not play with values that guide people toward public good and heroic feeling.

At the same time, research is certainly necessary to free people from practices that enslave them to superstition in the name of God or religion.

## PDF 117 / printed p.116

Such research should continue.

I am also glad to learn that one of the University's endowments is being inaugurated today in Periyar's name. Research needed for rationalism should certainly take place under it. If research can help lift a society kept down in the name of caste, then it must be put to that use.

I do not question the ability of researchers. I am indicating the kind of purpose research should serve. I ask the University to examine the conditions under which its books are published and take sound decisions.

As Tamil works publish Tamil Nadu's literature and history, works from other languages must also come into Tamil. At the same time our Tamil classics, literature and history must be carried into other languages and to other countries.

Just as we read the many ideas of the world, we must create ways for the world to know us. The University should work toward that too.

## PDF 118 / printed p.117

I praise and congratulate those who have brought out 108 books in four years. This pace should continue without obstruction. The Vice-Chancellor mentioned at the beginning that the Government should increase its grant. I say only this: the responsibility for earning an even larger increase lies in the pace you demonstrate; and, in keeping with that pace, you can certainly obtain the necessary support from me as Finance Minister.
