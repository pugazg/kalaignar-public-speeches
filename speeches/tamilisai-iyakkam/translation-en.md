## Tamil Isai Movement

At a time when we are striving for Tamil music to grow and live, it is surprising that a conference should be conducted under the new name “Tamilar Isai Iyakkam”—the Tamil People’s Music Movement.

You know that a condition has developed in Tamil Nadu in which it has become difficult to determine what, among the music being performed, is actually Tamil music.

The distinction between “Tamil music” and “music of Tamils” cannot be denied.

At a time when the music of Tamils may consist of Telugu music or music in many other languages, it is better and more courageous to call what we seek plainly “Tamil music.”

Therefore, when such conferences are convened, they should be convened in the name of Tamil Isai itself.

Some will say: “Is not Tamil Isai automatically Tamilar Isai? Are not Tamils the people singing it? Therefore, even if it is in Telugu, is it not still the music of Tamils?”

That is precisely why it should be called Tamil Isai.

Until around 1940, hardly anyone dared to come forward openly to work for Tamil music.

## PDF 96 / printed p.95

In 1940, at a convocation at Annamalai University, Raja Sir Annamalai Chettiar called for people to work for the advancement of Tamil music and to engage vigorously in developing it.

Following this, Thanthai Periyar, Arignar Anna, Muthamizh Kavalar Ki. Aa. Pe. Viswanatham and others made determined efforts to develop Tamil music.

Those who know political history understand that the issue was also taken up from social and political standpoints.

I do not wish to mix politics into the Tamil Isai movement. But you know how much opposition the movement had to endure when it began.

When I was working at the “Kudiyarasu” office in Erode, music festivals were customarily held in connection with the Thiruvaiyaru Thyagaraja festival.

One musician who took part came on stage and sang Tamil songs throughout his performance, then finished and left the stage.

The next musician declared, “I shall mount this stage only after it has been purified, punyaha-vachanam performed, and the pollution removed.” Only after those rituals had been carried out did he perform.

That incident led me to write an editorial titled, “Has it become polluted?”

## PDF 97 / printed p.96

I concluded it by asking: “A stage on which Tamil music was performed is being ritually purified of pollution. Are Tamils truly living with dignity?”

That was the degree of opposition that had to be endured.

Has the Tamil Isai movement advanced? We must admit that it has not advanced enough.

How far has it progressed? Apart from holding a Tamil music festival for five or six days once a year at Annamalai Mandram, we have not developed Tamil music across the country.

At a Tamil music festival in Chennai I said: “Music has no language; caste alone has a language.”

Some publications seized favourably upon my statement that music has no language and printed my speech. They had rarely printed my speeches so prominently before. What “offence” had I committed? I had said that music itself has no language and that caste is what possesses one. Perhaps only when one commits some offence against Tamil identity does one’s speech find a place there!

When music comes through the nagaswaram, it has no language.

When it comes through the flute, it has no language.

When it is born on the veena, it has no language; when it comes through the fiddle, it has no language.

## PDF 98 / printed p.97

But when music comes through the voice of a singer, it does have a language.

That language should be one’s own language—and for us that language is Tamil.

I regret that some people have set out to lecture me by reducing my statement to “music has no language.”

Many of the terms now used in music have equivalents in old Tamil.

Some say that swaras, ragas, bhavas and talas all belong to another language and are not Tamil.

The morning mode called Bhupalam is described in old Tamil literature as “maruthappann.”

The evening mode Sankarabharanam is called “sevvazhippann.”

Arohanam, avarohanam and gamakam can be expressed in Tamil as “etram,” “irakkam” and “alukkal.”

Strangely, we often fail to understand when something is said in a language familiar to us, but accept it when expressed in an unfamiliar language.

That is why it has become necessary to speak to Tamils themselves about Tamil music.

If Tamil Isai is to grow, Tamil music festivals and conferences must be organised throughout Tamil Nadu.
