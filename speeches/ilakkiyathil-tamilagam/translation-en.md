Karunanidhi opens at the Government College in Ponneri by reflecting on how the social status of Tamil speech has changed. He remembers a period when speaking English at public functions was treated as a mark of prestige, while Tamil was pushed aside even in Tamil Nadu. He links this change to the institutional adoption of the Tamil Thai Vazhthu, based on Manonmaniam Sundaram Pillai's “Neerarum Kadaludutha.”

He then turns from ceremonial Tamil to the deeper antiquity of the language. Referring to the Tolkappiyam and its references to earlier scholars, he argues that Tamil must have possessed a long spoken and intellectual history before its earliest surviving grammatical works. He recalls Periyar's deliberately provocative description of Tamil as a “barbaric language” and interprets it humorously as testimony to the language's existence from very ancient times.

From there the speech moves to Anna and the triad of duty, dignity and discipline. Karunanidhi identifies three major achievements of Anna's short period as Chief Minister that, in his view, permanently changed Tamil society: legal recognition of Self-Respect marriages, the two-language policy excluding compulsory Hindi in Tamil Nadu schools, and the renaming of Madras State as Tamil Nadu.

He recounts the earlier legislative argument over whether “Tamil Nadu” had literary authority and invokes the Silappathikaram phrase “இமிழ் கடல் வரைப்பில் தமிழகம்.” He then describes the renaming ceremony and Anna's declaration that governments might change, but these achievements could not be reversed.

The final section places particular weight on the sacrifices of young people in the anti-Hindi struggles. Karunanidhi argues that the price paid by Tamil youth created the political reality in which leaders visiting Tamil Nadu must assure people that Hindi will not be imposed.

He closes with a wordplay on the names of the student leader Ravi and principal Padmanabhan: just as sunlight makes the lotus bloom, Anna's intellectual light, he says, helped Tamil society flower. The responsibility for protecting that flowering now rests with the younger generation.

## Separate source closing note

Speech delivered at the Government College, Ponneri, on 15 October 1980.
