## Kambar Festival (1)

As usual, the Kambar Festival is being held in Karaikudi; unusually, I have come to it.

I know that all of you have gathered with a question mark in your minds about what I am going to say.

Rather than asking, “Was he not the man who once said that the Kamba Ramayanam should be burned? How has he come here?”, the more appropriate question would be: “Was he not the man who said that the Kamba Ramayanam should be burned? How did they invite him here?”

So I believe that those who invited me possess even more courage than I do.

Some fifteen or twenty years ago, the Kamba Ramayanam became a matter of controversy in Tamil Nadu. A storm-like campaign calling for it to be burned was launched by Periyar, and many wars of words took place across Tamil Nadu. Perarignar Anna debated against Navalar Somasundara Bharathiar and against Sollin Selvar Sethu Pillai. In the end, those who came to oppose Anna's arguments asked that the Kamba Ramayanam not be burned for the sake of its poetic beauty,

## PDF 34 / printed p.33

but they did not express devotion to its characters. It was because of that victory that the attempt to burn it was abandoned.

There are many kinds of burning. A stove is lit so that delicious food may be cooked; a lamp is lit so that the house may have light. Likewise, when it was said that the Kamba Ramayanam should be burned, the purpose was to make scholars bring Kamban's poetic genius alone before the people. The learned scholars understood this.

Once the scholars understood it, there was no need to carry out the act; that is why the attempt was given up.

The friend who presided over this function made one point very clearly.

He said that this festival is not being held to praise Rama or Lakshmana; it is a festival to praise Kamban. That is precisely why I have come.

Our appreciation of Kamban did not begin only after Thiru Sa. Ganesan invited me here. When statues were installed on the Chennai seafront for the great Tamil scholars during the World Tamil Conference, we also installed a statue for Kamban. We are therefore not people who failed to honour Kamban, nor people who failed to honour Kamban's Tamil. You must understand that.

## PDF 35 / printed p.34

In Anna's play, Ravana brings a case before a court against Kamban for calling him a merciless demon.

As Ravana stands in court accusing Kamban, the judge, Neethidevan, listens to Ravana's argumentative skill and Kamban's poetic skill, becomes unable to decide what judgment to give, faints and falls. The curtain drops; the play ends.

Those who hear the argument themselves become dazed. Anna therefore ends the play, titled *Neethidevan Mayakkam*, with the idea that there is nothing surprising about Neethidevan himself fainting. It is worth remembering that Anna himself played Ravana in it.

Kamban tried very hard to give the characters of Valmiki's Ramayana a Tamil form — to adapt them to Tamil culture, Tamil values and Tamil norms.

The word to underline is “tried.” That is exactly what I said.

Ultimately, Kamban did not fully turn them into Tamil characters. If proof is needed, Silambu Selvar M.P. Sivagnanam is here as my protection.

With him sitting before me, I begin this discussion. A friend wrote to me: “I hear you are participating in the Kambar Festival. Very good! We are surprised — but also pleased. I am sending you Silambu Selvar

## PDF 36 / printed p.35

M.P. Sivagnanam's book *Kambar Kaviyinbam*, which may be useful to you at the festival.” He sent me the book, and I read it fully yesterday while travelling by train.

The chairman said that he himself had argued that Rama was right to kill Vali. But if you look at what Silambu Selvar M.P. Sivagnanam, who will speak after me today, says of the episode, he ends the book by saying: “I am compelled to conclude that Rama's killing of Vali was an unjust act.”

There is no room left in the book to say what else he thinks.

Thiru Sa. Ganesan says there is no room in Kamban either. Because attacking from behind — from concealment — is not the Tamil way of warfare, Kamban makes Vali himself speak of the glory of valour.

Through Vali, Kamban himself seems to raise the question: “Can even a great warrior like Rama attack me from hiding?”

Even Rama cannot answer it. Yet the chairman of this festival explained: “Vali seized Sugriva's wife; therefore Rama's killing Vali from concealment was justice.”

But Sugriva, who accused Vali of taking his wife and secured punishment for him —

## PDF 37 / printed p.36

what did that Sugriva do in Valmiki's Ramayana? After Vali died, he took Vali's wife Tara as his own wife. That is according to Valmiki's Ramayana. Kamban, however, did not allow that.

In Valmiki's Ramayana, after Vali's death, Lakshmana goes to Vali's palace. Tara, Vali's widow, appears beautifully adorned and attractive; Valmiki writes that even Lakshmana is reminded of his wife in Ayodhya.

Kamban did not wish to portray Tara in that way. When Lakshmana goes to Vali's palace, Tara appears in white, in the widow's form familiar to Tamil women, with the appropriate hair and appearance. That is how Kamban describes her.

Thus, taking the narrative from Valmiki's Ramayana and handling it in a way suited to Tamil Nadu, Kamban composed a Ramayana for Tamils. In one place he treats the characters fairly; in another, less fairly.

The characters themselves are not the main thing. I too have written many stories.

## PDF 38 / printed p.37

No special meetings have been held to praise the characters I created. After writing many stories, I have been praised only in my capacity as a writer.

Silambu Selvar M.P. Sivagnanam has written many fine books and created many characters in them. Festivals are not held to praise all those characters. I praise him because he has created such characters and expressed such ideas.

Likewise, our Sa. Ganesan has received much appreciation, not because of characters he created, but because he possessed the ability to create such characters.

In *Kambar Kaviyinbam*, Silambu Selvar compares Krishna and Rama at one point.

On the battlefield Ravana stands unarmed. Rama says, “Go today and come tomorrow,” and sends him away. Is this not the tradition of war? Is this not the dharma of war? Silambu Selvar praises Rama for this. He then brings Krishna into the comparison and says Krishna did not act justly on the battlefield: by strategic devices he agreed to Karna being killed contrary to the ethics of war. Therefore, M.P. Sivagnanam argues there that he cannot accept Krishna's method; Krishna may be great in strategy, but the methods adopted must themselves be good. He makes Krishna the accused and presents Rama as righteous.

## PDF 39 / printed p.38

Turn another ten pages and once again he brings Krishna and Rama together, this time in the episode of Vali's death.

There he says that Rama's act was wrong: it was not right to intervene in a dispute between two brothers and end it by killing one with a weapon. Rama is thus made the accused. But when the dispute between the Pandavas and Kauravas is described as a quarrel among brothers, Krishna did not himself bear a weapon and did not intervene in that way. Therefore Krishna's conduct is superior in this instance, and Rama's conduct cannot be accepted. Thus, in two places in the same book Silambu Selvar gives two apparently different judgments.

I am not finding fault with Silambu Selvar. The characters themselves have been created in such contradictory situations. Whatever the work and however the characters are shaped, they can leave even the critic confused. Yet the Tamil spoken through those characters, the truths Kamban shaped through them, and the service he thereby rendered to Tamil — as long as Tamil lives, as long as Tamil Nadu lives, as long as the last Tamil person lives in Tamil Nadu —

## PDF 40 / printed p.39

I do not deny that all this deserves praise.

Thiru M.P. Sivagnanam does not forgive even a small fault. Thus, page after page in *Kambar Kaviyinbam*, whether the character belongs to the Kamba Ramayanam or to Valmiki, he pours out forceful criticism and, at the same time, offers reconciliations.

He says that the Vali-vadha episode is a blemish even on the Kamba Ramayanam and then adds: “What can be done? Is there not a blemish even on the moon?”

By saying that the moon is beautiful precisely with its blemish, he seeks to protect the Kamba Ramayanam. Why? For Tamil.

So the very arguments that we made when we said it should be burned — and that we still make — were clearly written even then by Silambu Selvar M.P. Sivagnanam from the side that said it should not be burned. I therefore greatly appreciate his capacity to condemn a fault wherever he finds one.

The Silambu Selvar participating in this festival once held a conference against the Dravidian movement. Today he supports the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam government. Why?

He publicly declared that he joined us because we had abandoned the Dravida Nadu secession demand; and he joined us.

## PDF 41 / printed p.40

In the same way, if I have come to speak at the Kambar Festival, it is because most scholars now praise only Kamban's poetic beauty and leave aside the characters. When a willingness to concede develops on both sides, there is an opportunity to meet, approach and come closer to one another. It is in that manner that I obtained the opportunity to take part in this festival.

Through this festival, while we rejoice together in Kamban's fame, it is not enough merely to praise Kamban. The glorious Tamil that Kamban nurtured must grow further; that Tamil must receive still greater strength and encouragement. I request that everyone work for the advancement of that Tamil.
