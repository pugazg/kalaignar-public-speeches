## Let Us Foster the Arts

This pleasant annual function, conducted on behalf of the Tamil Nadu Sangeetha Nataka Sangam, gives all of us an opportunity to meet and renew, from time to time, our Tamil feeling and our artistic feeling when they begin to flag.

A Tamil will not consent to losing Tamil art. The renaissance that has arisen today in Tamil society—in fostering art, conveying ideas through art, and performing social service—is genuinely worthy of praise and welcome. Yet because, from time to time, room is given for things that distort art to arise, my affectionate request is that all of us engrave in our minds this resolve: art must remain art, and art must be fostered without allowing distortions to arise.

When we all gathered in this great hall last year, we came at the invitation of the Sangeetha Nataka Sangam. This year we have gathered at the invitation of the Iyal Isai Nataka Mandram. Though the name has changed, there is no difference in the programmes held here, nor in the honours conferred,

## PDF 65 / printed p.64

nor any contradiction in the qualifications by which recipients are selected. But in Tamil feeling there is a great difference between the old and the new names. A couple of months ago, at another function attended by Navalar and me, I asked that the name “Tamil Nadu Sangeetha Nataka Sangam” be changed to “Iyal, Isai, Nataka Mandram.” The members of this body and the Government immediately accepted that suggestion, and the name has now been changed.

To some, changes of name may seem ordinary. They may ask, “It is only a name change—is this necessary?” Others may ask, “Why should it be changed?” But this change is a fitting example of the feelings that can be carried within a name.

When awards were given under the name Sangeetha Nataka Sangam, it could appear that only those accomplished in music and drama were eligible. In practice the bounds had already been extended: people from cinema and even writers of story and dialogue had received awards. Under the new name, anyone receiving an award can properly be understood as belonging within the three fields of iyal, isai and natakam—literature, music and drama. In that sense, the new name is appropriate.

Every year, Navalar and I find reason to take pride on behalf of our own district, Thanjavur, when these

## PDF 66 / printed p.65

awards are presented. Seven portraits of departed artists were unveiled here; except for Madurai Ponnusamy, all the others belonged to Thanjavur district. And among the twenty-nine people receiving the Kalaimamani award today, more than twelve are from Thanjavur district. Do not therefore think that I am speaking with narrow district self-interest. It only shows that, just as Thanjavur is the granary of paddy, it is also a treasury of art.

Friends from other districts should not be angry. It used to be said in Thanjavur that great masters and distinguished artists—people such as Thittappa and Smt. Sundarambal—would regard participation in an artistic event in Thanjavur with a pride like that of one receiving the title of Brahmarishi from Viswamitra or Vasishta. Do not think that I use the past tense because that age is dead. I say with pride that it continues even today. Art born, nourished and developed in Thanjavur has helped make the whole of Tamil Nadu an artistic garden. All artists are entitled to that pride. I therefore congratulate every award recipient.

Navalar observed that in ancient times artists received gifts from kings and zamindars.

## PDF 67 / printed p.66

Today royal houses that once bestowed gifts have disappeared and the zamindars have been abolished. But the lineage of artists who receive honours has not disappeared; it continues to grow. Art cannot be destroyed, and people with artistic hearts cannot be shut inside cages.

Only when those who assume responsibility possess an artistic heart can they fully feel humanitarian emotions. When people of artistic sensibility enter positions of responsibility, they begin to see, in the country itself, the very characters they once created.

Whether it is Sethu Nath Arangannal, honoured today, or other writers, they have the power to create many kinds of characters. We portray the poor person, the beggar and the leprosy patient; we write about all their sufferings. In doing so, we become those characters. When writing about a beggar, the writer must become the beggar if the character is to live truthfully. If, after creating such characters, writers remain only writers, they can do no more than argue on behalf of those characters.

But if those writers assume governmental responsibility, they see that the characters they created walk not only across a stage but across the country. Then the task is not merely to create them as characters; it is also to take away the begging bowls in their hands

## PDF 68 / printed p.67

and give them a new life. The basis for that impulse is the habit of approaching problems with an artistic heart. We may therefore trust that those with artistic hearts will possess humanitarian feeling. There may be exceptions; this should not be treated as an absolute rule. Yet we can reasonably believe that artistic sensibility nurtures humanity.

How greatly artists were honoured in earlier times! The poet Nappasalaiyar, an intimate of the great king Killivalavan, sang of him.

Killivalavan dies. The poet asks: how could he possibly have died? No one could defeat him.

> “O Death that took Valavan of the strong chariot,  
> whose army crossed the clash of shining battle...”

How, the poet asks, could Yama—Death—have taken the life of such a king? Why could he not have taken it by force?

> “Not by enmity,  
> not by rage,  
> not by direct assault  
> could you have escaped defeat!”

Death could not have taken Killivalavan’s life by opposing him, befriending him, or fighting him. Then how could it have happened?

## PDF 69 / printed p.68

> “Like a bard,  
> with folded hands in praise,  
> you must have begged  
> and received it.”

Death, says the poet, must have disguised himself as a poet and asked Killivalavan, “Give me your life.” Believing Death to be a poet, Killivalavan would then have given his own life.

Nappasalaiyar’s poem is thus an example of the esteem in which kings of that age held poets.

The Government of Tamil Nadu continues to honour men and women of the arts who possess such renown, distinction and excellence.

We have not forgotten that this practice was begun by the previous government. But this Government never withdraws from continuing what is good. It not only continues such work; it expands it and makes it more fruitful. I place this before you as evidence that the Government will continue its constructive work, and I congratulate and felicitate all those who have received awards.
