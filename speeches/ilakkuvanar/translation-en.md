## PDF 74 / printed p.73

### 8

## Ilakkuvanar

Many speakers here have described the debt owed throughout the country to Professor Ilakkuvanar for the service he rendered to Tamil and to Tamils.

Were all Tamil scholars united whenever danger came to Tamil or to Tamils? Painfully, the answer is no.

When compulsory Hindi—compulsory imposition of Hindi—threatened Tamil in 1938, did all Tamil leaders and scholars unite to oppose it? We may like to say yes. But beyond names such as Thiru. Vi. Ka. and Maraimalai Adigal, we cannot truthfully say that a whole army of Tamil scholars rose together against Hindi.

At every such moment, as though disunity itself were an essential characteristic of Tamil scholars and they feared that characteristic might somehow be blemished, they have faithfully preserved that habit of disunity from then until now.

## PDF 75 / printed p.74

Differences of opinion among Tamil scholars are necessary. If those differences help Tamil grow, advance Tamil research, or reveal more of Tamil history, we will always welcome them.

Competition is necessary. But competition and jealousy are not the same. Competition must not turn into jealousy.

If someone says, “Ilakkuvanar wrote the Tolkappiyam in English; I will write it in German or Russian—watch me,” that is competition.

If someone says, “Ilakkuvanar wrote the Tolkappiyam in English? What has he really accomplished?” that is jealousy.

The day this spirit of competition arises among Tamils—and the jealousy that now prevails diminishes—is the day Tamil society will be redeemed and advance.

We are often unwilling to praise the person beside us.

Knowing this, Anna said, “Even the jasmine in another man’s garden has fragrance.” Many people suffer because they have not learned to appreciate the neighbour next door or across the street.

Today the habit of appreciation in other political parties is not a full moon; it appears only as a crescent.

## PDF 76 / printed p.75

That crescent-like deficiency must become fullness. Without regard to party differences, people must develop the habit of praising ability wherever it exists and honouring good and capable people in any movement.

Without that, the Tamil cannot raise his head. Among those who rose as pioneers to create this outlook in Tamil society was the late Professor Ilakkuvanar.

Everyone who spoke here noted that I had been his student. I am proud to say so. He too would repeatedly take pride in having been my teacher; such was his affection for me.

When I studied at Thiruvarur High School, I had the opportunity to receive instruction from him for some months.

Later I studied Tamil under Vidwan Dandapani Desikar and Muthukrishna Nattar—advanced Tamil and literary Tamil. Outside the hours in which they taught at school, I went to their homes and studied Tamil poetry and literature.

Dandapani Desikar taught me the sweetness of Tamil.

Muthukrishna Nattar taught me Tamil consciousness.

## PDF 77 / printed p.76

Ilakkuvanar taught me the self-respect that the Tamil people require.

That quality of self-respect remained with him until his death.

At times he was carried away by emotion rather than guided by considered feeling.

Even so, this Government has done everything it could in recognition of his greatness.

Some have said that the Chief Minister bears a great responsibility to protect his family. I would say this to them: it would not honour Ilakkuvanar if one individual alone took responsibility for maintaining that family. No one should think I am avoiding responsibility; it is a responsibility that all of us must share.

Without considering the family’s condition, his children, and what specifically must be done, I cannot make a hasty announcement.

But I can firmly say that I am prepared to do whatever lies within my power for that family.

Ilakkuvanar went to prison for opposing Hindi. He worked so that Tamil might live with vigour and dignity.

It would be more accurate to say that he trained not merely students but disciplined fighters.

## PDF 78 / printed p.77

He lived without bowing his head to anyone. He was like Cheran Senguttuvan, who confronted and defeated Kanakavijayan.

Do we live today in the age of Cheran Senguttuvan? No. In that age, a poet returned from the north and told the Chera king:

> “Kanakan and Vijayan, whose tongues knew no restraint,  
> spoke their words among the assembled kings;  
> I shall report those words to the Chera.”

The moment Senguttuvan heard that two northern kings had insulted him, he flared up, gathered an army, marched north, subdued his enemies, and made those who had disparaged the Tamils seek forgiveness from the Tamils. That was that age.

We, by contrast, live in an age almost upside down from Senguttuvan’s—an age in which one may have to travel north and say, “Look, I shall ask them to make you apologise!”

Can such a condition be allowed to continue? Our language today occupies second place. Though administratively we give Tamil first place, some people still keep Tamil in second place in their own minds.

We have developed a fascination for English. We think English is necessary for contact with the world.

## PDF 79 / printed p.78

Are people in foreign countries themselves enslaved to English? No. When I travelled abroad, I visited England and London, Rome, France, West Germany and other places.

In places where we travelled and stayed, I saw people unable to answer when we asked them something in English.

They speak in their mother tongues. In France we searched for an English newspaper and could not find one; only French newspapers were available. Likewise in Rome, an English-language newspaper was not readily available.

To that extent, they think, speak and write in their own languages.

Even for international contact, English is used mainly at official and governmental levels.

Or it may be needed to read the works of major scholars.

Therefore I say emphatically: even if English is necessary, please teach the children in our homes first to think in Tamil.

Today our children think first in English.

When they see a loudspeaker, let them first think “oliperukki” in Tamil; only if they must explain it to someone else need they say “loudspeaker.”

## PDF 80 / printed p.79

But children today grow up thinking “loudspeaker” first rather than “oliperukki.”

When they think of the sheet in their hand, they think “paper,” and only when asked what “paper” means do they say “kagitham.”

I say: think first in Tamil—think “thal,” a sheet. If necessary, say “paper” to make another person understand.

Let us therefore think in Tamil and act in accordance with Tamil tradition. There was a time when the Tamil ruled with his flag flying. Even today, in one sense or another, flags are flown!

But the flag he raised in that earlier age flew in Kadaram and in Lanka. Tamils proudly engraved the tiger, bow and fish emblems in the Himalayas.

If the Tamil who possessed such a history has today lost direction and fallen, I ask in Ilakkuvanar’s name: should we not take a pledge to advance Tamil society?

Do not imagine that, by speaking of Tamil, the Tamil people and Tamil society, I am trying to inflame some narrow separatist ethnic feeling.

## PDF 81 / printed p.80

I speak in the sense laid down by Perarignar Anna at the Virugambakkam conference: government is needed to protect Tamil tradition and Tamil dignity. It is along that line that this Government functions.

Tamil — Nadu!  
There is nothing wrong in seeking Tamil.  
That is why our land is called Tamil Nadu. Tamil was placed in the very name of the land so that we would not forget the language.

Why is it called Tamil Nadu?

Seek Tamil.  
Seek Tamil culture.  
Seek Tamil character.  
Seek Tamil consciousness.  
Seek Tamil literature.  
Seek Tamil advancement.

That is the meaning.

Do not regard “Tamil Nadu” merely as the name of a territory; regard it as advice that we continually give ourselves.

Therefore protect and preserve Tamil society. That society, once radiant in the world, has today grown dim.

## PDF 82 / printed p.81

We must labour to make it shine again. We must remove every obstacle in its path. We must adopt that determination and take that pledge.
