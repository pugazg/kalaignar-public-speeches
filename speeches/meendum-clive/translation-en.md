#### Clive Again?

The Congressmen who had been speaking of democracy, democracy—the way they govern the country today compels one to wonder in dismay whether they have fashioned democracy itself, the very meaning of democracy, in a new way. In a way directly opposite to it.

The people of the North are very concerned to keep the South, the Madras State—the Dravidian land—as their market.

They have not failed to devote their full attention to ensuring that conditions suited to this exist everywhere, at all times—the people of the North, the leaders of the North.

Sri Prakasa was appointed Governor of Madras, and before he left for Madras, a felicitation function was held for him in Delhi.

The speech Sri Prakasa delivered at that felicitation function is one that deserves very close attention.

### PDF page 47 — printed page 46

‘I am going to Madras as Governor. It is a very difficult responsibility. I am going as Clive's heir,’ he is reported in the newspapers to have said in this manner!

Clive's heir! Sri Prakasa imagines himself thus as soon as he is appointed Governor of Madras!

Clive, Robert Clive, was the man who sowed the seed for the British grip to arise in India, for British dominance first to take root in India! To carry on that Clive's work—to proceed in a manner, in a course of action, that would arrange the ways, means and facilities for the grip of the North to be firmly established over the Madras Province—he has dreamed that he is being sent as Clive's heir; not only has he dreamed it, he has also said that he is going to Madras precisely to make it a reality.

What else can be the meaning of saying, ‘I am going as Clive's heir; I am being sent as Clive's heir’? Think, friends, think!

In his youth, Clive was spoken of with contempt and abuse in England.

History describes him as a roughneck, a fool, an utter idler, someone incapable of getting ahead, a brute.

### PDF page 48 — printed page 47

As a boy, Clive was one who did no work at all, not merely one who did nothing. He wandered about the town unwilling to do anything at all!

He went about in a state in which local quarrels and local fights were his world, his excitement, his life of pleasure.

Going down into drains, rolling in mud, creating noise and commotion—Clive regarded these as his daily duty.

Unable to bear the townspeople's scorn and the taunting words of relatives, his parents kept crying out in anguish as they thought of their son.

At this time, they placed Clive in an ordinary job with the English trading company called the East India Company in India and sent him to India.

Unable to endure his uproar and the trouble he caused in England, they suffered and sent him to India thinking that it would be enough if he were simply gone. His family did so with the fervent hope that perhaps his rampage would subside at least in a foreign country.

Clive arrived in India, joined the army and, for England's grip and dominance in India—

### PDF page 49 — printed page 48

—for its sovereignty to be established, he sowed the seed and laid the foundation: a truth history has witnessed, and a truth known to everyone as well!

Clive was rough, brutal and obstinate, a man who went about possessed by the frenzy that whatever he thought of had to be done blindly and had to be carried through to completion.

This kind of Clive's brutality and obstinate frenzy, in one way, under the circumstances of that time, greatly helped England's victory in India!

For this reason, in history Clive is sometimes called the man who built the foundation of British imperialism, of British rule.

Having spoken while imagining himself to be such a Clive and then come here, perhaps that is why Governor Sri Prakasa rules roughly, in the manner that what he thinks is justice—what he decrees is law. If the people think so, one has to consider that there is nothing surprising in it.

The British imperialism that arose in Clive's time endured and operated in India for at least a few centuries.

### PDF page 50 — printed page 49

The British imperialism that came in the form of Clive remained, even after the country attained independence, for a few days in the form of Lord Mountbatten, and then departed!

But according to Governor Prakasa's speech, I declare that one thing alone is certain, certain: the northern imperialism that comes in the form of Prakasa will return in the very form of Prakasa—it will be sent back from the Dravidian land.

Dravidians have become conscious of themselves, gained feeling, embraced self-respect and risen eagerly to conduct self-rule.

The Dravidian has begun to free himself from the condition of living on earth as a slave and servant, to free himself from the grip of the North. To forget and conceal this, and for those who came to rule to proceed only in their own way, is not merely to fail to understand the condition of the country properly; it is also to lose the goodwill of the people.

The Congressmen who had shouted “democracy, democracy” until their throats hurt have made themselves servants of the people of the North and continue praising and worshipping the line of Clive; toward the country, toward the Dravidian—
