### Speech at Nellikuppam Pugazhendhi's Wedding

At this moment when I express my heartfelt great joy at this wedding, our dear friend and party treasurer Arcot Veerasamy, who has presided over this pleasant function, the ministers, our younger brother Panneerselvam, the district party secretary who delivered the welcome address, our dear comrade Govindarasan, one of the pioneers of the Marxist Communist Party and a former Member of the Legislative Assembly, as well as the legislators and people of this area, have conveyed their loving and heartfelt wishes to the bride and groom. Just as the word “Pugazhendhi” is in the groom's name, he is living with fame. They have conveyed wishes, filled with eagerness, that he should earn still greater fame.

In expressing my desire regarding this life-partnership, I am in no way behind the ministers and friends who spoke before me — in wishing Pugazhendhi well, in honouring him —

## PDF 185 / printed p.184

in praising him! Pugazhendhi is one of the foremost speakers of the DMK. I will not say that there are hundreds of outstanding speakers in the DMK. Nor will I say, for that reason, that the number of speakers in this party is very small. Among the party's speakers — particularly in that list carrying the stamp of being speakers of the headquarters party organisation — I wish to point out here that one name shining brightly is that of our younger brother Pugazhendhi.

The younger generation is capable of determining the future. It is capable of winning the future. It is in the advancement of that generation that the welfare of Tamils, Tamil ethnic consciousness — indeed, the welfare of the country — are all contained. As far as the DMK is concerned, because of the rare efforts of Perarignar Anna and the immense affection he had for young people, large numbers of people committed themselves to this movement in order to embrace Thanthai Periyar's principles through Anna's path.

Anna had opportunities to explain Thanthai Periyar's principles not merely where the general public gathered, but also in student forums, especially in universities and colleges. At a time when he kept receiving such opportunities, there was one event eagerly anticipated throughout Tamil Nadu in those days — and there is

## PDF 186 / printed p.185

so much meaning in saying “in those days.” In such a period Anna delivered a speech at Annamalai University. When he was about to speak, the topic itself was given to him on the stage. He was asked to speak on the topic “By the Riverbank.” Those who had given the title, and those gathered in the hall, all wondered what he was going to say under a title such as “By the Riverbank.”

But when Anna spoke for nearly an hour on “By the Riverbank,” he explained the culture of the banks of the Ganges, the culture of the banks of the Cauvery, and the differences and contradictions between the two, and on that day he made that title, “By the Riverbank,” spread throughout Tamil Nadu.

Likewise, at the same university, Anna's speech under the title “O, Fallen Tamil Nadu!” was the speech that made fallen Tamil Nadu raise its head. Anna's powerful speeches, ideas, thoughts, and the forms of action produced by those thoughts were the reasons young people came into this movement like spearheads. Following that path, the DMK today continues to work to bring more and more young people into this movement. Young people too are joining and working in it.

But I cannot refrain from expressing one regret. There was once a time when, in general, the younger generation

## PDF 187 / printed p.186

greatly valued social consciousness, language consciousness, ethnic consciousness and national consciousness, and wished to commit themselves to a movement on the basis of principle and work in it. I will not say that this has been turned completely upside down today. I only wish to say that it has fallen behind to some extent.

You know how deeply, in our youth, people like us involved ourselves in this movement on the basis of principle. You also know that those of us who joined it still remain in this movement with the same firmness to this day. A few may have changed. That may be their nature, or it may be a disorder caused by opportunism. But when the battalion of youth blossomed under Anna's leadership, I, who grew up holding their hands, our party's general secretary, the self-respect Professor, and others still continue to walk with the same firmness on the foundation of Periyar's principles and along Perarignar Anna's path of ideals.

How do young people generally think today? After finishing their studies, once their student days are over, a young person begins to think. Many plan what they should study in order to shape their lives, complete their studies, think about what professional education they should pursue, and then

## PDF 188 / printed p.187

qualify themselves and become experts in various offices and different fields, engaging themselves in that effort.

Some other young people, when they think about whether they should involve themselves in social work or politics, think in this way today: in those days, a young person would ask what the policy of that party was, what the policy of this movement was, for whom it worked, and on the basis of what ideal it functioned, and would choose such a movement.

But many among today's young people — I am not speaking of everyone — calculate like this: in which party can I get a post immediately; in which party can I make money immediately; in which party can I gain influence immediately? When he calculates in that way, what does he think about the DMK? “Oh, my! All those young people who have been there for a very long time are big people there today. Only if they vacate their places will there be room for us if we go there. So we do not want that place.” They look for whichever place is vacant and go there, without any thought, rational inquiry, commitment to principle or sense of ideal; that is how young people today think about their lives.

## PDF 189 / printed p.188

This comes from the fact that they think about themselves. If they begin to think about the people, the country, society, the language, the race and ethnic consciousness, all these matters I have spoken of will certainly move away from their lives without obstructing them.

Our younger brother Pugazhendhi, instead of thinking in that way, thought in terms of principle and committed himself to this movement. Another reason he developed such thinking is his father. All those who spoke before me explained how deeply his father involved himself in this movement and worked with a profound sense of ideal. Our younger brother Durai Murugan, who first brought Pugazhendhi to me and introduced him, was very proud that a younger brother born in such a family is today one of the foremost speakers in this movement.

Today's speakers receive many invitations to meetings. I think Durai Murugan intended to say one thing but left it unsaid. He said that wherever Pugazhendhi goes, he mixes simply and naturally with party friends there, and that his friendliness and way of dealing with people are such that they invite him again to their meetings. Pugazhendhi does not have one shortcoming that some other speakers have. Sometimes even I am shocked when I look at *Murasoli*. There will be an announcement that one speaker will speak on the evening of the 5th

## PDF 190 / printed p.189

at Nellikuppam, and on that same evening of the 5th he will also speak at Palayamkottai in Tirunelveli district. I myself telephone *Murasoli* and ask how they published an announcement saying that he would speak in two towns on the same date. They say that information came from both towns, so they printed it. That should not happen. In Pugazhendhi's case, it is worthy of praise that this practice is not followed.

Some speakers, out of what is called personal consideration, give a date to whoever asks for one, and then think only about which town it is convenient to reach on that date and go only to that town. They do not think about how much distress this causes those who organise the meeting. If speakers had themselves been organisers of meetings, they would be moved to think about that situation. As for me, I was not someone who simply climbed onto a stage and began speaking at the outset. I was one who put up stages for many others — not only Periyar and Perarignar Anna, but also Navalar Nedunchezhiyan, Professor Anbazhagan and Thalapathi Azhagirisamy — when I was a student in Tiruvarur.

Therefore, I am able from experience to understand how difficult it is when a leader invited to a meeting does not come. In 1942, in Tiruvarur, I conducted the conference of the “Tamil Nadu Tamil Students'

## PDF 191 / printed p.190

Forum.” I had asked Salem Ramasamy Gounder to preside over that conference, and he had agreed. At that time, Nadigavel M.R. Radha, who was famous as an actor in the world of art and in this movement, had a play running in Nagapattinam. So, thinking it would be easy for him to come to Tiruvarur, I had invited him too and had put his name on the programme. I had also invited several other learned and distinguished people.

Believing that all of them would come, I went and stood at the railway station that morning. No one came. Telegrams came in their place. Ramasamy Gounder's wife was unwell, so he sent a telegram saying he could not come. Nadigavel Radha could not come from Nagapattinam because an accident there had created a serious problem for him and left him unable to leave.

When it became clear that no one could come, I, who had gone to the railway station, nearly fainted. I became so dizzy that I had to hold on to someone standing nearby. The programme was at 10:30 in the morning. Thinking, “Everyone was expected to come; how embarrassing this will be, how many people will be disappointed,” I immediately learned that our Professor and our late friend Mathiazhagan had come to an event being held that day in Kumbakonam, and at once gave a letter to two people

## PDF 192 / printed p.191

and sent them by bus: “Both of you come to Tiruvarur; those who had agreed to come are unable to do so.” They came and took part. Only then did I feel relieved.

Only those who organise meetings know the pain caused when someone agrees to attend a meeting and then does not come. That is why our comrades should not merely be speakers; they should also be functionaries who act. That does not mean I am now inviting Pugazhendhi to come tomorrow itself saying, “I am contesting for the post of town secretary in Nellikuppam.”

A secretary must be one who acts. Our speakers should not merely be performers who say, “I will go only to the concert.” They must also be people who work. When they go to various places, after meetings are over they should sit and talk with our branch organisations, union organisations, district organisations, area organisations and town organisations, asking how work is proceeding there: Have you enrolled members? The leader and the general secretary keep issuing statements, do they not? Have you brought the membership forms? How many forms did you bring? How many members have you enrolled? When are you going to send them to party headquarters? They must involve themselves in such activities as well.

## PDF 193 / printed p.192

Therefore, one cannot grow merely by being a speaker. That speaker must become a functionary — a person who acts. If I stand before you today as the leader of the DMK, I could not have reached this position had I grown merely as a speaker. The reason I am today the chief volunteer of the DMK is that my life began then as a volunteer; that is why I am today the chief volunteer of this party. Through our younger brother Nellikuppam Pugazhendhi I say this to all the speakers in the party: rather than asking others about one's speech by saying, “Do you know how I spoke?” our dear party cadres should conduct themselves in such a way that our actions themselves are praised. I offer this, at this wedding — the life-union ceremony of our younger brother Pugazhendhi and Delsi — as a message to all the young people in the party, and I wish the bride and groom many long years of life.
