## Two Languages Are Enough

Tamil must be developed. Its strength and splendour must be made known to the country. We are people who grew through Tamil and prospered through Tamil. Therefore we have an enduring duty to foster Tamil and make it flourish. Perarignar Anna, who assumed the responsibility of government, and those of us who, after his death, stand in his path and work to fulfil his cherished principles, have done all we can to spread Tamil in Tamil Nadu and elsewhere.

A small example is the area long called “Foreshore Estate,” which has now received a new—no, an old—Tamil name: Pattinappakkam.

In ancient Tamil Nadu, settlements where people lived were often given names ending in “pakkam.”

Tamils named their residential areas “kuppam” and “pakkam.” In particular, communities living along the coast commonly called their settlements “pakkam.”

## PDF 90 / printed p.89

From Silappathikaram we know that the coastal residential quarter of Kaveripoompattinam was called Maruvurpakkam, a beautiful Tamil name.

Therefore this Government considered “Pattinappakkam” a fitting name for this coastal locality. Many asked: will people understand it? Will it roll easily off the tongue? Will it come into ordinary usage?

The same doubts were raised when Kellys became Kilpauk names in Tamil usage, and when “Paris” became Parimunai. These names have entered common speech. In the same way, we now see a desire among the people to restore or Tamilise the names of other places.

Tamil once had a golden age. Then came a stone age—the age in which we failed to care for Tamil. Now a golden age has returned.

How laughable it would be to tell a mother, “Call your child ‘cuckoo’; call the infant ‘gem’ and cherish it.” In the same way, it ought to be unnecessary to tell a Tamil: “Protect Tamil; nurture Tamil.”

We are a country that has repeatedly proclaimed the old glory of Muthamizh, the three seas and the three royal drums. But if we stop with speaking of old greatness, Tamil will remain merely

## PDF 91 / printed p.90

a language of epics, literature and verse. It must not be confined there. That is why we insist that Tamil must become a medium of instruction.

Scientific terminology must arise in Tamil. New words must develop rapidly enough for Tamil to move with the world of knowledge. They must be created and established.

English is needed for international communication and contact among countries. At the same time, in our own State our mother tongue must grow. That is why we advocate the two-language policy.

Within our State we need Tamil. For communication with other States and with countries abroad, English is sufficient.

Therefore we strongly argue that these two languages are enough.

Some say:

> “Tamil for Tamil Nadu —  
> Hindi for India —  
> English for the whole world.”

It is easy to argue this way, because those who make the argument will not themselves have to learn all three afresh. We must not forget that the burden falls on the younger generation.

A Hindi-speaking child has to study only two languages.

## PDF 92 / printed p.91

An elderly leader living in Tamil Nadu, who once lost office over the language question, no longer has to study languages himself. So he can easily say: Hindi for India, Tamil for Tamil Nadu, English for world contact. But under that scheme the children in our homes must learn Tamil for their State, Hindi for communication within India, and English for international contact—three languages.

A child in Uttar Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh or Bihar, whose mother tongue is Hindi, needs only Hindi and English. With two languages, that child’s burden ends.

Our children would study three languages and then compete with those who studied only two.

We would even have to learn Hindi as another State’s language and compete with those who possess it as their mother tongue.

When we point this out, people ask: even if English serves everyone internationally, do we not need Hindi to communicate with neighbouring States?

There is a humorous story about a great scientist. He raised a beautiful cat and made a large hole in the door so that it could come and go. Later the cat had a kitten.

The scientist made a second, smaller hole beside the large one. His friends asked why. “For the kitten,” he said.

## PDF 93 / printed p.92

Only after they asked, “Can the kitten not use the same large hole made for the cat?” did the scientist realise how unnecessary the second hole was.

Likewise, if English is already the common language for international contact, can we not use that same language for contact with neighbouring States?

If the kitten can pass through the cat’s opening, why make another opening? That is our argument. Others understand it. But they have already declared that Hindi must occupy a particular place in India, and they feel embarrassed to retreat from that declaration.

Second, because ordinary people make the argument that two languages are enough, some regard accepting it as beneath their prestige. That is why efforts continue to introduce Hindi again and strengthen its position.

At a Muthamizh festival I mention these matters because we must think about the dangers that may arise for our society, our language, our linguistic rights and the future of Tamils.

## PDF 94 / printed p.93

At functions like this we should arrange good programmes every year that present our ancient history, explain our literary wealth, recount the victories of our heroes and demonstrate the learning of our great poets.
