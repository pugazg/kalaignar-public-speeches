# Connemara Public Library Centenary Function

Karunanidhi opens by expressing happiness at taking part in two linked celebrations: the centenary of the Connemara Public Library and the silver jubilee of the Public Libraries Department. After noting that speakers before him have described the library's achievements, he makes a practical suggestion: at functions of this kind, books would be more useful gifts than ceremonial shawls. He jokes that a shawl too is made from thread, but says the same expenditure could instead place many books in people's hands.

He broadens the point from library celebrations to public life generally. Even at weddings and other functions, he suggests replacing garlands and ceremonial gifts with books. He recalls that books and reading rooms had been part of his own public activity since youth, beginning with a children's reform association in Tiruvarur that kept a small collection of books.

Karunanidhi then recounts his years in the mid-1940s at Singanallur near Coimbatore while working in the film industry. The area, he says, was politically violent, yet he and a friend, Annasamy, wanted to spread the ideas of Periyar and Anna. Because a library openly named after Dravidian leaders might not have survived, they established a Bharathi Reading Room / Bharathi Library. Alongside general newspapers and magazines they gradually introduced `திராவிடநாடு`, `குடியரசு`, and `விடுதலை`. He credits that reading-room effort with helping turn Singanallur into a stronghold of their movement.

The speech then turns to Annadurai's passion for reading. Karunanidhi recalls that even shortly before being taken for surgery at the Adyar hospital, Anna continued reading a book and put it down only when doctors came to take him to the operating theatre. He presents the episode as a lesson for a younger generation that may regard reading as tedious or unnecessary.

Karunanidhi next remembers C. W. Damodaram Pillai and U. V. Swaminatha Iyer, who searched for, collected and preserved old manuscripts so that later generations could read Tamil's older literature. He says commemorating such figures at library functions is itself a debt of gratitude.

He quotes Bharathidasan on the role of a repository of books in broadening human feeling, overcoming narrow individualism and enabling people to recognise their common humanity. He then recalls Annadurai's 1949 radio address, **“வீட்டிற்கு ஒரு புத்தகசாலை”** (“A Library for Every Home”), and reproduces at length Anna's argument that homes routinely make space for furniture, utensils, clothes, medicines and ceremonial objects while rarely reserving space for books.

Anna's prescription, as quoted in the speech, is that a household library should rank immediately after the basic needs of food and clothing. Public libraries must help those who cannot afford many books, but every home should still keep at least a basic collection. Such a collection should include useful knowledge, history, geography, an atlas, the `திருக்குறள்`, accessible selections from Sangam literature, science, and biographies of people who worked for freedom and social progress. Karunanidhi emphasises Anna's warning against filling a library with books that reinforce fatalism, prejudice, or degrading ideas.

Karunanidhi then sketches the history of the Connemara Public Library. He says Lord Connemara planned it in 1890, after which it opened in 1896; the library later rose to become a public and state-level library, while legislation in 1954 helped give major libraries at Mumbai, Calcutta, New Delhi and Chennai national standing. He states that the collection grew from **48,000** books in 1910 to roughly **five-and-a-quarter lakh** books, used by about **three-and-a-half lakh** readers.

He recalls that in 1974, during a DMK government, a three-storey addition was built beside the older structure. For the centenary, he says, the government had sanctioned **₹1.48 crore** for additional building facilities — joking that the professor on stage had understated the amount as ₹1.45 crore — and another **₹1 crore** for furniture and equipment.

Karunanidhi describes efforts begun during the 1989–91 government to turn libraries into a people's movement. He gives figures for library membership, part-time libraries, and assistance from the Raja Rammohun Roy Library Foundation, noting that Tamil Nadu had received especially high support because of the state's own allocations.

He also cites staffing figures for district library officers and says the current government, shaped by Anna's respect for learning and books, intends to extend library services throughout Tamil Nadu.

In closing, Karunanidhi welcomes a donation of **₹5 lakh** to the library and jokes that if he had been consulted in advance he might have named a larger amount. Responding to a proposal for a permanent book-exhibition building, he urges the philanthropist Nalli Kuppusamy to build one in his own name, so that his name too will become permanently associated with books and libraries.
