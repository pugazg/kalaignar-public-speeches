### 17

## Nadaga Dasar

I am very happy that this forum is observing with feeling the 105th birth anniversary of Sankaradas Swamigal, who attained such distinction and success that his fame remains established in the world of drama.

We are not holding this function because Sankaradas's spirit will come and watch it. We honour the great dead for our own benefit: to inspire ourselves for the future, to create courage for the work before us, and to strengthen our minds. That work must continue.

I do not, in fact, believe in spirits. Even among those who do, there are contradictions.

When a father dies, people say annual rites must be performed because his spirit remains and receives what we offer. The same people say he has already been reborn in another birth.

One must either reject rebirth or reject the continuing spirit.

## PDF 127 / printed p.126

If people come to a conclusion between the two, I may be ready to accept one. Until then, I am not ready to accept both.

If rebirth has occurred, how can the same spirit still remain to receive annual rites? I know where those offerings actually go.

Even today some notices say that a dead person has attained Sivaloka or Vaikuntha. It appears that even after death human beings cannot give up their desire for office and status. If everyone is assigned to such heavenly posts, who is left to go to hell?

We therefore avoid such confusion and say, “Some say it exists; some say it does not; that is not our worry.”

Sankaradas Swamigal has died and yet has not disappeared. He lives in his writings, songs and plays, and in the fame of the actors he created.

## PDF 128 / printed p.127

It is no ordinary work to remember those who served the country in many fields and to make others remember them. T. K. Shanmugam and others have undertaken that rare task. No one can deny the great service of the T. K. S. brothers in keeping Sankaradas Swamigal alive in the world of art and in public memory.

Through their efforts a statue of Sankaradas Swamigal was erected in Madurai some years ago, and a memorial function has continued every year.

At such functions, what matters is not who attends but what subject and memory the function carries forward. Some people here knew Sankaradas personally, some acted in his plays, some heard about him, and some have come simply to learn. I want the number in that last group to increase year after year.

I assure this forum of my support in making that happen.

## PDF 129 / printed p.128

We need not claim that Sankaradas created a modern revolution in drama fifty years ago. It is enough to recognise that, for his time, he saw the stage as a means to convey necessary counsel to the people and used it successfully.

I may differ from some of the subjects of his songs, but I have lasting respect for the Tamil that plays through them and for the Tamil feeling that moved him to write. That is why I accepted this invitation without hesitation.

Earlier generations laid down exact rules for how a stage should be built, where it should stand and how large it should be. Today it is difficult enough simply to keep drama alive; if all those old rules were imposed, even the few remaining plays might disappear. Yet their precision shows how carefully the art was once cultivated.

They even specified the quality of the ground on which a stage should stand.

## PDF 130 / printed p.129

Perhaps a Krishna-Leela required sweet soil, since Krishna eats earth and his mother sees the universe in his mouth! Today's stage expert can simply say “earth” and put sugar into the actor's mouth.

Everyone will agree that live theatre has declined.

Cinema was born as a child of drama. But instead of being merely a supporting offshoot, it has become a vigorous shoot of its own.

Film producers drew skilled actors from drama companies with higher salaries, and many theatre troupes closed. The T. K. S. brothers survived that storm.

A film can carry an idea to far more people in less time. If a play reaches a thousand, a film may reach ten thousand in the same period.

## PDF 131 / printed p.130

I am speaking of plays and films that try to convey ideas. Even in mythological and social drama, some companies of that period consciously used the stage to communicate principles; the T. K. S. company was one of them.

Acting for film is, in one sense, easier. A stage mistake cannot be retaken, whereas film can repeat a scene until it is correct. A stage actor who accidentally uses yesterday's Mahabharata character name while performing today's Ramayana has no second chance.

Nawab Rajamanickam Pillai used to say that on stage one must not only say, “I shall eat and return,” but also gesture it clearly so the last row understands. Cinema has the close-up, and can even create the appearance of speaking to another actor who is not present.

## PDF 132 / printed p.131

Drama has gradually adapted to a scientific age. Yet even in his own time Sankaradas Swamigal brought novelty into older forms and spread a new light in theatre.

Songwriters often placed a signature in their final line so others could not appropriate the song. Bharathidasan too used such signature lines. Sankaradas was different: he did not openly stamp his name, but cleverly wove it into the song.

K. B. Sundarambal made the song “Gnana Pazhathai Pizhindhu” famous. We remember her when we hear it and may forget the writer. I had often wondered who wrote those beautiful Tamil lines; only today did I learn that they were Sankaradas Swamigal's.

## PDF 133 / printed p.132

At the end he cleverly inserts his own name through a double meaning.

In another song he describes Narada—the trouble-maker of mythology—with the elegant Tamil expression “Kalagamuni,” the sage of discord.

We should continue to honour the fame of this great dramatist who could create such Tamil songs in that era.
