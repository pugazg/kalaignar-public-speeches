### Thiruvalluvar Function

Following Thai Pongal, in this sequence of Tamil festivals, we are celebrating Thiruvalluvar Day with pride.

On this day, we are presenting the Thiruvalluvar Award; holding the release function and address for the book *The Age of the Great Chola Kings*, prepared and published on behalf of the historical committee functioning under the leadership of C.E. Ramachandran as part of the history of Tamil Nadu; presenting prizes for outstanding books and honouring the fifty-six authors of those books, rather as fifty-six princes of fifty-six countries were honoured in olden times; and providing the monthly assistance given to four elderly Tamil scholars. I am greatly delighted to have received the opportunity to take part, along with all of you, in this distinguished and rare function.

When our Professor spoke about Sarangapani, who has received the Thiruvalluvar Award, he said that Sarangapani “is like a lamp placed inside a pot.” He added that that itself was good.

## PDF 35 / printed p.34

But because Sarangapani is one who has identified differences in interpretations, I did not merely move a little away from the Professor’s interpretation; I ran away from it! The lamp is indeed inside a pot, but that pot is made of glass. (loud applause) If it had been an earthen pot or a brass pot, the light would not be visible outside. Therefore, I thought we might say that, from today, we have placed him inside a glass pot.

From within such a glass pot he continues to give light. He is worthy of honour not merely because of the books of commentary he has written on the Tirukkural, and not merely because of his research books on the Tirukkural, but because, through his profound ability, he has studied and published many works of Sangam literature. When that is his stature, he said, “Today I have the good fortune of receiving this award from Kalaignar’s hand.” I say this not out of modesty but because it is true: I take pride that I have received the good fortune of presenting an award to Tamil scholars such as him. (applause) Such a fine function is taking place here.

As far as the Government of Tamil Nadu is concerned, from the time this government was formed in 1967 under the leadership of Perarignar Anna, it has carried out many good deeds for the growth of the language and the growth of Tamil culture. It has continued to carry out valuable work.

## PDF 36 / printed p.35

It was during the period when Anna assumed the responsibility of government and became Chief Minister that a long-standing dream of the Tamil people was realised. The demand was that the land in which we were born should no longer bear the name “Madras State,” but should be given the name that belonged to this land of Tamil—“Tamil Nadu.” Perarignar Anna fulfilled that demand.

Those who had argued that the name “Tamil Nadu” was unnecessary were made to stand in the Assembly. Anna rose and said, “I will say ‘Tamil Nadu’ three times; all of you must say ‘Long live! Long live!’” Thus even those who had argued against the need for the name Tamil Nadu were made to say “Long live! Long live!” That distinction too belongs to Perarignar Anna.

It was also during Anna’s time that, so that the rights of Tamils would not be damaged and so that a situation would not arise in the future in which the Tamil language was completely brought down, the declaration was made in the form of a clarion call that there would henceforth be no place for Hindi domination in Tamil Nadu and that Tamil and English alone would constitute the two-language policy. All of you know that this distinction belongs to the Kazhagam government formed under Anna’s leadership.

Following in Anna’s footsteps, on the basis of the service rendered by us—by me, the Professor and the other ministers—we have carried out many good measures for the growth of Tamil and the growth of Tamil culture,

## PDF 37 / printed p.36

and we continue to do so.

In particular, I do not wish to narrate the whole long record from 1967. We returned to office in 1996; I, in particular, assumed office as Chief Minister for the fourth time. In these two years, you should consider for a moment what all this government has done for the development of Tamil.

★ It was this government that created an entirely new ministry for Tamil Development and Culture and appointed a minister exclusively for it. (applause) That was something Tamil Nadu had not had until then.

★ After “Madras State,” that is, “Chennai Rajyam,” became Tamil Nadu, we felt that “Madras” or “Madaras” should not remain, and it was under this administration that the Tamil name “Chennai” was given. (applause)

Before 1976, *Poompuhar* was revived as far as possible—as a reflection of the *Silappathikaram*, as a treasury of Tamil history, and as historical testimony to the ancient city ruled by Karikala Peruvalathan. Though it was not possible to restore the old Poompuhar completely, this administration renewed it to the extent possible and said, “Behold this Poompuhar!” It was this administration that conducted the Indra festival there every year; you know that.

★ That festival later came to a halt. This government now has the desire to revive it. That is why, in the ruined Poompuhar, at a cost of one crore rupees,

renovation work is now under way. Once those works are fully completed, the Indra festival will be celebrated there in grand fashion. I convey that good news to you.

★ At the square on the seafront where scholar Anna rests, who nurtured such feelings, improvements are being made at a cost of one and a half crore rupees. A museum is being established.

★ Scholar Anna’s house in Kanchipuram has been renovated at a cost of four lakh rupees.

★ Valluvar Kottam—they spoke about it here. Award recipient Sarangapani spoke; Dr. Tamilkudimagan too explained it. Even without explanation, all of you know Valluvar Kottam. But all of you also know what Valluvar Kottam suffered during the five-year period. I am not saying this on the basis of *Murasoli*, or some other partisan paper. In neutral magazines such as *Kalki*, *Kumudam* and *Vikatan*, publications not tied to a political party, photographs were published showing the injustices taking place at Valluvar Kottam. You would have seen all those images. **You saw them; I saw them and shed tears.** With the feeling that Valluvar Kottam must be restored and must remain Valluvar *Kottam*, and must not become merely a “garden,”

## PDF 39 / printed p.38

it has today been improved at a cost of 84.94 lakh rupees; you can now see the result.

★ This government has provided financial assistance of 80,000 rupees to the Southern Languages Book Trust in Chennai.

★ This government has provided 43.75 lakh rupees to the International Institute of Tamil Studies in Chennai.

★ The Veerapandiya Kattabomman Fort at Panchalankurichi lies in a ruined state. Work to renovate that Kattabomman Fort is being carried out at a cost of 25 lakh rupees.

★ Five lakh rupees has been spent for the maintenance of the artistic treasures of the temple at Gangaikonda Cholapuram.

★ At the Arunachaleswarar Temple in Tiruvannamalai, one of the mandapams collapsed. Financial assistance of 50 lakh rupees has been given on behalf of this government. You ask whether we have devotion. It is because we have a commitment to art that this work is being carried out. (applause)

★ Under the heading of enhanced grant assistance by the Kazhagam government to the School of Dravidian Linguistics, the International School of Dravidian Linguistics at Thiruvananthapuram had been receiving five lakh rupees every year for research work related to Tamil studies. From 1996–97, that assistance has been increased to ten lakh rupees.

★ The Institute of Asian Studies has been given grants of 19.57 lakh rupees in 1996–97 and twenty lakh rupees in 1997–98 for research work relating to Tamil.

★ A grant of ten lakh rupees was given to the Tamil Sangam in New Delhi for construction of a Thiruvalluvar auditorium, and a few months ago I myself inaugurated that auditorium.

★ The books of Devaneya Pavanar were nationalised, and his heirs were given prize money of twenty lakh rupees.

★ This year, the books of Maraimalai Adigal were nationalised, and a total prize amount of thirty lakh and thirty thousand rupees was given to his descendants.

★ Bharathiyar’s house in Triplicane, Chennai, was renovated at a cost of 8.30 lakh rupees, and a library costing 80,000 rupees was established there by this government. A film on Bharathiyar’s life history is shown there three times a week.

★ For the development of the Madurai Tamil Sangam, accepting the requests of the Thevar Peravai, the Kazhagam government has provided financial assistance of 45 lakh rupees.

★ This government has given financial assistance of 50 lakh rupees to the Dravidian University in Andhra Pradesh.

★ The annual grant given to the Maraimalai Adigal Library, which had been ten thousand rupees, has been increased to one lakh rupees by this government.

## PDF 41 / printed p.40

★ It was also this government that, from this year, increased the grant to the U.Ve. Swaminatha Iyer Library at Thiruvanmiyur from 60,000 rupees to one lakh rupees.

★ Not only that. When I spoke about Kattabomman Fort, my friend Paramaguru, without even asking aloud, kept asking with his eyes, “What happened to Puli Thevan?”

Three months ago, our ministers Durai Murugan, Mullaivendhan and Aladi Aruna went and inspected the site. A plan was prepared to renovate Puli Thevan’s old ruined palace; I approved the drawing, and work has begun to build the Puli Thevan palace at a cost of 53 lakh rupees. (applause)

★ In memory of Veeran Sundaralingam, “Sundaralingam Nagar,” consisting of two hundred houses, has been established for his descendants to live in, at a cost of about sixty lakh rupees! (applause)

★ Likewise, the martyr and dramatist Viswanatha Das—the stage actor who raised the heroic cry against the white ruler, “Kokku parakkuthadi paappa,” and who died on the stage itself while acting—for that martyr Viswanatha Das, the house in which he lived at Tirumangalam in Madurai is being purchased, and a Viswanatha Das memorial hall is being established at a cost of 50 lakh rupees.

★ Our man of literary wealth Kumari Ananthan often keeps prodding me. In a manner comparable to the line, “Friend, amid the trouble I found happiness,” I accept that trouble and, near the Kamarajar Memorial,

## PDF 42 / printed p.41

the Rajaji Memorial Hall and Gandhi Mandapam at Guindy, a Martyrs’ Memorial Hall is being constructed at a cost of 30 lakh rupees. I convey that news here as well.

★ This government has provided 50,000 rupees to install a statue of U.Ve.Sa. at his library. Nobody even asked for it. I saw a report in the newspapers saying that those who wished to install the statue were unable to do so. An order was issued immediately, and the government provided 50,000 rupees for installing that statue.

★ In keeping with Bharathi’s words, “Go forth in all eight directions; bring and gather here all the treasures of art,” and with the feeling that the treasures of other languages should be translated into Tamil, the Kazhagam government introduced a new scheme from 1996–97 to help bring excellent translated works into Tamil. Five lakh rupees has been allocated for a programme to translate and publish works from other languages in Tamil and outstanding Tamil literary works in other languages.

★ Freedom fighters were being paid 400 rupees a month. From 10-9-96 the government increased it to 500 rupees. It did not stop there. From 10-10-96, in response to the request of many freedom fighters, the amount was raised to 1,500 rupees a month. In addition, a medical allowance of 15 rupees was also added by this government.

## PDF 43 / printed p.42

In connection with the golden jubilee year of Indian Independence, this assistance was increased further, and from 15-8-97 it was announced that freedom fighters would receive 3,000 rupees a month. The same 3,000 rupees paid to those freedom fighters is also being paid to the guardians of the Tamil language; more than a thousand Tamil language guardians receive 3,000 rupees every month.

I have mentioned these matters here in order to show that we have a duty of service toward everyone who has fostered Tamil in any field, and to tell you that we will continue to render this service to Tamil.

Our Professor of self-respect spoke here about all the greatness and distinction of Thiruvalluvar. Our elder Sarangapani—perhaps I should not say elder, for he is actually one year younger than I am; I said elder because he is great in Tamil (loud applause)—has written on differences in interpretation. While speaking, the Professor said that the fact that many people have written commentaries on the Tirukkural according to their own thought and understanding adds strength to the Tirukkural and enriches it.

Even in my own case, when it came to finding an interpretation for the Tirukkural, once, while I was speaking with revolutionary poet Bharathidasan, he said, “All the old commentaries

## PDF 44 / printed p.43

have been written wrongly, to a degree that cannot be accepted,” and he gave one example.

“தெய்வந் தொழாஅள் கொழுநன் தொழுதெழுவாள்
பெய்யெனப் பெய்யும் மழை.”

Before considering Bharathidasan’s explanation of this Kural, we must first see the explanation that had already been given for it.

The old explanation was this: a woman who does not even worship God, but rises after worshipping her husband, can say to the rain, at a time when no rain is falling, “Rain, fall,” and the rain will pour down.

Even in this age, the explanation written with that old way of thinking remains: “If a woman who does not even worship God but rises worshipping her husband says so, rain will fall.” That cannot be tested. Suppose we bring a woman and say, “Tell the rain to fall,” and that lady looks at the sky and says, “Rain, come,” but the rain does not come. We must think of all the ways in which the affection we have for that woman might then change. Doubt itself would arise! That is why Bharathidasan said: that is not the meaning; it has been written wrongly. When it says “rain that falls when one says ‘fall’,” it means this: when a farmer, needing water for his field, pond and lake, longs and wonders, “Will it not rain?”, and at the very moment when he wishes that it should rain,

## PDF 45 / printed p.44

the rain falls—such is that woman. The woman who does not even worship God but worships her husband is comparable to rain that falls at the moment one wishes for it; it does not mean that rain will actually fall merely because she commands it. That is what Bharathidasan said.

That is the same meaning I referred to when I created *Kuraloviyam*. That was during the period when I wrote *Kuraloviyam*.

Later I wrote an actual commentary on the Tirukkural. With a slight change from the interpretation given by the revolutionary poet, what I wrote was this. In those days—and why, even today—many women who praise and honour the Tirukkural point to some Kurals about women and say, “Whatever may be said, has not Thiruvalluvar portrayed women as slaves?” My thought was that Valluvar should not be made subject to that accusation. Therefore, when I interpreted the Kural containing “பெய்யெனப் பெய்யும் மழை,” I wrote: the woman who does not even worship God but rises worshipping her husband is like rain that falls the moment it is told to fall—she is in a state of servitude. A woman should possess rights; she should not live in a condition of slavery. I wrote such an interpretation in order to bring out that point.

In this way, from the time of Parimelazhagar’s commentary up to the time of revolutionary poet Bharathidasan,

## PDF 46 / printed p.45

and even after that, interpretations have changed at the hands of different commentators. Even so, Thiruvalluvar belongs to everyone. He is praised by everyone. He is such a figure that everyone can say, “He belongs to me.” People of every religion can point to him and say their religious ideas are there: Jains say Jain ideas are there; Saivites say Saivite ideas are there; Buddhists say Buddhist ideas are there; Vaishnavites say Vaishnavite ideas are there. To such an extent, Thiruvalluvar is honoured by people of every religion.

It was for that Thiruvalluvar that, even before 1976, this government deliberated and decided that a statue comparable in scale to some of the great statues of the world should be erected. A plan was made to erect a statue of Valluvar on the seashore at Kanyakumari. It was announced even then, and work began. The stones for the statue were selected. After the government was dismissed in 1976, however, the work stopped without being completed. Later, after my dear friend M.G.R. assumed office, he thought of continuing that work. It was taken up, but delay set in; more and more delay followed.

Now, during this period in which we have returned to office for the fourth time, most of the work on Valluvar’s statue has nearly been completed. Very soon that statue will be installed on the Kanyakumari seashore. (applause) A 133-foot-high statue of Thiruvalluvar will be installed there—

## PDF 47 / printed p.46

I convey this happy news at this Thiruvalluvar function.

I wish to say one thing, not merely for humour. From the time our Tamilkudimagan assumed responsibility as minister for this department, he has been working admirably to foster Tamil and secure influence for Tamil, and likewise, in the Hindu Religious and Charitable Endowments Department, to prevent looting in temples and to recover temple properties from those who have appropriated them.

There is a Kural of Valluvar:

“சொல்லுதல் யார்க்கும் எளிய அரியவாம்
சொல்லிய வண்ணம் செயல்”

“To speak is easy for anyone; rare indeed is action exactly as one has spoken.”

That Kural comes to my mind now because I read an article by Tamilkudimagan in a weekly magazine. He will not get angry. He will listen carefully without irritation, thinking, “After all, he is my elder brother.”

I read it in a weekly. Because I want you to enjoy the pleasure I received, I have torn out that very page and brought it with me. (loud laughter) It was in the magazine *Rani*. See what he says there; look at his yearning.

“Yes! Tamils living in Singapore and Malaysia celebrate ‘Pongal festival’ specifically as ‘Tamil Festival Day’. Because they have the unavoidable need to live together and progress among the majority communities, the Chinese and the Malays, it is no surprise that the Tamils there celebrate it as ‘Tamil Festival Day’. Yes, Tamils believe that they must not lose their identity and that preserving it is itself a mark of worth. Therefore they celebrate Pongal with full enthusiasm.

## PDF 48 / printed p.47

“Does this feeling exist in Tamil Nadu? It does. It exists only in `Sitrarppuzham` [source form: `சிற்றார்ப்புழத்தில்`]. From the first day of Margazhi itself, the women of Sitrarppuzham become ready to celebrate Pongal.

“`Aniyam` means that they become prepared. That is, on the very first day of Margazhi they become ready to celebrate Thai Pongal.”

Do you know how our Tamilkudimagan, who wrote such an article, begins the article?

“Dawn broke; the birds cried out.
Breaking my sleepiness, I rose. As usual—
only afterwards did I remember that today was Pongal
Day. When that memory came, I sprang up.
This indeed is ‘Tamil Festival Day’. I rose
thinking that it must be celebrated.”

He says that all the village women apparently know from the very first day of Margazhi itself that Pongal is going to come. (loud laughter) But he alone, on Pongal morning, apparently rose while breaking his sleepiness, and only afterwards remembered that it was Pongal! (loud laughter)

I did not sleep the whole night. I thought, “Tomorrow morning is Pongal; I must get up early, wear new clothes, welcome all those who come and offer them greetings,” and so I stayed awake all night—

## PDF 49 / printed p.48

I did not sleep. But he apparently got up in the morning as usual, stretching himself lazily.

If even Tamilkudimagan, who is fostering Tamil, is like this! (loud applause, laughter) Only afterwards did he remember that it was Pongal Day. Only after seeing the sun did he remember it. I say this only as a joke. I told him the same thing. That very morning he came to my house to offer Pongal greetings. As soon as he arrived, I asked him, “What, did you get up at dawn stretching yourself lazily?” He laughed. He said, “Anna, I happened to write it that way; please leave it.” I am not going to leave it. One Tamilkudimagan may be corrected privately—but should we not correct all these Tamil people too? (loud applause)

Therefore all of you must know that Pongal Day is coming from the previous day itself. Only if you know it beforehand can you clean away all the dirt in the house. That is Bhogi—the festival of *removing* and casting away! All the dirt, rubbish and refuse must be cleared. In the same way, all the blind and obsolete customs that have spread through Tamil homes, the Tamil country and Tamil society must be removed. That is the Pongal we must celebrate. Therefore I ask all of you to wake up in advance.
