# Inauguration of Salem Periyar University

Karunanidhi opens by describing Salem as being in a festive mood from the morning. He says he had authorised only three lakh rupees for the arrangements, asking organisers to keep expenditure restrained, but the visible scale is greater because colleges and schools across the surrounding three districts voluntarily formed committees and contributed arches, decorations, garlands, lighting and other arrangements.

He connects this civic participation with the government's **“நமக்கு நாமே”** and **“அண்ணா மறுமலர்ச்சித் திட்டம்”** schemes, arguing that people should take responsibility for common needs and improvements. He praises Veerapandi Arumugam for carrying tasks through energetically and points to Salem's organisation of the day as an example of collective effort.

Karunanidhi says the procession deeply impressed him. He and other ministers watched for about three hours, while the Governor watched for part of it. The discipline, enthusiasm and sense of purpose among students reminded him of a recent anti-untouchability human chain in Madurai involving students and teachers over roughly fifteen kilometres. Seeing comparable discipline in Salem, he says, doubled his confidence in the younger generation.

He was especially moved by the sight of an estimated lakh of students carrying flags bearing Periyar's image. Recalling his own time as a student of Periyar in Erode in 1945, he says he could scarcely have imagined that Periyar's ideas would one day receive such acceptance among future leaders, thinkers and scientists. These, he stresses, were not party flags but symbols of the Self-Respect movement.

Karunanidhi quotes Bharathidasan's praise of Periyar, including the line **“தூயதாடி நெஞ்சில் விழும்”**, and says Periyar's rationalism, courage and relentless attack on caste and religious inequality are now acknowledged far beyond Tamil Nadu. In his view, social-justice discourse across India increasingly invokes Periyar as an intellectual guide.

He argues that naming the university after Periyar is therefore especially appropriate in Salem. He then places the institution in a longer educational history. The University of Madras began in 1857, when the old **“Madras Presidency”** covered a much larger region and its head of government was called the **“Premier.”** With the new Salem institution, he says, Tamil Nadu now has fifteen universities.

Karunanidhi lists universities established under DMK governments, including Manonmaniam Sundaranar University, the agricultural university in Coimbatore, the veterinary university in Chennai, and the M. G. R. Medical University. He praises Kamaraj for opening primary schools widely, then argues that the DMK sought to ensure that students emerging from rural schools also had access to colleges.

He gives a series of comparative figures for colleges opened under different administrations and distinguishes regular colleges, self-financing arts and science colleges, and engineering colleges. He says that recent approvals for additional engineering colleges have created places for about **10,000** more students.

A major theme is rural access. Karunanidhi says the government required **15 percent** of places in engineering colleges to be allotted to rural students, a policy that survived legal challenge. He also describes a scheme for first-generation graduates: where a student is the first graduate in a family, top performers in professional courses can receive not merely prize money but government support for further higher professional education.

He presents these educational measures as ways of carrying forward Periyar's principles and Anna's ideals: education opens the eyes, enables ideas to take root, and allows society to progress.

Karunanidhi contrasts Periyar's preference for social reform over electoral politics with Annadurai's decision to build a political movement without abandoning Periyar's principles. He cites measures associated with that tradition: legal recognition of Self-Respect marriages, the two-language policy, renaming Madras State as Tamil Nadu, equal property rights for women based on Periyar's 1929 Chengalpattu resolution, and **30 percent** reservation for women in local-body elections.

Veerapandi Arumugam then asks that Periyar's ideas be incorporated into the university curriculum. Karunanidhi responds that this will certainly be done. Arumugam also seeks **₹10 crore** for the first phase of construction. Karunanidhi answers not merely that it will be considered, but emphatically that it **will happen**, and concludes by wishing the Periyar University continued growth.
