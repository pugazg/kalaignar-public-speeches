### T.N. Rajarathinam Pillai Centenary Celebration

Our Sangeetha Kalanidhi Semmangudi, in the closing part of his speech, compared Muthiah Bhagavathar and Sankara Narayanan and narrated a small story, an incident. In the art of speaking, if one wishes, he may be Sankaranarayanan. But in the art of music he is Muthiah Bhagavathar and I am Sankara Narayanan — I am duty-bound to remind him of that. (Applause.)

The musical genius, the emperor of the nadaswaram, T.N. Rajarathinam, who was born into this world one hundred years ago on 27 August 1898, left us in his fifty-eighth year. He passed away in 1956. Our `Hamsadhwani` secretary Ramachandran took the initiative and made efforts because he wanted the centenary celebration of the man born a hundred years ago to be held on that very specified date. When he

## PDF 257 / printed p.256

came to ask me for a date, he specifically pointed this out and said that the function had to be held on this very day. It was not merely a question of fulfilling his wish; since I too had that wish, I had the opportunity to agree to this function being held on this very day.

Even now, at Valluvar Kottam, the centenary celebration of Omandur Ramasamy Reddiar is taking place. But a centenary celebration that ought to have taken place in 1995 is taking place only in 1998, after a delay of three years. When functions are thus held years late, even his own family may not have thought that not even a delay of days should occur and that this function must take place on the very same date. What our secretary Ramachandran, who belongs to that musical family, has done deserves appreciation.

I am one of those who knew T.N. Rajarathinam not merely in the way everyone knew him, but very closely. Our Semmangudi has spoken here in great detail about the friendship, relationship and association he had with T.N.R. in the field of music. He mentioned that until Rajarathinam actually arrived at a function, the organisers would remain doubtful.

Even in politics, our leader Anna would similarly keep those who organised functions

## PDF 258 / printed p.257

waiting. While all of us would worry, wondering, “Will he come? Will he not come?”, he would arrive late. Sometimes he would not come at all. I have often reflected on this and told friends that, just as Anna was like that in politics in the early days, Rajarathinam was like that in the field of music.

But, as Semmangudi pointed out, however many people may have waited and been disappointed, if Rajarathinam came to the next function and displayed his musical ability for just five minutes, they would forget everything. In the same way, if Anna failed to attend one function but came to the next and spoke for five minutes, his younger brothers would forget all the anger they had felt because he had not come.

We have listened with delight and rejoiced in our hearts when Anna praised T.N. Rajarathinam. In Chennai city there was a very close friend of Anna named S.S.P. Lingam, a man devoted to our movement. An ear-piercing ceremony was held at his home. Rajarathinam played the nayanam there. He had said that he would play for only one hour, but the performance went on for more than three hours.

## PDF 259 / printed p.258

At that function, after blessing the children, Anna referred to T.N.R.'s performance and said:

“When Westerners play an orchestra, the musical pleasure heard when each instrument is played is one thing; the musical pleasure heard when all of them are played together is another. Likewise, when Rajarathinam's playing is heard from a distance, at times it sounds like the music of a flute. At times it seems like the nayanam. At times it sounds like the music of an orchestra. No other musical genius in the world possesses this ability; Rajarathinam alone has it.”

That is what Anna said at that function.

Rajarathinam had the distinction of bringing together the sounds of all those instruments in an orderly way and presenting them in a manner that gave joy and could be relished. He was born into an ordinary middle-class family. In those days, musical families were ordinary, modest families; they were not great wealthy families. Not only that: it was a period when that community did not enjoy great standing or respect. If others were to speak of this it might be considered improper; since I speak of it myself, it will not be improper.

## PDF 260 / printed p.259

However great a vidwan one might be, he could not participate in a performance wearing a shirt. To say even more, he could not even attend wearing an upper cloth. Whether they were great nayanam vidwans, thavil vidwans, or anyone else, according to the custom of those days all of them had to wear a kudumi.

He changed all of this. “Can the nayanam be played only if one wears a kudumi? Look, I will play it with a crop haircut,” he said, and played the nayanam with a crop haircut. He asked, “If I play the nayanam wearing a shirt, will that tone not come from the nayanam? Can alapana not be done? Does alapana reside in one's clothes?” and he played the nayanam wearing a shirt. At a time when one was not even supposed to wear footwear, he played the nayanam wearing boots on his feet. Not only that: when he played the nayanam in the durbars of maharajas, he dressed like a maharaja, wore a turban like theirs, wore a sherwani like theirs, put on fine ornamental clothes, and sat on the stage as their equal. I am duty-bound to say here, with great pride and satisfaction, that this distinction belonged to T.N. Rajarathinam.

## PDF 261 / printed p.260

How do I know about this inequality? I have mentioned it myself once while speaking at the Music Academy. As our elder Semmangudi pointed out, when I was young I too learned singing, and my father placed me in a relative's house so that I could continue and become a nayanam vidwan.

But after about three months I did not continue that training. I returned home, in the village of Thirukkuvalai, and said that I could no longer undergo that training. My father angrily asked why. I said: “The vidwan who trains me is another great vidwan in this village, like you. When a man who works as a `karvar` at the monastery in this village comes along the street, the vidwan takes the cloth from his shoulder and ties it around his waist. He removes the sandals from his feet, sets them aside, stands and bows. If I too become a great vidwan, will I not have to do this very thing? Therefore I cannot continue this training.” Only after that was I sent to Tiruvarur to study.

Otherwise, I do not know whether I too might today have received from our Semmangudi the praise that I was a good and distinguished nayanam vidwan. I say this because such a sense of self-respect

## PDF 262 / printed p.261

arose in me in my youth. I say it for no other reason than that Thiruvavaduthurai T.N. Rajarathinam had the distinction of sharpening that feeling, putting it into practice, demonstrating it through his conduct, and making other vidwans follow that path.

It was during the period when he moved through Tamil Nadu as a great vidwan that Raja Sir Annamalai Chettiar, Professor Kalki, statesman Rajaji, Thanthai Periyar, Thiru. Vi. Kalyanasundaranar, Perarignar Anna and others decided that Tamil music must be given respect in Tamil Nadu and that Tamil music must be spread, and they engaged in a campaign for it.

Among those who were of great help and support to that cause in those days, no one can deny that the late M.K. Thyagaraja Bhagavathar, Dandapani Desikar and T.N. Rajarathinam Pillai were foremost.

T.N.R. said many times, with anguish: “In a concert, in a musical performance, instead of saying, ‘Sing a Tamil song,’ should the standing of Tamil songs and Tamil sahityam fall so low that people say, ‘Sing one `uruppadi`, sing one `sillarai` piece’?”

We have seen this many years ago: after listening to a concert, when people came back and spoke about

## PDF 263 / printed p.262

the concert the next day, they would say, “He sang well. At the end he sang two or three `uruppadi`; those too were good.” What does `uruppadi` mean? When we count out clothes to a washerman, we say how many `uruppadi` we have given him: “I gave ten `uruppadi`; bring back all ten `uruppadi` properly.” It was in that same way that Tamil songs had come to be called `uruppadi`.

Perhaps Tamil songs were given that name because they alone were the worthwhile `uruppadi`; I do not know. The man who angrily opposed this in those days, who acted with the determination that it had to be corrected, and who put that correction into practice, was the late Thiruvavaduthuraiyar. When I think of that, I feel proud.

Once I had the good fortune to travel with him from Chidambaram to Kanchipuram. You know that Chidambaram Jayaraman, the Isai Chithar, was my brother-in-law. In 1944 I married his younger sister Padmavathi. In 1945, at Kanchipuram, Arignar Anna and a friend named Ponnappar together conducted a music festival. The name of that

## PDF 264 / printed p.263

event was the Dravidian Music Festival. The programmes went on for about ten days. T.N.R. had been invited to participate. C.S. Jayaraman too had been invited. I also happened to travel with him to that event. Although I did not have the ability to participate fully in the musical discussions the two of them carried on throughout the journey, I enjoyed listening to them again and again.

On that one-day journey I had the opportunity to travel with T.N.R. as far as Kanchipuram. He moved to Chennai. While he was living in the Adyar area of Chennai, I had frequent opportunities to meet him. As our elders here have pointed out, the Independence Day celebration began with his mangala music. Likewise, the first State Conference of the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam at Tiruchi began with his nayanam music. When we think of this, as our Nalli Kuppusamy pointed out, we can see that in this country in the fifty years of independence, T.N.R.'s nayanam music was used to open two great events.

In his name, an award called `Rajarathina` is being presented every year today by the Muthamizh Peravai.

## PDF 265 / printed p.264

In the year he died, a ceremony to unveil his portrait was held at R.R. Sabha under the chairmanship of our dear C. Subramaniam. At that time I was in the opposition benches. C. Subramaniam was a minister. At that function I made a request to him. I also made a request to those who organised the function. Just as the Bharat Ratna award is presented to eminent persons, I asked that a `Rajarathina` award be presented to musical geniuses.

My request was not rejected then, but it was not implemented. Only after I became Chief Minister did we decide, on behalf of the Muthamizh Peravai, that a `Rajarathna` award should be presented to outstanding masters of music, and all of you know very well that it has been presented since then.

Even now, a building for the Muthamizh Peravai is to be established in Chennai. At a Muthamizh Peravai function I stated that the building would be named “Rajarathinam Memorial Hall.” I wish to state here that this will be fulfilled and that efforts toward it have already been undertaken. (Loud applause.)

## PDF 266 / printed p.265

Our `Hamsadhwani` secretary Ramachandran placed a request. In the concluding part of his welcome address, he asked that some 40 plots at Taramani be given to them. Perhaps, because the name itself can be split as `thara mani`, he thought they would give it; I do not know.

All the land allotted in the Taramani area has been set aside for various industries that are to be run either by the government or as joint ventures. I will examine the matter. I will examine it with the Industries Secretary and also with the Chief Secretary. He asked me to give an assurance from the stage. The assurance I can give from the stage is that I will certainly examine it. I do not dare give any other assurance now, because if I make a promise without knowing what the matter is and later fail to fulfil it, it may turn into something like certain people's election-time promises.

Our friend Nalli Kuppusamy Chettiar said, “A university must be established for music.” Whether a separate university is necessary solely for music is a matter for consideration, because even now some universities have separate departments for music.

## PDF 267 / printed p.266

In particular, at Annamalai University, for which our M.A.M. is responsible, there is a music department in which more than a hundred students are receiving training.

Dandapani Desikar has already served as the head of that music department. Chittoor Subramaniam Pillai has served as its head. M.L. Vasanthakumari has served as its head. Sirkazhi Govindarajan has served as its head. Thus it continues to function.

Whether a separate university is now necessary for this — and, even if the facilities and opportunities to create such a university were provided, how it would function — are matters that need to be examined. If it must be established no matter what, then if the generous donor Nalli Chettiar himself establishes a university (Applause — laughter), I am ready to attend both its foundation-stone-laying ceremony and its inauguration. I say this here, and I also note that our Semmangudi has spoken here about T.N.R.'s greatness, distinction and good qualities, while at the same time pointing out both sides of his life.

Tamilkudimagan spoke here as well. Semmangudi is ninety years old. From the position of a father, from the position of a grandfather,

## PDF 268 / printed p.267

he can speak about all these matters here. I too am seventy-five. Even so, I continue to speak to those younger than I am. We must think about this.

T.N.R. was a man who ought to have lived for many more years before passing away. If he died at the age of fifty-eight without even having a Mani Vizha, what was the reason? Likewise, when do you think Kalaivanar N.S.K. died? Kalaivanar N.S.K. died at the age of forty-nine, before even a golden-jubilee celebration could take place.

We must think about the reason for all this. We foster music, we become masters, we give joy to everyone. But we must find within our own bodies the strength needed for that. We must not destroy ourselves. If we realise that destroying ourselves means destroying art, destroying society, and reducing with our own hands the service that we ought to render to this society, no one will engage in actions that destroy oneself.

Especially in the world of music — and particularly nayanam artists — they have to struggle greatly to obtain that musical training. They have to struggle greatly to take part in those musical performances.

## PDF 269 / printed p.268

Therefore they need something else for that fatigue to subside. It should be used, as doctors say, as a medicine; if it is used as a feast instead (Applause), we will be forced to see outcomes like these.

I had been thinking of saying this. I sat wondering whether I should say it. But our elder Semmangudi gave me courage by offering that advice to all musical geniuses. Growing musicians and music artists, as well as musicians who have already grown and established themselves, should follow it not for themselves but for society and for the country. In this way, I ask that T.N.R.'s image should be a lesson to all of us. Long live the fame of T.N. Rajarathinam! With that, I take my leave.
