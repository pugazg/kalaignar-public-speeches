## PDF 121 / printed p.120

### 16

## Kalaivanar

Just as we are holding a Kalaivanar festival today, this same committee will conduct one every year.

At those functions, suitable honours and purses will be presented to senior artists and colleagues who worked closely with Kalaivanar.

What Kalaivanar did for this country continues to live on the screen of our memory.

After the World Tamil Conference, the late S. S. Vasan and A. V. M. Chettiar, both members of the procession committee, asked me what should be done with its surplus funds. I suggested, “Why not erect a statue of Kalaivanar?”

Within ten days they produced the statue splendidly.

It is almost as though Perarignar Anna resolved not to leave us until he had unveiled that statue: the Kalaivanar statue unveiling was his last public function.

## PDF 122 / printed p.121

Likewise, Kalaivanar's own last public function was the unveiling of Arignar Anna's portrait in Salem district.

Many speakers have recalled incidents from Kalaivanar's life.

Do not think that I recount events involving me out of self-praise, or because those who are gone cannot return to contradict me. I am referring to what Kalaivanar himself wrote in an article in his own hand.

The film *Manamagal* was produced by Kalaivanar and directed by Krishnan–Panju. I wrote its dialogue.

One day I sat in my room beneath a fan, writing scenes rapidly. The wind blew the sheets I had written onto the floor. Because stopping to retrieve them would break my writing, I kept going.

After two or three scenes I noticed that all the fallen pages had been neatly gathered and stacked on the table. I turned around to see who had done it. Kalaivanar was standing there.

Startled, I asked, “Should you be doing this?”

## PDF 123 / printed p.122

He replied, “For a long time I have wanted to watch you write. Today that wish has been fulfilled!”

He later recorded this incident in an article for the souvenir marking the hundredth performance of *Manimagudam*.

Under the support of such a generous Kalaivanar, Udumalai Narayana Kavi conveyed rare ideas through his songs. We cannot forget Nadippisai Pulavar K. R. Ramasamy, who wanted such a poet to be encouraged with a purse. When I visited Ramasamy as he fought for life in Vellore hospital, that was among his final wishes; today's function gives it form.

This is an example of how Anna's successors will honour those who worked to spread our principles.

Together, Kalaivanar and Udumalai Narayana Kavi produced a revolution of ideas in Tamil.

In the old film *Uthamaputhiran*, Kalaivanar appears as an Iyer who falls in love with an Adi-Dravidar woman. The film uses the resulting conflict to create a situation in which she is finally married to an upper-caste man.

Social reform is easy to speak of today. Kalaivanar had the courage to insert such scenes then.

## PDF 124 / printed p.123

Here is another example of his delightful humour.

A father is away; his daughter is inside; a young man played by Kalaivanar wants to approach her.

Kalaivanar tells the father that he will go inside and obtain a mango from his daughter. The father agrees and calls to his daughter, “He will come in; give him one.”

Inside, Kalaivanar asks the young woman for a kiss. She refuses.

He calls out, “Your daughter says she won't give it!”

The father replies, “Give him one!”—and Kalaivanar gets the kiss.

What kind of humour is this? Such humour could arise only in Kalaivanar's mind.

Even when he acted in a mythological film, viewers did not leave as devotees but as rationalists; that was the force of his ideas.

In *Poompavai*, Nadippisai Pulavar appears as Thirugnanasambandar. The story says Sambandar sang at the age of five.

Wondering how a five-year-old child could sing, Kalaivanar mocked the claim in a song within the film itself.

## PDF 125 / printed p.124

The rationalist song written by Udumalai Narayana Kavi—“The world will praise the one who perceived truth and saw the greatness of the people”—once rang from public stages.

Even now, though he is seventy, he can compose love songs like an eighteen-year-old.

We have presented a purse to such a senior artist. The gift may be modest, but he is generous enough to regard it as great.

We erected a statue to Kalaivanar; today we honour Udumalai Narayana Kavi, who worked with him.

However great the events and however eminent the participants, functions here are still described merely as functions at the Children's Theatre. Therefore, from tomorrow, this hall will be called **Kalaivanar Arangam**.
