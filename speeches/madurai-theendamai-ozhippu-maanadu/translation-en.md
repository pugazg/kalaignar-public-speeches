# Madurai Untouchability Eradication Conference

Karunanidhi opens by thanking the political leaders, religious figures, officials and organisers who participated in a day of anti-untouchability activities in Madurai: a human chain in the morning, the unveiling of a statue of Kakkan, communal meals at the Meenakshi temple, a church and a mosque, and the conference itself.

He says the gathering has functioned more like a serious seminar than a conventional political conference. Fifty years after independence, he argues, sections of society are still struggling for another kind of freedom — freedom from caste oppression and untouchability.

Responding to Ilayaperumal's proposal, Karunanidhi announces that peace and communal-harmony committees will be formed across Tamil Nadu within a week, at district and taluk levels, involving MPs, MLAs and other elected representatives. Their task is to build peace and intervene before local tensions become violence.

He then turns to reservation in public employment. Karunanidhi recalls raising the Scheduled Caste reservation from 16 to 18 per cent after becoming Chief Minister in 1969, and later distinguishing an additional one per cent for Scheduled Tribes, making the combined allocation 19 per cent. He acknowledges the frustration that reserved posts have still remained vacant.

The government, he says, has begun a special recruitment drive to fill such vacancies. Where qualified intermediate teachers are unavailable, rules have been relaxed to permit B.Ed.-trained candidates, and thirteen intermediate teacher-training institutes have recently been established at a cost of five crore rupees. He also announces a monitoring committee, convened by the Adi-Dravidar Welfare Minister and including Adi-Dravidar representatives from parties in the Legislative Assembly, to supervise the filling of reserved posts within a defined period.

Karunanidhi supports the suggestion that an anti-untouchability pledge should be repeated in schools and colleges. He cites the morning's human chain, in which large numbers of students and others stood for roughly thirteen kilometres — and, he says, would have extended to around twenty-five kilometres if arranged in a single line — as evidence that the pledge can take root among younger people. He says similar conferences and human chains should be held in every district headquarters.

The speech then draws on a passage from a volume of Ambedkar's writings and speeches concerning Swami Anand Tirth's experiences in Madurai district in 1952. Karunanidhi recounts discriminatory tea-shop practices, including serving Adi-Dravidars in coconut shells, a court case that resulted in only a ten-rupee fine, and a later violent assault when equal service in glasses was demanded. His point is that caste discrimination remained severe even several years after independence.

Quoting Ambedkar's argument that inter-caste marriage is a fundamental means of breaking caste, Karunanidhi says communal dining alone is insufficient unless social relationships themselves change. He refers to inter-caste marriages in his own family as grounds for speaking with personal commitment on the subject.

He then announces an enhancement to the Anjugam Ammaiyar Memorial Inter-Caste Marriage Assistance Scheme. The existing assistance had risen from 5,000 to 10,000 rupees; where either the bride or groom is from an Adi-Dravidar or Scheduled Tribe community, the assistance will now be 20,000 rupees. He also notes the broader marriage-assistance programme for poor women.

Karunanidhi closes by saying that Madurai's message — “Let us eradicate untouchability” — should spread across the State. Similar human chains, communal meals and anti-untouchability conferences are to be organised in other district headquarters, and the true success of the conference will be measured only when untouchability itself disappears.
