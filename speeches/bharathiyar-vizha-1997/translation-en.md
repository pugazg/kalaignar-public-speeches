# Bharathiyar Function

Karunanidhi says that, as T. K. S. Kalaivanan had noted, he is attending a Bharathi celebration after roughly twenty-seven years, though he has spoken about Bharathi and Bharathidasan at many poetry gatherings in the intervening period.

He begins with a humorous complaint about the small attendance. Responding to Kalki Rajendran's explanation that office-goers may be sitting at home watching television, Karunanidhi says that better publicity and less excessive thrift could fill not merely the present hall but even larger venues. He jokes that every person present can be counted as a thousand, ten thousand, or even a lakh of listeners.

Two requests made from the stage lead into policy discussions. The first concerns linking India's rivers. Karunanidhi recalls earlier Cauvery negotiations involving Karnataka Chief Minister Veerendra Patil, Kerala Chief Minister Achutha Menon, and Union Irrigation Minister K. L. Rao. He recounts Rao's explanation of the immense electricity required to pump Ganga water over elevation differences and says he had suggested, at least as an intermediate step, linking the Godavari and Cauvery. He adds that the immediate task remains resolving the Cauvery dispute.

The second request concerns Tamil as the language of administration and instruction. Karunanidhi recalls Annadurai's assurance that Tamil would become the medium of instruction and official language, his own later attempt to implement Tamil as a medium of instruction, the large student agitation that followed, and a committee headed by A. L. Mudaliar that helped calm the dispute by making Tamil-medium study optional while offering incentives.

He argues that circumstances have changed and that support for Tamil as a medium of instruction is now much broader. He says the government will proceed step by step but firmly toward a stronger role for Tamil. At the same time, he explains the complication created by the Union's language structure: simply removing English while Hindi remains dominant could strengthen Hindi. His preferred national arrangement is for all constitutionally recognised Indian languages ultimately to share official status, with Tamil capable of taking such a role immediately.

Karunanidhi says a separate ministry and minister exist specifically to advance Tamil, its culture, and its use in administration. Responding to poet Andal Priyadarshini's request, he announces support for a Bharathi award for young poets and directs the Tamil Development and Culture Minister, Tamilkudimagan, to take up the matter.

He then addresses the relationship between Bharathi and the Dravidian movement. Although the movement did not always accept Bharathi without reservation, Karunanidhi says Bharathi's greatness lies in the fact that those once critical of him have come to recognise the force of his poetry and ideas. Bharathidasan himself, despite his later rationalist and Self-Respect commitments, had begun with devotional poetry and took the name “Bharathidasan” out of admiration for Bharathi.

Karunanidhi quotes Bharathidasan's praise of Bharathi as a poet born to answer the needs of Tamil society and as a force against caste, oppression, and intellectual sleep. He also cites Somasundara Bharathi's recollection that Bharathi displayed extraordinary poetic ability from childhood.

Turning to Bharathi's life, Karunanidhi discusses his family connections with Sevalapperi and Ettayapuram, his poverty, the death of his mother Lakshmi, his father's second marriage, and his upbringing under his stepmother Valliammal. He recalls the story of Bharathi growing his distinctive moustache while studying in Kasi after fellow students teased him for repeatedly shaving.

Karunanidhi also responds to criticism of the Dravidian movement's history under British rule. He distinguishes political freedom from social liberation and cites M. P. Sivagnanam's argument that the Justice movement worked for social emancipation while nationalists fought colonial rule. He points out that Congress leaders too sometimes formally welcomed British royalty and quotes Bharathi's own journalistic comments about the Prince and Princess of Wales, including his praise of the princess's simplicity.

He notes Gokhale's welcome to the royal visitors at the 1905 Kasi Congress and argues that historical episodes of formal welcome should not erase later commitment to freedom. He recalls Annadurai's refusal to call 15 August 1947 a day of mourning, despite Periyar's position, because the departure of British rule was itself worth celebrating.

Karunanidhi then quotes Bharathi's rejection of countless gods and criticises the irony of turning Bharathi himself into an object of worship. He stresses Bharathi's social reformism: cutting off his tuft, discarding the sacred thread, growing a moustache, and performing an upanayanam for Kanakalingam, an Adi Dravidar youth. Bharathi, he says, treated national liberation and social reform as two eyes that could not be separated.

The closing section returns to caste and religious conflict. Karunanidhi invokes Bharathi's question about whether caste and religious quarrels have actually disappeared and connects it with the Sangam ideal **“யாதும் ஊரே யாவரும் கேளிர்”** — all places are our own and all people are kin. He laments contemporary killings, burnings, caste hatred and religious hatred, saying that the golden jubilee of freedom risks becoming a “wound jubilee” rather than a true golden jubilee.

He concludes that Bharathi's work, songs, service and humane spirit are still needed.
