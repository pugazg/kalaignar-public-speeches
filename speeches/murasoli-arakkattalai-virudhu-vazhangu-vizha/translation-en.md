### Murasoli Trust Award Presentation Function

At this fine function, at a time when, on behalf of the Murasoli Trust, awards are being presented to director Sami, journal editor Saavi, and poet Erode Tamilanban and they are being honoured, our Kavikko Abdul Rahman said, “I will make a reservation for myself; as a poet, I will speak mostly only about Tamilanban. I leave the other two places to Tamilkudimagan.” Having allotted them as he pleased and gone away, this gentleman, as he pleased, took up two constituencies for himself and spoke after imagining that one constituency had been allotted to Abdul Rahman.

My plight is that, whoever allots whichever constituency, I am the one who has to go to every constituency (loud laughter, applause); in that way, I have to speak about all these constituencies.

## PDF 20 / printed p.19

Here, in a physically weakened state, with tears in his eyes but at the same time with joy welling up in his heart, A.S.A. Sami came to this stage and received the award and the purse of one lakh rupees presented on behalf of the Murasoli Trust. He lives in Tirupattur in North Arcot district. Wishing that he should come here, we were engaged in efforts to bring him and informed him of this announcement. Though he came with the desire to come and see all of us, the journey left him weakened and physically exhausted, and today he was admitted to a hospital in Chennai. However much we told him, “You need not come here; take rest,” he would not listen. He came to this stage, saw all of us, and received in our presence the award that had been presented to him.

Our journal editor, the long-time sovereign of letters Saavi, received the award and gave his thanks here as an acceptance speech, briefly but with flavour.

For this mere one lakh rupees, our Erode Tamilanban (applause) has given all of us here, in the name of an acceptance speech, a poem that could not be obtained even for a crore rupees.

As younger brother Maran noted, the Murasoli Trust was announced at the function held at Anna Arivalayam at the time of the golden jubilee of the Murasoli journal,

## PDF 21 / printed p.20

and, on behalf of that Trust, these functions are being held every year with the aim of presenting awards and honouring Tamil scholars, poets, and those who are proficient in the Tamil language.

I mentioned that Murasoli’s golden jubilee was in ’92. Murasoli was started in Tiruvarur in 1942. When it began, it did not begin as the large daily newspaper that appears today. To say more, it did not even begin as a weekly. It appeared from Tiruvarur as a leaflet—once a month, twice a month if necessary, and three times a month if there was further need.

Because I was a student, that leaflet was published under the pen name “Cheran”; some four thousand or five thousand copies were printed and sent free of charge to our movement organisations here and there. That was the period when we were engaged in that effort to spread the principles.

After that, it blossomed in Tiruvarur as a weekly. When it did so, there were times when debt troubles and shortage of money distressed me to the extent that I could not take the printed copies of Murasoli out of the press on the appointed day. At such times, I pawned my sisters’ jewellery from my house and raised money,

## PDF 22 / printed p.21

and, in a situation where we could not even hire a cart to carry those printed copies of the Murasoli weekly, I, Murasoli’s manager, and my friend Thennan carried them on our heads. We walked across the bamboo bridge that stood in the middle of the Odampokki river running through Tiruvarur, came to the Murasoli office on South Street, and engaged in the work of sending them to agents and to those to whom they had to be sent. That is how Murasoli spread!

That Murasoli later became a daily published from Chennai. Following that period when it became a daily, we held its fiftieth-year celebration—its golden jubilee—in Chennai. It was at that function that this Trust was announced, and today awards have been presented on behalf of that Trust.

It is not enough merely to say “Trust”; I have told you this history briefly because this Murasoli Trust has a history.

Murasoli was not a paper that, from the very outset, began with facilities, growth and opportunity that enabled tens of thousands of copies to be printed!

That paper has grown today. Everyone who spoke here praised Murasoli. Even if Murasoli does not circulate in lakhs of copies like some other papers today,

## PDF 23 / printed p.22

if Murasoli has become a paper with the strength to face the impact created by newspapers circulating in lakhs (loud applause), that is not a strength belonging to Murasoli alone; it is not its power alone. It is the strength of lakhs upon lakhs of comrades like you who stand behind the movement through which Murasoli is conducted that is Murasoli’s strength (loud applause). It is because of that strength that today the Trust has attained the distinction of being able to present this award to men of skill, great minds, Tamil scholars and poets.

In 1942, during the period when I published Murasoli as a leaflet, its anniversary was held in Tiruvarur. The Professor and Navalar had been invited to that anniversary. The speeches both of them delivered at that anniversary still resonate in my ears.

After inviting the Professor and Navalar to that anniversary, we accommodated them in a reading room called “Rajarathinam Reading Room,” held the meeting in the evening, and then had to send them back to Chidambaram—they were studying at Annamalai University at that time. We had to send them there. We struggled without even having money to give them for the bus fare. I myself stole a piece of jewellery that was in my house and pawned it,

## PDF 24 / printed p.23

and sent the speakers on their way. I mention all these incidents only as examples of the difficulties that a person involved in public life has to face.

The Professor and Navalar Nedunchezhiyan had arrived the previous day to take part in that function. At the railway station they asked, “Where is the Karunanidhi who invited us?” I too was searching, asking, “Where is Anbazhagan? Where is Nedunchezhiyan?” If they came when we called them even though we had not been introduced and had not seen one another’s faces, what had introduced us before we ourselves met was Periyar’s principle—Anna’s principle! That was what introduced us to one another! (loud applause)

Today, in this period, even though we are holding this pleasant function in this great hall, we must not forget that we are standing and sitting here with our hearts trembling with the fear that those principles may be placed in danger.

In 1944, I spent one year in the Kudi Arasu office under the protective care of Father Periyar. During 1944–45, while I was there, the monthly salary given to me for the responsibility of assistant editor was 40 rupees. Do not regard that as ordinary. Forty rupees in those days was equivalent to 400 rupees in these days.

## PDF 25 / printed p.24

If I am to give a comparison, before that, Perarignar Anna, who had been editor at the Viduthalai office, was paid 50 rupees. I was paid 40 rupees. I worked there for a year. It was only after that that the invitation from A.S.A. Sami came to me.

There was a man called Tabla Muthukrishnan who was working at the radio station in Tiruchi; R.M.V. and S.S.R., who are seated here, know him well. He came to Erode saying that A.S.A. Sami had invited me and took me with him. After obtaining Periyar’s permission, I took Erode Subbaiah with me as a companion and went to Coimbatore. In Coimbatore I met A.S.A. Sami. At that time he was a young man who had not even completed thirty or thirty-five years of age. I was about nine years younger than he was. He said that I should write dialogue for the films he directed. He was then going to direct the film Rajakumari. It was on that very day, through that film, that my dear friend M.G.R. and I were introduced to one another for the first time. In that film my dear friend M.G.R. acted as the hero. Some among you, the older ones, may still remember. In it, when M.G.R. leapt onto a platform to fight with a sword, the entire cinema hall would thunder. Applause would rise to that extent. The reason was that, when M.G.R. made his appearance in that way, he would be wearing a black costume,

## PDF 26 / printed p.25

and in those days even seeing a black costume on the cinema screen was an event that gave such joy to the people of our party and our movement.

During the period when Rajakumari was released with M.G.R. acting in it, A.S.A. Sami paid me a monthly salary of 150 rupees. In Erode it was 40 rupees. At Coimbatore Central Studio, 150 rupees—a promotion! After that I came to Modern Theatres to write dialogue. The monthly salary was 500 rupees. Thus, in the film field, I earned salaries at the levels of 40, 150 and 500.

I wanted to come to Chennai. I wanted to continue taking part in movement work, to take part in it without interruption. When, with that intention, I came to Chennai, Kalaivanar N.S.K. told me, “Come and settle in Chennai”; when National Pictures Perumal invited me for making the film Parasakthi, Salem Modern Theatres T.R. Sundaram said, “No, do not go to Chennai. Stay here in this studio and direct films; be a director. I will give you 10,000 rupees a month.” Ten thousand was no ordinary amount in those days.

Thinking that I would become bound there, become subject to it, and be tied down to such an extent that I would be unable to take part in party work, I said, “It is not possible,” and came to Chennai. What happened after that, all of you know. What happened, all of you know.

## PDF 27 / printed p.26

In this intervening period, I cannot forget the affection A.S.A. Sami showed me when I was in Coimbatore, the fondness he showed me, and the attachment he had for me. There were even some differences of opinion between him and me. There were also those outside who magnified those differences into something enormous. Yet the affection between us never withered. I have eaten in his home on many days. He treated me with great affection. He never underestimated my ability. Even if today he is in circumstances in which his life has not received that measure of light, the respect and affection shown to me by the members of his family have not diminished in any measure.

Therefore it is not merely the debt of gratitude I owe him. In the film field, through his fine ability as a director, he took Perarignar Anna’s revolutionary picture Velaikkari to the people of the country through cinema, and, as the director of that film which for the first time brought about a revolution in the film field, it had long been my thought and my long-standing wish that A.S.A. Sami should be honoured. Beyond all those things, he had also been an early teacher to me in the film field, and I wanted to show that affection in return,

## PDF 28 / printed p.27

to offer a tribute of gratitude. I convey my congratulations and appreciation to younger brother Murasoli Maran and all the friends in the Murasoli Trust for fulfilling that wish of mine by presenting him with an award and a purse of one lakh rupees.

Next, our editor Saavi. Everyone who spoke here in praise has mentioned the friendship between him and me. He is not merely a humour writer; he is a writer possessed of deep patriotism. He had even received the opportunity to move closely with the noble Gandhi. When Gandhi undertook his journey of sacrifice in the North, Saavi, who had gone to see him as a journal editor, compiled all the conversations he had with Gandhi and wrote an entire book about that journey. It is a book that creates a fresh awakening in our hearts as we read it. The testimonial from Thuglak has mentioned the Washington Thirumanam that he wrote.

How many times has that play been staged in Tamil Nadu? Not only in Tamil Nadu; it is impossible to count how many performances have been held even in places such as Bombay and in other states. The play has been staged to that extent. I myself have watched Saavi’s Washington

## PDF 29 / printed p.28

Thirumanam five or six times and have been enthralled by it.

There is a small book called Vedaviththu. A novel, a novella. I finished reading it in a single night and, the next morning, wrote and sent a foreword for it. A wonderful story.

By making people from oppressed and backward communities into characters, by also creating a character born in an upper-caste community, and by the manner in which he has constructed it, we can understand the extent to which Mr. Saavi is a man of progressive thought. He was in prison for the freedom struggle of India. He was in prison for many months. For many months in prison he suffered alongside various leaders. Even after Independence, prison gave him a one-day welcome—four or five years ago!

During the previous administration, we suddenly received a piece of news. The news was that Saavi had been arrested. We asked why. There was a cover picture in his magazine. It was supposedly an obscene picture. They said he had been arrested for that. Immediately, my friend Veerasamy, I and others went in search of where he was. They had kept him at a police station in Anna Nagar. When we went there and saw him, even then he was smiling. I even asked him, “What is this? You are sitting here with a smiling face just as in the old days when you fought for the freedom of the country and went to prison—”

## PDF 30 / printed p.29

I asked. He said, “All of you have come to see me, haven’t you? Because of that, I do not feel this hardship.” But I could understand clearly the anguish that was revealed in his heart.

Then he said, “This condition, this punishment, has come to me for putting a cover picture in the ‘Saavi’ magazine.” We secured bail for him and brought him out. Why did this happen in his life? It happened to him because of the friendship between him and me. For the sake of friendship, he was a person with the nature to bear even all these sufferings and sorrows. He remains so even today.

On so many tours, my friend Saavi has come with me. Why does he come? Because of the desire to learn directly, during the tour, about the people’s awakening and the people’s enthusiasm. Therefore, in those days it was customary for Mr. Saavi to accompany me on all my tours. Even the women in our two families—his household and those in my household—became close in such a good way that the two families associated with one another as though they were one family.

Even if he went to America, or to any part of the world, from there he would

never fail to contact me. Even here, when he is in Chennai, he and I speak by telephone every day. Such a dear friend. A simple friend. A friend whose feeling of friendship never changes in any circumstance. To Saavi—not merely because he is a friend. He is a good writer. He has created many writers and, as writers themselves praise him, he is acclaimed as the “patriarch of writers.” By presenting this award to him, the Murasoli Trust takes pride. Erode Tamilanban; he said here, “It was Karunanidhi who attached the qualifier ‘Erode’ to me.” You also heard Tamilanban’s poems here. You heard the speech of our Kavikko Abdul Rahman, who offered criticism of the poems he had written. We can take pride, thinking again and again, about the kind of talented people Tamil Nadu has been given to keep Tamil alive, to serve Tamils, and to carry on Tamil work.

As they pointed out, if each poetry gathering took place with thousands upon thousands of people overflowing the venue, and if the next poetry gathering too drew the same great multitude, the reason for all of that was people such as Tamilanban and Abdul

Rahman, and the talent they displayed at those poetry gatherings.

From the time in 1967 when, for the first time, I presided over a poetry gathering on Tiruchi Radio and began reciting under the title Freedom Fighters, until now, it can be said that not a single poetry gathering under my leadership has taken place without Abdul Rahman and Erode Tamilanban. From time to time, some people moved away because of pressure. As though saying, “If we are too close, let us move away,” some moved away at a time of pressure. But even in that time of pressure, if I say that those who pressed close and remained with me were Abdul Rahman and Erode Tamilanban, it would not be an exaggeration. (loud applause)

Of those two, one has received an award and the other poet, Rahman, has had the opportunity on this stage to praise the award recipient. Thinking of that again and again fills me with delight. Amid so much political heat and so many official duties, because I listened this evening to the speech and the poetry of these two poets, the time has become a useful one.

I often say that literary events like these help me greatly to cool the heat of politics! Today Abdul Rahman and Erode Tamilanban have given me that help. For that

reason too, I convey my appreciation to them.

These awards have been presented to people who are most fitting, in the presence of fitting people such as yourselves. Long live them! Long live them! That is my wish.
