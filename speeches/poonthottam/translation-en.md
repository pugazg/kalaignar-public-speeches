# Poonthottam

Mr. Chairman! Comrades! I convey my thanks to all the friends who wished me to participate in this function being held on behalf of the Tamil Association of Guindy Engineering College. In his introductory remarks, the chairman said that I was going to take all of you to a flower garden; wondering with curiosity what sort of flower garden it might be, and suspecting that, if it were perhaps a literary flower garden, he might have to follow behind, enjoy literary delight, and then return with regret, he concluded his introduction by hinting that he trusted the speaker would not create such a situation. I have not come to take all of you to a flower garden. I am going to speak about the flower garden in which we are all seated.

Creating a flower garden is certainly no ordinary matter. To establish a garden, forests must be cleared. Mounds must be levelled. Hollows must be filled and cultivated

properly. A beautiful flower garden created through all such toil may suddenly, or little by little, fall into ruin.

In a flower garden brought into being by constantly withstanding flood and fire, venom-spitting snakes, and unceasing diseases, the flowers that bloom and spread fragrance are plucked away by the man who did not labour, who did not shed sweat, and he gives himself over to delight—on one side! A pleasure-grove created by an iron hand! In it, a honeyed flower dripping sweetness! A golden hand that plucks it away! Such a painful situation in the flower garden! Next comes the question: what flowers ought to be cultivated in the flower garden?

Should there be a rose in it? Or an erukku flower? A jasmine creeper pouring forth beauty? Or a cactus wilderness? Such question marks roam about in the flower garden!

Next, an urgency arises to drive away the cattle and goats that trampled and destroyed the flower garden. To those rejoicing that the cattle and goats have run away, a cry rises on one side of the flower garden: it is true that the goats and cattle are gone, but the small insects and parasitic growths that remain within and kill prosperity must also be removed!

A flower garden besieged by these three kinds of problems is indeed worthy of our attention.

Not merely worthy of attention; the ‘flower garden’ we are going to examine can also perform the higher task of mingling with the air a sense of duty that makes us act with concern, and touching our consciousness.

If we imagine the Tamil land in which we live as a flower garden, we see the rose of *Silappathikaram*, the champak called *Civaka Cintamani*, the cluster of *Kundalakesi* flowers, the jasmine of *Manimekalai*, and the freshly blossomed mullai flowers called `அகம்புற மென்ற அன்றலர்ந்த`, all spreading fragrance. ## PDF page 8 / printed page 7

we see the poems of Bharathiar and Bharathidasan floating like a breeze. In such a splendidly flourishing garden, it is not as though there is no cactus wilderness in one corner. Nor is the scentless kanakambaram absent. Nor have great anthills, inhabited by snakes that frighten people so that they cannot take a leisurely evening stroll, failed to appear.

I call it cactus. It may even seem harsh to some because they have not entered and looked within: the *Kamba Ramayanam* has grown as cactus in the garden of Tamil Nadu. There is beauty in the cactus flower, just as there is poetic grace in Kambar's story! There is no fragrance in the cactus flower, just as there is no ethnic consciousness in Kambar's portrayal!

Cactus is full of thorns! The *Ramayanam* places an obstacle in the way of Tamil consciousness!

I described *Silappathikaram* as a rose, because the rose too has thorns!

*Silappathikaram* is a rose flower amid thorns! *Kamba Ramayanam* is a cactus flower amid thorns!

There may be events in *Silappathikaram* beyond the reach of imagination. But when the anklet sounds, the character of classical Tamil sounds with it. The life of the Chera, the prosperity of the Chola, the integrity of the Pandya—in all, references to the three great lands ruled by the three crowned kings—lie there in abundance, in that delightful treasure given by Ilango! *Silappathikaram* is a golden rose fashioned by the Chera's younger brother!

Events beyond the reach of imagination are the thorns in it! Whether the rose is needed or not is not a question that requires debate!

A powerful voice says that such wild growths must not spread through the flower garden! The voice of rationalism! No—there is a faint tone saying that they too must remain! A tone arising from the side of the pundits!

## PDF page 9 / printed page 8

Thus, in the flower garden, a struggle is taking place between the movement of reason and the movement opposed to it!

Merely because the glories of those days and the noble qualities of Ayodhya Rama are narrated in beautiful Tamil, we cannot believe that those days were a marvellous age.

Nor are those who stand arrayed in the army of reason people seized by a hasty passion to reject those days root and branch. Those days may have been excellent in several respects. But who can accept that this age should lie crushed and writhing under their domination?

There is no objection to bringing the good things of those days and joining them with the innovations of this age!

What causes pain is that the two ages are made to clash while people stand watching for amusement!

If, for a moment, we turn our eyes and ears toward practical happenings and the mocking talk that arises in the country, we may say that a strange situation emerges in which amusement joins with pain.

On one side comes the muttering: “It is because all these people speak like this in this age that even rain no longer falls in the country!”

From another side comes the reply: “But in those days—in the time of your Nallathangal, in the age before atheism spread—was there not said to have been no rain for twelve years?”

“Do children of this age have even a little brotherly affection?

“In those days, Bharata, the younger brother, carried on his head the sandals of Rama, who was born to rule, seated them upon the royal throne, and ruled as their representative—was he not the true younger brother, and was that not brotherly affection!” Thus, on one side, the praises of those days are sung!

## PDF page 10 / printed page 9

Immediately, from another side comes the reply that in those very same days in which Bharata lived as an example of brotherly affection, carrying his elder brother's sandals, there also lived Vibhishana—the brother who betrayed his own elder brother Ravana to `அயோத்தியானுக்கு`, his elder brother!

Bharata, who bore his elder brother's footwear upon his head! Vibhishana, who asked that his elder brother's head be placed at the enemy's feet!

Both lived in those very same days!

“Students of this age put thorns on the teacher's chair—but in those days there lived Ekalavya, who cut off his thumb and offered it as a gift to the guru Dronacharya. Can anything equal the devotion to the guru of those days?” Thus a hymn of praise is sung on one side!

But from another side comes the reply that in those very same days in which Ekalavya, the exemplary disciple who gave his thumb to Acharya Drona, lived, there also lived Chandra, who joined with Tara, the guru's wife; and the question is posed: how, then, can those days be said to have excelled in devotion to the guru?

Two old men see two young men walking along the street. One of the young men is wearing spectacles. Seeing this, one old man says to the other, “Did you see! At such a young age his eyesight has already become defective; he is wearing spectacles. This age itself is like this, my friend—did such things exist in those days?” The old man thus pleads the case for those days!

What do you think those young men would say if this conversation fell upon their ears? In this age, if one's eyesight fails, there is an eye hospital where one can receive treatment; spectacles have been invented to sharpen one's vision. But those facilities did not exist in those days!

What Rama told Hanuman—“My wife Sita has such-and-such identifying features; go and search for her”—he could have advertised in the newspaper and very easily found out where she was! Has your daughter come there?” and sent a telegram to Ayodhya asking, “Have you found your sister-in-law?”—how quickly he could have learned the particulars about Sita.

How readily the conveniences of this age lend themselves to accomplishing tasks.

When we see even some of those who understand this age—those who speak of the glories of this age—still clashing with the ideas of those days, it is indeed a cause for concern to us as we stroll through the flower garden!

They wear a ‘wrist watch’ and consult Rahu Kalam!

They wear spectacles and read the *Bharatham*. They break a coconut before a camera made in Hollywood, apply kumkum to it, burn camphor, bow before it, and then make a film!

Do these oddities seem merely amusing? Do they not make storms of anguish suddenly sweep through the flower garden again and again?

If Truman retires from political life in America, what do you expect him to write about?

Would he be writing a “Bhaja Govindam”? Rajaji—the intellectual, the wise man, the scholar, the political strategist—retires from his duties

## PDF page 12 / printed page 11

and if, to someone expecting that he will pile up writings of lofty principles and emotionally stirring words that touch the people's hearts, he says, “I will not give you the delightful lotus; I will give you only the erukku flower,” how is such a condition to be described!

At a time when we think the cactus wilderness itself must be removed from the garden, if those regarded as fully learned say, “I am bringing in erukku plants as well and cultivating them,” how can one close one's mouth and remain silent!

It is these distressing happenings that have created, in the grove of Tamil Nadu, a struggle between the people of rationalism and the people of the island of antiquity over which flowers ought to remain and what further kinds of flowers ought to be cultivated!

Which flower should be planted is one problem! Next comes: to whom do the flowers already planted belong?

The man who shed sweat says that the flowering plant he planted belongs to him. The idler who regards another's sweat as a feast says the flowers must be crushed upon the bed in his mansion.

This struggle assumes an important place in the flower garden!

“Is the flower for the labourer? Or for the pleasure-seeker?”—that is the struggle taking place between common ownership and bourgeois doctrine. Russia and China, countries that have planted the flag of victory in this struggle, smile at us.

There is a third struggle: between those who boast, “We have driven away the white cattle that grazed in the garden—there is no need even for a fence any longer,” and those who say, “The white cattle have run away; but insects and parasitic growths are ruining the flowering plants”—and that, to destroy them, the medicine called separate sovereignty must be sprayed!

Which flower is needed—that is social reform.

To whom the flower belongs—that is the explanation of the demand for equality.

## PDF page 13 / printed page 12

“To whom does the flower garden itself belong; under whose domination is it now; should not the beautiful grove, freed from that domination, come into the possession of its owner?”—that is the question concerning the demand for separate sovereignty!

Which of these three must take place first is something to be considered!

1. Which flower?
2. Who is to pluck the flower?
3. Whose is the garden?

When we take up these three questions in order and examine them, for us to cultivate the flower we desire, the garden must belong to us. Until then, we can only keep speaking of the virtues of the flower we desire.

For us ourselves to pluck and enjoy the flower, the garden must belong to us.

Until then, if the flower comes into our hands, we can only say how a garland may be strung `பெய்ப்படி`!

Therefore, the first thing that must be done—urgently and necessarily—is that the garden must become ours!

Once the garden becomes ours, the troublesome insects will perish.

Pure flowers will smile—we ourselves will become those entitled to the flower—it is to engage in this good work that I invite all of you.

Those who come to take part must be people who proclaim in a loud voice that the flower belongs to the one who laboured.

They must possess the maturity of mind that wishes spotless flowers to spread fragrance.

In the flower garden where we are seated, the warrior Senguttuvan has strolled.

## PDF page 14 / printed page 13

The Chola who conquered Kadaram has sung a song of rejoicing.

The Pandya has made the Classical Tamil Sangam resound.

The flower garden I speak of has seas on three sides, shimmering with pearls!

On one side it has a lofty mountain range!

It has fertile fields—perennial life-giving rivers!

It gives not merely shoots and flowers; it gives gold!

Come, come, take part in the work of making such a flower garden our own—to the `வழக்கு மன்றத்திற்கு`—that is how I invite all of you.

There is no communalism in inviting you in this way—there is no room for bourgeois doctrine to raise its head—there is certainly no narrow-mindedness.

I invite everyone. I do not invite people by observing distinctions of high caste and low caste.

I am one among those engaged in the great effort to remove that distinction.

Our aim is neither to abolish the people of the high castes nor to turn the people of the low castes into high-caste people.

All that people like us desire is to bring the man above down from the summit of the tower, raise the man below from the rubbish heap, and seat both in one hall on an equal level.

Apart from this, we harbour no kind of malice toward the upper classes.

We are the people who have been in a lowly condition—we are the people who have fallen into a pit. Should not those in the higher position have extended a hand and saved all of us?

Had they performed that duty, would there have been a situation in which two castes clash with one another?

## PDF page 15 / printed page 14

They are educated people. Degree-holders. Ninety out of a hundred are people who entered colleges, institutions of learning, and schools. They have walked through the streets of history. They have strolled in the grove of geography. They have sat upon the shore of science.

If they—the upper classes—had taken upon themselves the responsibility of making all those uneducated people—people who were made to remain uneducated, people who had not come ashore from the condition of the common masses—sing the song of rationalism, condemnation would not have arisen; a great society would instead have expressed gratitude mingled with joy!

From that day to this day, has not the fortress called the caste system been built upon a strong foundation? I invite even those inside the fortress to take part in the work that will reduce that fortress to dust. Friends! When a river merges into the sea, the sea does not become the river. The river too becomes the sea.

But if a great society possessing a distinct culture reaches a condition in which, through cultural mixture with a smaller society, it loses its own distinctive greatness and is destroyed—if it has acquired the disposition to accept even erukku flowers into its flower garden—is that not a matter for sorrow!

If a drop of poison is mixed into a pot of milk, the poison does not become milk. The milk becomes poison. The same condition is developing in our flower garden.

Caste consciousness is just like that: it has invaded and subjugated a society in which there was not even a word called caste.

Is that consciousness found only among ordinary human beings? Do not the ancient texts say that it exists even in the Supreme Lord?

Only after Kannappan lost his eye was he able to receive the title ‘Nayanar’ from the Lord with the eye upon his forehead.

Only after Karaikkal Ammaiyar disfigured her beautiful body and walked upside down in skeletal form did Father Paramasivan address her as ‘Ammaiye’.

## PDF page 16 / printed page 15

Only after Siralan had been cooked as meat and served as a feast to Siva was Siruthondan decorated with the badge of ‘devotee’. The reason: all these people were of the lower castes. At the very same time, a man of the higher caste who committed the great sin of calling his mother to the bedchamber and killing his father stands on the bank of the Vaigai and cries, “Grant me a boon, O Supreme One!” and the Lord comes to meet the devotee with a ‘passport’ to the world of salvation, saying, “I have come, devotee!”

Whom do such stories of caste frenzy endanger? Is it not the duty of the upper classes to declare all these stories invalid and throw them into the sea?

If these stories, and those who walk in the path shown by these stories, had changed themselves before becoming the objects of opposition from the lower castes, there would have been no need for a storm to blow in the flower garden; would not the breeze instead have sung a *themmangu* song?

The man standing opposite holds a knife in his hand; surely the man passing beside him will at least go with his fist clenched. If he clenches his fist like that, can it simply be called communalism?

I ask that the knife be thrown down. I assure you that the very situation requiring a clenched fist will disappear on its own. If the knife is thrown down, the man approaching with his hand raised will lower it—the bitterness will vanish—and we will be able to see the sight of hands being clasped.

The knife is not merely the Puranas; it is bourgeois character—the doctrine of parasitic growth—and all the rest as well!

Will that knife be abandoned? Will the dove of peace fly? Or must dust alone be made to fly in the flower garden?

If those holding the knife say that there is no need to engage in the work of raising that dust, everyone can join hands with a sense of fulfilment and take part in the work of bringing the flower garden into our possession.

Otherwise, while many perform that work and a few laugh and mock, an unwanted hatred will inevitably grow.

## PDF page 17 / printed page 16

It is clearly evident that such hatred can do no more than delay a little the work of bringing the garden into our possession; it cannot build a dam against victory.

Let us conduct the case to bring the forest of colourful flowers into our possession.

Let us spread ideas about how the problems that have arisen in the garden are to be resolved. So that the case may conclude and a good judgment be obtained, let us engage in the campaign over which flower is to be planted in the garden and who is to pluck the flower that has been planted.

But, for the sake of this campaign, let us proceed without slipping away from the intensity with which we conduct the case.

It is precisely the good younger brothers who walk in this way that the flower garden I speak of desires. Become warriors who fulfil that desire.

Honours await. For the song of victory to arise, all that remains is for you to pluck the strings of the veena!

The breeze will glide—the new fragrance will float—`மானிடம்`, the honey-bird, the peacock, the cuckoo, and the *mattuppura* will all create a new artistic feast. I once again extend an invitation to the world of youth to behold that delightful flower garden.

**Greetings**
